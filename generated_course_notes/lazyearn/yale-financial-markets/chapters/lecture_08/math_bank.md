## Definitions And Objects
- Interest rate `r`: the percentage earned on a loan or paid on a loan; in the technical sections it is usually the annual rate unless another compounding interval is specified.
- Böhm-Bawerk causes of interest: technical progress, roundaboutness of production, and time preference.
- Two-period Fisher economy: current consumption versus next-period consumption, used to represent intertemporal choice and production opportunities.
- Production possibility frontier: the feasible set of present and future consumption combinations generated by current endowment plus productive technology.
- Indifference curves: utility contours over present and future consumption.
- Saving: the gap between current endowment and current consumption in the Crusoe setup.
- Discount bond: an asset that pays a fixed face value at maturity and is purchased for less than face value today.
- Yield to maturity: the annualized rate `r` implied by the bond’s current price and terminal payoff.
- Compounding interval: annual, semiannual, quarterly, daily, or continuous; the discount formula depends on this choice.
- Present discounted value `\mathrm{PDV}`: the present worth of one or more future payments after discounting by an appropriate interest rate and compounding rule.
- Consol / perpetuity: a bond paying the same coupon forever.
- Coupon `C`: the periodic payment made by a bond.
- Annuity: a fixed payment stream that ends after a finite last payment date `T`.
- Conventional bond: a coupon annuity plus repayment of principal at maturity.
- Term structure of interest rates: yields quoted today for different maturities.
- Spot rate: the rate quoted for borrowing/lending from today to a stated maturity.
- Forward rate `f`: the rate today that is implicit for borrowing/lending between two future dates.
- Expectations theory: the view that forward rates reflect expected future spot rates.
- Risk premium `\pi`: the gap by which forward rates may exceed expected future spot rates.

## Derivation Steps
Fisher one-person interest rate
1. Put present consumption on one axis and next-period consumption on the other.
2. Mark the endowment point and the production possibility frontier generated by planting grain.
3. In the linear case, the frontier has a constant slope, so technology alone fixes the intertemporal tradeoff.
4. In the diminishing-returns case, the frontier becomes concave, so the slope depends on where Crusoe chooses to operate.
5. Crusoe picks the highest indifference curve tangent to the frontier.
6. The common slope at the tangency is read as the gross intertemporal tradeoff, hence the interest rate.
7. More impatience moves the tangency toward more present consumption and raises the implied interest rate.

Two-person lending-market equilibrium
1. Give both Crusoes the same technology and endowment but different intertemporal preferences.
2. Let each choose a no-trade tangency on the same production possibility frontier.
3. The impatient Crusoe wants more current consumption; the patient Crusoe wants more future consumption.
4. Because marginal productivity differs across their separate planting choices, there are gains from trade.
5. A loan reallocates current grain/consumption from the patient Crusoe to the impatient Crusoe.
6. A common tangent line supports both post-trade consumption choices.
7. The borrowing and lending amounts clear on that common line.
8. Both agents move to higher utility, and the slope of the common line defines the market interest rate.

Discount-bond pricing and yield to maturity
1. Start with a bond that pays `100` at maturity `T`.
2. Under annual compounding, discount the maturity payment back `T` years.
3. This gives $p=\frac{100}{(1+r)^T}$.
4. Rearrange to $(1+r)^T=\frac{100}{p}$.
5. Take the `T`th root to extract the annual yield.
6. Interpret `r` as the yield to maturity implied by price, not as an explicit coupon rate.

Compounding conventions
1. Under annual compounding, one dollar becomes $(1+r)^T$ after `T` years.
2. Under semiannual compounding, replace the annual rate by the half-year rate `z=r/2`.
3. Count half-year periods with `n=2T`.
4. The same discounting logic becomes $(1+z)^n$ instead of $(1+r)^T$.
5. Continuous compounding is the limiting case as the compounding interval shrinks to zero.
6. The balance formula becomes $B(T)=B_0e^{rT}$.

Present discounted value
1. Begin with a single future payment `x`.
2. Discount it back using the compounding rule relevant to the payment schedule.
3. For annual payments, sum the discounted terms over dates `t=1,2,\dots`.
4. This produces $\mathrm{PDV}=\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\frac{x_t}{(1+r)^t}$.
5. For semiannual payments, replace the denominator by the half-year discount factor.
6. For a continuous payment flow, replace the sum by an integral and the discount factor by $e^{-rt}$.

Consol, annuity, and conventional bond
1. A consol pays a constant coupon forever.
2. Discount the infinite stream to obtain the perpetuity rule $\mathrm{PDV}=C/r$.
3. An annuity is the same constant stream truncated after the last payment date `T`.
4. Treat the annuity as a perpetuity minus the discounted tail beyond `T`.
5. This yields $\mathrm{PDV}=\frac{x}{r}\left(1-\frac{1}{(1+r)^T}\right)$.
6. A conventional bond pays coupons until maturity and principal at the end.
7. Price it as coupon annuity plus discounted principal.

Forward-rate construction
1. Fix the date at 1925 and suppose an investor expects to have `£100` available in 1926.
2. Ask for the one-year rate from 1926 to 1927 that can be locked in already in 1925.
3. Buy a scaled quantity of two-year discount bonds.
4. Short one one-period bond so that there is a `£100` obligation in 1926.
5. The long position pays $100\cdot\frac{(1+r_2)^2}{1+r_1}$ in 1927.
6. The short one-period bond makes the 1926 outlay effectively `£100`.
7. Therefore the gross return from 1926 to 1927 is $\frac{(1+r_2)^2}{1+r_1}$.
8. Define that gross return as `1+f`.

Expectations theory and risk premium
1. Read the forward rate as the market-implied rate for a future interval.
2. Under pure expectations theory, interpret it as the expected future spot rate.
3. Compare ex ante forward rates with later realized or forecast-optimal spot rates.
4. Hicks’s qualification is that forward rates tend to sit above expected future spot rates.
5. Label the gap a risk premium rather than a failure of bond arithmetic.

## Notation Choices
- Use `r` for the annual effective interest rate unless a different compounding convention is explicitly in force.
- Use `c_0` and `c_1` for present and next-period consumption in the Fisher diagrams, even though the lecture labels them verbally rather than symbolically.
- Use `T` for maturity measured in years and for the final payment date of an annuity.
- Use `t` as a generic discrete payment index in sums; for continuous time use `t` inside `x(t)` and the integral.
- Avoid the transcript’s unstable reuse of lowercase/uppercase `t/T` in the semiannual section by introducing `n=2T` for the number of half-year periods.
- Use `z=r/2` only when reproducing the Fabozzi-style semiannual convention discussed in the lecture.
- Use `p` for current price of a discount bond.
- Use `100` as the face value in the lecture’s worked discount-bond and forward-rate examples.
- Use `x` for a generic annuity payment and `x_t` for a discrete payment sequence.
- Use `C` for the coupon payment on a conventional bond.
- Use `F` for principal / face value at maturity in reconstructed conventional-bond formulas.
- Use `\mathrm{PDV}` in upright roman type as the operator for present discounted value.
- Use `f` for the one-period forward rate implied by the one-year and two-year yields `r_1` and `r_2`.
- Use “spot rate” for the rate quoted on the relevant future date, not the forward-implied rate quoted today.
