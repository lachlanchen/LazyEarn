## Definitions And Objects
- `\text{Too big to fail}`: large-firm failure expected to trigger public rescue because collapse would threaten the whole system.
- `\text{Microprudential regulation}`: prudential regulation aimed at protecting smaller firms, households, and individual investors/borrowers.
- `\text{Macroprudential regulation}`: prudential regulation aimed at protecting system-wide stability.
- `\text{Board of directors}`: within-firm regulatory body; outside directors are the key quasi-regulatory element in the lecture.
- `\text{Tunneling}`: extraction of value by insiders from the corporation and thus from shareholders.
- `\text{Duty of care}`: board obligation to act as a reasonable, prudent, rational person; requires attention, information, and monitoring.
- `\text{Duty of loyalty}`: board obligation primarily to shareholders in the lecture’s baseline formulation.
- `\text{Self-regulatory organization}`: trade-group rulemaking body; stock exchanges are the main example.
- `\text{Buttonwood Agreement}`: early NYSE agreement combining minimum commissions with preference for trading among members.
- `\text{Blue Sky Laws}`: state-level securities laws aimed at fraudulent or overpromoted securities offerings.
- `\text{SEC}`: national regulator built around disclosure, surveillance, investor protection, and public/private issuance rules.
- `\text{EDGAR}`: SEC disclosure platform for public-company filings.
- `\text{IPO}`: initial public offering; regulated path from private to public securities issuance.
- `\text{Hedge fund}`: private investment vehicle for wealthy investors; lightly regulated relative to public funds in the lecture.
- `\text{Accredited investor}`: investor meeting income or wealth thresholds used to restrict access to private offerings.
- `\text{2 and 20}`: fee structure with annual management fee plus performance fee on gains.
- `\text{Market surveillance}`: monitoring of trading records and related data to detect manipulation or insider trading.
- `\text{FASB}`: private accounting-standards body recognized by the SEC.
- `\text{GAAP}`: generally accepted accounting principles; the accounting standard set used in SEC disclosure.
- `\text{SIPC}`: brokerage-account protection corporation, parallel in role to bank deposit insurance.
- `\text{FSOC}`: Dodd-Frank macroprudential body focused on systemic risk and too-big-to-fail.
- `\text{Bureau of Consumer Financial Protection}` / `\text{Consumer Financial Protection Bureau}`: microprudential consumer-protection body in the Dodd-Frank discussion.
- `\text{BIS}`: Bank for International Settlements; international coordination forum without direct coercive authority.
- `\text{Basel Committee}`: source of Basel I/II/III suggested bank-regulation frameworks.
- `\text{G6},\ \text{G7},\ \text{G20}`: progressively broader intergovernmental coordination groups.
- `\text{FSB}`: Financial Stability Board; G20-linked international regulatory coordination body.

## Derivation Steps
Too-big-to-fail mechanism
1. A large financial firm fails.
2. Its failure threatens the rest of the financial system.
3. Government bailout becomes likely.
4. Large firms therefore carry an implicit guarantee.
5. That guarantee weakens effective downside discipline.
6. Large firms take more risk.
7. Systemic-crisis risk rises.
8. The problem becomes macroprudential, not merely microprudential.

Microprudential / macroprudential split
1. Start from `prudential` as prudent business standards.
2. Ask whether the object of protection is the individual firm/person or the whole system.
3. If the target is households, small firms, or ordinary investors, classify as microprudential.
4. If the target is the stability of the whole financial system, classify as macroprudential.
5. Place `too big to fail` in the macroprudential column.

Within-firm investor-protection logic
1. Shareholders supply capital but do not manage day-to-day operations.
2. Insiders control assets, contracts, compensation, opportunities, and information.
3. Those control rights create tunneling channels.
4. Unchecked tunneling makes outside investment fragile or irrational.
5. Boards, especially outside directors, are introduced as internal regulators.
6. `\text{Duty of care}` and `\text{duty of loyalty}` are the operative constraints.

Trade-group to cartel logic
1. A trade group can exclude unreliable actors and impose ethical standards.
2. The same trade group can fix commissions and exclude competitors.
3. Ethical screening and cartel power can coexist in one institution.
4. Public regulation later targets the cartel element.
5. Surveillance and ethical oversight can remain even after commission deregulation.

Disclosure logic for national regulation
1. Local regulation works poorly once sales cross state lines.
2. Telephone-based selling and boiler rooms defeat fragmented state enforcement.
3. A national regulator is created.
4. Its core method is disclosure rather than secrecy.
5. Public filings reduce informational asymmetry.
6. Surveillance backs disclosure with enforcement.

Hedge-fund fee arithmetic
1. Let `A` be assets under management.
2. Annual management fee is `0.02A`.
3. Let `\Pi` be trading profit.
4. Performance fee is `0.20\max\{\Pi,0\}`.
5. For `A=10^9`, management fee alone is `20{,}000{,}000`.
6. Losses do not symmetrically reduce manager compensation in the lecture’s simplified setup.

International-regulation spillover logic
1. National rules increase regulatory cost in some jurisdictions.
2. Financial firms can move offshore.
3. Offshore migration weakens purely national regulation.
4. Coordination bodies are created to reduce regulatory arbitrage.
5. Authority weakens as scope broadens, so recommendations substitute for direct command.
