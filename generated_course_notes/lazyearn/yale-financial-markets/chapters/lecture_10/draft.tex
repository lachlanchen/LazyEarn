\chapter{Real Estate}

These notes follow Robert J. Shiller's lecture on real estate finance as a sequence of problems rather than as a static taxonomy. We begin with the long history of mortgage lending, move to the institutional preconditions that make mortgage markets possible, then turn to the ownership forms used in commercial real estate, the redesign of residential mortgage contracts in the United States, and finally the larger architecture of securitization, crisis, and reform. The mathematical center of the lecture is the fixed-rate amortizing mortgage, but that mathematics is introduced only after the historical and institutional need for it has been made clear.

\section{Mortgage Lending in Long Historical Perspective}

The lecture begins by insisting on a long horizon. A mortgage is not a recent invention. The word itself is presented as very old and associated with the idea of a ``death pledge,'' though the precise medieval philology is less important here than the broader point: secured lending against property reaches far back into the history of trade and law.

This long view matters because it prevents us from telling the wrong story. The mortgage is not an artifact of modern Wall Street. In the lecture, old documents from the Tang Dynasty trade routes already suggest loan contracts secured by property, and even by persons. That is morally disturbing, but analytically useful: it tells us that the idea of collateralized borrowing is ancient.

What is modern, then, is not the existence of the contract in the abstract. What is modern is the institutional environment that makes mortgage lending a large-scale and widely trusted business. That distinction prepares the next move in the lecture.

\section{Property Rights as the Precondition for Mortgage Markets}

An old institution does not automatically become a deep market. The lecture's first major claim is that mortgage finance depends on the clarity of ownership and on the practical ability of lenders to enforce claims against collateral.

The Hartford Courant example from 1778 makes the point vividly. Elisha Cornwell sells a farm on mortgage terms, is not fully paid, and then watches the buyer mortgage the same farm again and again. The problem is not merely dishonesty. The deeper problem is that there is no systematic public machinery for verifying title. If ownership cannot be checked, the collateral is not dependable, and if the collateral is not dependable, mortgage lending cannot become routine.

This is why the lecture lingers on the development of title systems such as the Prussian \emph{Grundbuch}. A mortgage market requires a ledger of ownership, not neighborhood rumor. It also requires functioning foreclosure institutions. If a borrower stops paying but the lender cannot in practice recover the property, the contract ceases to work as collateralized finance.

\subsection{Question \& Answer}

\paragraph{Question.} Why can mortgage lending not rest on local knowledge, custom, or hearsay about who owns a property?

\paragraph{Answer.} Because mortgage finance is not just a social understanding; it is a priced financial transaction. The lender must know, in a form that survives dispute, that the borrower has title and that the lender can seize the collateral if the contract fails. Informal community knowledge may be enough to guide ordinary life, but it is not enough to support standardized lending, resale of loans, or securitization. In the lecture's logic, modern mortgage markets expand only when title and foreclosure become administratively legible.

The lecture then widens the point beyond the United States. Hernando de Soto's theme is that many countries still lack the property-rights infrastructure needed for mortgage markets to grow. In those places, one can often ask around and learn who is said to own a parcel of land. But one cannot safely lend against hearsay. That is the difference between an informal economy and a mortgage market.

\section{Commercial Real Estate Before Democratization}

Having established the institutional preconditions, the lecture narrows to the United States and asks a practical question: who owns the buildings we see around us?

The answer is often not ``a corporation'' in the familiar public-equity sense. Much commercial real estate is owned through partnerships. The reason given in the lecture is straightforward: partnerships avoid the double taxation associated with corporations. If a corporation owns a building, the corporate entity pays tax and the shareholders pay again on distributed income. In a partnership, income flows through more directly.

This tax logic explains the importance of the direct participation program, or DPP. A DPP is a real-estate partnership structure in which investors participate directly in the profits of the property rather than indirectly as shareholders in a corporation. The lecture emphasizes three structural features:

\begin{itemize}
\item access is generally restricted to accredited investors;
\item the vehicle typically has a limited life rather than perpetual duration;
\item ownership is often organized around a general partner and passive limited partners.
\end{itemize}

The contrast with an ordinary corporation is important. A corporation can live indefinitely and derive part of its value from perpetual growth prospects. A DPP, by contrast, is often built around a specific property or project, depreciated and managed over a fixed span, and then wound down when the property is sold. The lecture uses the local example of 360 State Street in New Haven to make this concrete.

So the basic commercial real-estate structure is already telling us something larger about finance: efficient ownership forms can coexist with restricted access. That tension will motivate the invention of REITs.

\section{REITs and the Democratization of Commercial Real Estate}

The lecture's next move is political as much as financial. If partnerships and DPPs are tax-efficient, why should ordinary investors be excluded from them?

Congress's answer in 1960 was the real estate investment trust, or REIT. The lecture presents the REIT as part of a long-run democratization of finance: a way to let smaller investors hold claims on real estate without subjecting the vehicle to ordinary corporate taxation, provided that the vehicle meets strict legal constraints.

The lecture gives the following rule-like conditions for a REIT:

\begin{itemize}
\item at least \(75\%\) of assets must be in real estate or cash;
\item at least \(75\%\) of income must come from real estate;
\item at least \(90\%\) of income must come from real-estate dividends, interest, and capital gains;
\item at least \(95\%\) of income must be paid out;
\item no more than \(35\%\) of income may come from sales of properties held for less than four years.
\end{itemize}

These conditions are meant to prevent an unrestricted escape from the corporate tax. Once Congress creates an untaxed vehicle, every firm would like to qualify. The restrictions keep the form tied to real estate and to long-horizon holding rather than pure turnover.

\subsection{Question \& Answer}

\paragraph{Question.} If partnerships avoid double taxation, why not simply let everyone invest through partnerships and stop there?

\paragraph{Answer.} Because the government does not want the partnership form to become an unrestricted substitute for the corporation. The lecture's answer is institutional rather than moral: partnerships are limited, access is restricted, and the law draws boundaries around what can count as pass-through real-estate ownership. A REIT is therefore a regulated compromise. It extends access to the public, but only by imposing a tight legal definition of the activity.

The lecture also insists that innovation in finance is slow. REITs do not instantly dominate after 1960. They expand in booms, first in the late 1960s, then after tax reform in 1986, and again during the real-estate boom of the 1990s. The broader lesson is that new financial forms often require decades, not years, to become standard.

\section{Residential Real Estate and the Great Depression}

The lecture now pivots to residential real estate, and the pivot is motivated by scale. Housing is larger and socially more pervasive than commercial real estate. In the United States, the lecture gives the rough figure that about two-thirds of households own their own home. That prevalence is itself a product of public policy.

To explain why modern home finance took its present form, the lecture turns to the Great Depression. Before the 1930s, mortgages in the United States commonly had short maturities, often two to five years, and were structured with balloon payments. A simple lecture example is:

\begin{equation}
H_0 = \$10{,}000, \qquad B_0 = \$5{,}000, \qquad D_0 = \$5{,}000,
\end{equation}

where \(H_0\) is the house value at purchase, \(B_0\) is the mortgage balance, and \(D_0\) is the down payment. Under the older contract, the borrower pays interest month by month, but the principal does not substantially amortize along the way:

\begin{equation}
x_t = \frac{r}{12} B_0 \quad \text{for } t=1,\dots,T-1,
\qquad
B_T = B_0.
\end{equation}

The entire principal comes due at maturity. The contract therefore assumes that refinancing will be available when the balloon date arrives.

The Great Depression destroyed that assumption. The lecture asks us to picture two shocks arriving at once. First, unemployment rises sharply. Second, house prices fall, sometimes by more than half. In the lecture's numerical illustration, a house originally worth \(\$10{,}000\) may fall to roughly \(\$4{,}000\), while the borrower still owes \(\$5{,}000\):

\begin{equation}
H_1 \approx \$4{,}000 < B_0 = \$5{,}000.
\end{equation}

Now the borrower reaches the refinancing date unemployed and with collateral worth less than the balance due. The bank refuses to roll the loan. The borrower is forced into distress, often losing the house and the down payment. The crisis, then, is not only a story about default; it is a story about rollover risk.

The Roosevelt administration's response in the lecture is twofold. The Federal Housing Administration insures mortgages, reducing lender losses, and it pushes the market away from short balloon structures toward long-term amortizing contracts. First these are required to be at least \(15\) years; later \(30\)-year mortgages become the emphasized form. The mathematical point is clear even before the formulas appear: the new contract is meant to remove the catastrophic refinancing date.

\section{The Mathematics of the Fixed-Rate Amortizing Mortgage}

Only after the crisis has made the contractual problem vivid does the lecture slow down and derive the arithmetic of the amortizing mortgage.

We fix the notation as in the lecture. Let \(m\) denote the maturity in months, let \(r\) be the annual mortgage rate, let \(B_0\) be the amount borrowed, and let \(x\) be the fixed monthly payment. The defining condition is that the present value of all monthly payments must equal the initial mortgage balance:

\begin{equation}
B_0 = \sum_{t=1}^{m} \frac{x}{(1+r/12)^t}.
\end{equation}

This is simply the present value of an annuity. Writing the same relation in closed form gives

\begin{equation}
B_0
=
x \frac{1-(1+r/12)^{-m}}{r/12},
\end{equation}

and solving for the monthly payment yields

\begin{equation}
x
=
B_0 \frac{r/12}{1-(1+r/12)^{-m}}.
\end{equation}

That gives the payment. But the lecture is equally interested in the composition of that payment through time. Each month, the payment splits into interest and principal:

\begin{align}
x &= I_t + P_t, \\
I_t &= \frac{r}{12} B_{t-1}, \\
P_t &= x - I_t, \\
B_t &= B_{t-1} - P_t.
\end{align}

This set of update rules is the heart of the lecture's mathematical exposition. Interest is computed on the outstanding balance. Because the balance falls over time, the interest component falls over time. But the total payment is fixed, so the principal component must rise over time.

\caption{Historical amortization table showing how a fixed mortgage payment shifts from interest toward principal over time.}
\end{figure}

The lecture then makes the arithmetic concrete with a mortgage-table example: a \(10\)-year mortgage at \(5\%\), scaled to \(\$1{,}000\) of initial balance. The monthly payment is about \(\$10.61\). The first two rows are enough to show the mechanism:

\begin{align}
I_1 &= \frac{0.05}{12}\cdot 1000 \approx \$4.17, \\
P_1 &= 10.61 - 4.17 = \$6.44, \\
B_1 &= 1000 - 6.44 = \$993.56,
\end{align}

and then

\begin{align}
I_2 &= \frac{0.05}{12}\cdot 993.56 \approx \$4.14, \\
P_2 &= 10.61 - 4.14 = \$6.47, \\
B_2 &= 993.56 - 6.47 = \$987.09.
\end{align}

\subsection{Question \& Answer}

\paragraph{Question.} If the monthly payment is fixed, why does the interest share fall while the principal share rises?

\paragraph{Answer.} Because interest is always computed on the remaining balance, not on the original balance. At the beginning of the mortgage, the balance is large, so the interest charge is large. As the balance is gradually paid down, the interest charge shrinks. Since the total payment \(x\) is fixed, the decline in \(I_t\) must be offset by a rise in \(P_t\). The contract keeps the household's total monthly burden stable while steadily accelerating principal repayment.

This is also why the table matters operationally. People do not always hold the mortgage to maturity. If a borrower sells the house early, one needs to know the unpaid balance. The table answers that question directly. The lecture's later checkpoint, at about six years and six months in this example, leaves a balance of about \(\$407.61\).

The lecture then gives this contract its broad name: the conventional fixed-rate mortgage. It is a financial innovation because it replaces a dangerous refinancing cliff with a smooth, predictable payment path. But the lecture immediately turns this success into a puzzle: why did such a useful contract become standard in so few countries?

\section{From Fixed Rates to Securitization, Crisis, and Reform}

The lecture's first answer to that puzzle is that long-term fixed-rate mortgages are expensive to supply. If a lender promises a rate for thirty years, that guarantee has value and must be priced. Borrowers may resist the higher rate. Regulators may also resist the asset-liability risk created when banks hold very long mortgages while funding themselves with short-term or withdrawable deposits. So even an apparently clean contract requires institutional support.

This leads naturally to government involvement in mortgage markets. In the United States, what became Fannie Mae begins as a government effort to buy mortgages and support lending during the Depression. The lecture's central economic point is simple: if banks can sell mortgages after origination, they recover funds and can lend again. A secondary market therefore supports the primary market.

Later Freddie Mac is created, and the lecture emphasizes the move from merely purchasing mortgages to packaging them into mortgage-backed securities and selling them with guarantees. In that setting the mortgage is no longer only a household contract. It becomes the raw material for a chain of claims held by investors.

\subsection{Question \& Answer}

\paragraph{Question.} Why were Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac ultimately rescued after repeated claims that the federal government did not guarantee them?

\paragraph{Answer.} Because the lecture's distinction between explicit and implicit guarantees collapses in crisis. Investors around the world treated these institutions as government-linked. Once they became large enough, letting them fail would have damaged not only mortgage markets but also the perceived safety of U.S. financial commitments more generally. The formal denial of a guarantee did not survive the political and reputational cost of actually refusing support.

The lecture then broadens the picture further. Canada provides a comparison case: it has analogous housing-finance institutions, but the lecture treats them as smaller and less aggressively expansionary. The comparison is used not to claim that Canada is wholly different, but to suggest that the United States pushed mortgage support further and thereby amplified the housing bubble.

The end of the lecture returns to securities. Two instruments are highlighted:

\begin{itemize}
\item collateralized mortgage obligations (CMOs), which divide mortgage cash flows into tranches, especially by prepayment exposure;
\item collateralized debt obligations (CDOs), which may hold mortgage-linked debt and repackage it again for investors.
\end{itemize}

The problem is no longer only one of mathematics, but of incentives. The lecture tracks the growing complexity of the chain:

\begin{itemize}
\item a mortgage originator writes the loan with the household;
\item the loan is sold to a securitizer or intermediary;
\item a servicer handles payment collection and delinquency management;
\item mortgage pools are restructured into securities and then sometimes re-securitized.
\end{itemize}

At each stage, distance grows between the original lender and the ultimate bearer of risk. The lecture presents the crisis in part as a moral-hazard problem. If the originator expects to sell the mortgage immediately, the originator may stop caring about long-run repayment quality. That is why the closing reform principle is so simple and so telling:

\begin{equation}
\text{retained share} = 0.05M,
\qquad
\text{sold share} = 0.95M,
\end{equation}

where \(M\) denotes a pool of originated mortgages. By forcing originators to retain \(5\%\) of the balance, post-crisis regulation tries to restore some alignment between screening and exposure.

The lecture closes with a pragmatic rather than revolutionary conclusion. The response is not to abolish mortgage securitization and return to eighteenth-century finance. It is to keep the basic system while tightening incentives, licensing, and professional standards.

\section{Summary}

The lecture's argument moves in a clear sequence. Mortgage lending is ancient, but large mortgage markets are modern because they require clear title, enforceable collateral, and standardized legal forms. Commercial real estate illustrates how tax and regulatory structure shape ownership, while REITs show the slow democratization of access. Residential finance then reveals the contract-design problem most sharply: the pre-Depression balloon mortgage failed because it loaded too much risk onto a refinancing date. The fixed-rate amortizing mortgage solved that problem by replacing rollover risk with a level-payment annuity whose balance declines by a transparent monthly recursion. Finally, the lecture embeds that household contract inside the larger machinery of secondary markets, securitization, implicit guarantees, crisis, and reform.
