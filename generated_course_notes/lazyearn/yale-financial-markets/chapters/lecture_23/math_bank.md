## Definitions And Objects
- Finance: a social technology for allocating scarce resources, creating incentives, and managing risks.
- Geometrical ratio: the lecture’s older term for multiplicative or exponential growth.
- Arithmetical ratio: the lecture’s older term for additive or linear growth.
- Malthusian problem: population pressure arising when population grows faster than subsistence.
- Dismal law: Shiller’s label for Malthus’s pessimistic conclusion that misery is structurally persistent.
- Financial arrangements: contracts and institutional commitments that can persist across regime change.
- Mathematical finance: formal modeling of financial relations and risks.
- Behavioral finance: application of psychology and other social sciences to finance.
- Cosmopolitans: actors with broad, externally oriented social and financial fluency.
- Locals: actors whose social orientation is narrower and less connected to cosmopolitan financial infrastructure.
- Democratization of finance: extension of financial knowledge, contracts, and risk management beyond elite households.
- Negative net worth: the state in which liabilities exceed asset values; in the housing example, mortgage debt exceeds home value.
- Informal bankruptcy: debt distress handled through nonresponse, collection, court action, and wage garnishment rather than formal Chapter 7 filing.
- Livelihood insurance: state-contingent protection against economic shocks to earning power, beyond medical disability.
- Home equity insurance: insurance against decline in the economic value of a house, not merely against physical destruction.
- Continuous workout mortgage: a mortgage contract with automatic, repeated, preplanned payment reductions in distress states.
- Joint liability group loan: a microfinance structure in which a borrowing group shares responsibility for repayment.

## Derivation Steps
1. UNICEF arithmetic: start from the lecture-time claim of \(\$3\) per child-life saved; take a \(\$100\) donation; compute \(100/3\approx 33\); use the result only as period lecture arithmetic, not current measurement.

2. Malthusian growth contrast: take “doubling every 25 years” as \(P(t+25)=2P(t)\); rewrite this as an exponential path \(P(t)=P_0\,2^{t/25}\); model subsistence in the lecture’s “arithmetical” sense as \(S(t)=S_0+gt\); conclude that unchecked population pressure rises relative to linear subsistence.

3. Wyman population statistic: use the verbal estimate that world population increases by about one billion every 12 years; record it as \(\Delta P \approx 10^9\) per 12-year interval; keep it as a coarse scale statement, not a continuous-time growth law.

4. Nature Conservancy land-share calculation: take \(500{,}000\ \text{km}^2\) protected land and \(150{,}000{,}000\ \text{km}^2\) world land area; divide to get \(1/300\); convert to decimal \(0.0033\); convert to percentage \(0.33\%\); preserve Shiller’s verbal reading as “one-third of 1%.”

5. Negative-equity household logic: let \(V_{\text{house}}\) denote home value and \(D_{\text{mortgage}}\) mortgage debt; when \(V_{\text{house}}<D_{\text{mortgage}}\), net housing equity is negative; add income loss or unemployment; infer elevated default risk and failure of ordinary household risk management.

6. Bankruptcy-friction mechanism: formal bankruptcy relief exists; entry requires roughly \(\$1{,}000\) in legal help; households without liquidity fail to file; distress moves into collection and wage garnishment; this is treated as a failure of financial design rather than just personal irresponsibility.

7. Draft-lottery natural experiment: define lottery number \(L\) by random birth-date draw; lower \(L\) means higher draft exposure; compare later earnings across lottery outcomes; use the result as evidence that random shocks materially affect lifetime economic paths.
