## Definitions And Objects
- Nonprofit organization: a legal corporation with a chartered purpose other than profit maximization; no shareholders; surplus is retained for mission.
- Corporate profits tax: a proportional government claim on firm profits; treated in the lecture as making government a partial claimant on the firm.
- Limited liability: shareholders lose invested equity but are not personally liable for catastrophic firm losses.
- Chapter 7: bankruptcy liquidation; assets sold off.
- Chapter 11: bankruptcy reorganization; operating business preserved if going-concern value remains.
- Chapter 9: municipal bankruptcy; applies to city or local government debt distress.
- Operating budget: current expenditures and taxes; this is the budget constrained by balanced-budget rules.
- Capital budget: long-lived infrastructure expenditure; can be debt-financed.
- Rainy day fund: reserve or endowment accumulated in better times to smooth bad times.
- Municipal bond: debt issued by state or local governments; lecture treats tax-exempt university debt as in the same broad category.
- Tax-exempt debt: debt whose interest is exempt from federal income tax in the lecture’s municipal-finance discussion.
- Endowment: stock of invested assets held by an institution such as Yale.
- EITC: Earned Income Tax Credit; can make net taxes negative for low earned income.
- OASDI: Old-Age, Survivors, and Disability Insurance; lecture transcript says `OASD`, but the object is the U.S. Social Security system.
- Workers’ compensation: government-backed insurance against workplace injury.
- Selection bias / adverse selection in disability insurance: people with private information about elevated disability risk disproportionately buy coverage.

## Derivation Steps
1. Corporate tax as implicit public equity claim: start with pretax profit \(\Pi\); government takes \(\tau_{\text{corp}}\Pi\); private claimants retain \((1-\tau_{\text{corp}})\Pi\); this is the lecture’s mathematical core for “partial nationalization.”
2. TEPCO public-private entanglement: shareholders enjoy limited liability; catastrophic loss exceeds private equity capacity; public authorities still bear cleanup and compensation burdens; the lecture uses the standing tax claim to argue that the public was already inside the firm.
3. Chapter 7 versus Chapter 11: insolvency triggers bankruptcy law; Chapter 7 destroys going concern and liquidates; Chapter 11 preserves operations if continuation has value; GM is placed in the Chapter 11 bucket because future operating value is asserted to exist.
4. Balanced-budget puzzle: state constitutions constrain the operating budget; capital projects are classified separately; capital budget may issue debt; therefore “balanced budget” and “municipal borrowing” coexist without contradiction.
5. New-town infrastructure logic: future city size is large but current tax base is tiny; sewer system is lumpy and must be built up front; current residents cannot fund it directly; debt shifts repayment toward future beneficiaries; if growth fails, bankruptcy risk appears.
6. Municipal bankruptcy logic: city has debt but no shareholders; it can tax residents, but excessive tax induces exit; repayment capacity is therefore endogenous to political and economic survival; this is why Chapter 9 is a subtler case than corporate bankruptcy.
7. Municipal-bond tax wedge: federal tax exemption lowers the required nominal yield on muni debt; high-bracket investors value the exemption more; municipal yields can still rise when default risk rises; tax status and credit risk move together in pricing.
8. Yale borrowing logic: Yale issues tax-exempt debt at a low rate; Yale holds or could hold higher-yield assets; as a tax-exempt institution it avoids tax on the asset side too; unrestricted arbitrage would seem attractive; legal constraints block free use of proceeds for pure spread capture.
9. Progressive-tax and EITC smoothing: low earnings can imply negative net taxes; after-tax income therefore falls less than pretax income after a bad shock; public services add further non-cash smoothing; the lecture classifies this as social insurance.
10. Social-insurance feasibility under Bismarck: workers contribute over decades; the state must preserve long-horizon records; cheap paper, carbon paper, typewriters, filing systems, post offices, and bureaucracy make this administratively possible; hence information technology is treated as a precondition for the finance.

## Notation Choices
- Use \(\Pi\) for surplus or profit; for nonprofits, interpret it as retained surplus rather than distributable shareholder profit.
- Use \(\tau_{\text{corp}}\) for corporate profit tax rates and \(\tau_{\text{inv}}\) for investor marginal income tax rates; do not reuse the same \(\tau\) without a subscript.
- Use \(T_t\) for tax revenue or net operating receipts, \(E_t^{\mathrm{op}}\) for operating expenditure, \(E_t^{\mathrm{cap}}\) for capital expenditure, and \(B_t\) for debt stock.
- Use \(P_0,P_1\) for before-and-after asset prices in the TEPCO example.
- Use \(s_i\) for ownership shares in the GM restructuring.
- Use \(A\) for assets or endowment and \(D\) for debt when writing Yale balance-sheet arithmetic.
- Use \(y_{\text{muni}}\) and \(y_{\text{taxable-equivalent}}\) for municipal-bond yields.
- Use \(T(y)\) for the net tax schedule inclusive of credits such as the EITC.
- Keep legal chapter labels as text objects: \(\text{Chapter 7}\), \(\text{Chapter 11}\), \(\text{Chapter 9}\).
- Use \(\approx\) for lecture-era figures and rough magnitudes; reserve \(=\) for arithmetic identities and explicitly quoted ownership shares.
