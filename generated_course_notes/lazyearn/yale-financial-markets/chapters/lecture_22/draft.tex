\chapter{Public and Non-Profit Finance}

This lecture broadens the course in a very deliberate way. We are asked to stop thinking of finance as a narrow theory of private firms and securities markets, and to see it instead as a general social technology for organizing incentives and managing risk. Once we adopt that wider point of view, nonprofit institutions, municipal borrowing, bankruptcy law, and social insurance all become part of one continuous financial story.

\section{Finance Beyond the Business Firm}

Shiller begins by restating a course-wide thesis. Finance is not merely about making money; it is about getting people to do good work together and about allocating risks that no one person should bear alone. In the lecture this claim is compressed almost to a formula:
\begin{equation}
\text{Finance} \approx \text{incentives} + \text{risk management}.
\end{equation}

The key move is then philosophical before it is institutional. We might object that some of the greatest achievements in history were the work of solitary geniuses. But the lecture blocks that objection one example at a time. Einstein still depended on formal education, journals, and a research community. Darwin still depended on private funding, a university world, and the patronage of Henslow. Even Homer, if we imagine him as an isolated poet, still required social transmission, travel, and support if the poems were to survive. The point is that good work is usually embedded in institutions, and institutions require financial structure.

Only after this widening move does the lecture announce its route. We will pass through four domains:
\begin{itemize}
\item nonprofit organizations,
\item government involvement in for-profit firms,
\item state and local project finance,
\item government social insurance.
\end{itemize}

That order matters. The lecture does not begin with tax law or municipal bonds. It first establishes that the conceptual problem is common across many institutional forms.

\section{Nonprofits as Mission-Driven Financial Forms}

\begin{definition}
A nonprofit organization is a legal corporation organized around a stated purpose other than the distribution of profit to owners. It has no shareholders in the ordinary sense, and any surplus is retained for the mission of the institution rather than paid out as equity income.
\end{definition}

This can be summarized schematically by
\begin{equation}
\Pi_{\text{nonprofit}} \longrightarrow \text{organizational mission},
\end{equation}
where \(\Pi_{\text{nonprofit}}\) denotes retained surplus rather than distributable shareholder profit.

The lecture insists that this is not a small corner of the economy. For the United States in 2010, Shiller quotes both the number of nonprofits and their aggregate scale:
\begin{equation}
N_{\text{U.S. nonprofits, 2010}} \approx 1.6\times 10^6,
\qquad
s_{\text{nonprofit in GDP}} \approx 4\%.
\end{equation}
Yale itself is the nearby example: a legal person without owners, governed by a board, and organized around a mission rather than shareholder payout.

The lecture then shifts from definition to possibility. Peter Tufano's Doorways to Dreams is presented as an institution that uses behavioral-finance ideas to help households save. One proposal is a tax-refund default into U.S.\ savings bonds. Another is prize-linked saving, which borrows the psychological appeal of lotteries while redirecting behavior toward balance-sheet improvement rather than expected loss. Dean Karlan's Innovations for Poverty Action then scales the picture up:
\begin{equation}
\text{IPA income}_{2010} \approx \$25\text{ million},
\qquad
\text{staff} \approx 500.
\end{equation}
Wendy Kopp supplies a different rhythm in the lecture: a student idea becomes an institution. Teach for America is introduced precisely because it began as a controversial educational proposal rather than as a conventional firm, and because the nonprofit form let the project be financed before it was politically settled:
\begin{equation}
\text{Teach for America initial fundraising} \approx \$2.5\text{ million}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Question \& Answer}

\paragraph{Question.} Why choose a nonprofit form rather than an ordinary for-profit form when the underlying project is socially valuable?

\paragraph{Answer.} The lecture offers several answers at once. First, the nonprofit form makes the mission legible: donors and counterparties know that the organizer is not privately extracting profits. Second, that mission clarity changes the atmosphere of fundraising. Third, the form creates room for projects that are too original, too controversial, or too morally charged to emerge first through ordinary government channels or through a standard profit-maximizing firm.

The New Haven hospitals deepen the point. Yale New Haven Hospital is traced back to a nonprofit origin in 1826. St.\ Raphael's is traced to physicians who saw a local need and then worked with the Sisters of Charity. The lecture does not present this as a simple morality play in which nonprofits are good and for-profits are bad. On the contrary, the hospital examples are used to show that social mission can live inside many forms, and that the distinction between for-profit and nonprofit can be morally and practically ambiguous. Still, affiliation with a religious or nonprofit institution can make purpose more visible and can attract donation more easily.

\section{Government Inside Private Enterprise}

The lecture now pivots from nonprofit form to a harder claim: governments are already inside apparently private enterprise. The first step is the corporate profits tax. Shiller treats this as a standing public claim on private profit:
\begin{equation}
\text{government claim on corporate profit}=\tau_{\text{corp}}\Pi,
\qquad
\text{private residual}=(1-\tau_{\text{corp}})\Pi.
\end{equation}
In that sense the government behaves like a partial claimant on the firm.

For the United States, the lecture gives the arithmetic in a stark way:
\begin{equation}
\tau_{\text{corp,U.S.}} = 35\% + \tau_{\text{state/local}},
\qquad
\tau_{\text{state/local}} \le 12\%,
\qquad
\tau_{\text{corp,U.S.}} \le 47\%.
\end{equation}
It then broadens the comparison:
\begin{equation}
\tau_{\text{Canada}} \approx 16.5\% + 16\%,
\qquad
\tau_{\text{Japan}} \approx 40.6\%,
\qquad
\tau_{\text{Brazil}} \approx 34\%,
\qquad
\tau_{\text{China}} \approx 25\%,
\qquad
\tau_{\text{India}} \approx 33\%.
\end{equation}
The lecture's interpretation is that the public-private division is already softened by taxation, regulation, and the mobility of business across borders.

\subsection{Question \& Answer}

\paragraph{Question.} In what sense is a private company partly public?

\paragraph{Answer.} The lecture answers this through tax law, limited liability, and crisis management. A firm may have shareholders and pursue profit, but it is also regulated by the state, taxed by the state, protected by legal rules created by the state, and sometimes rescued or restructured through state power. Once we account for those standing claims and interventions, ``private'' and ``public'' are no longer clean opposites.

The TEPCO example makes this vivid. The firm's share price collapse is presented numerically:
\begin{equation}
P_0^{\text{TEPCO}} \approx 25,
\qquad
P_1^{\text{TEPCO}} \approx 5,
\qquad
\frac{P_0^{\text{TEPCO}}-P_1^{\text{TEPCO}}}{P_0^{\text{TEPCO}}}=0.80.
\end{equation}
At the same time, estimated claims against the firm are placed on the order of
\begin{equation}
\text{Claims}_{\text{TEPCO}} \approx \$25\text{ billion}.
\end{equation}
The lecture's interpretation is not simply that TEPCO failed. It is that limited liability protects dispersed shareholders from personal liability, while the public still bears a large share of the social damage. The tax claim and the regulatory claim are therefore part of a larger public risk-sharing structure.

General Motors carries the point through bankruptcy law. The lecture distinguishes the main chapters in words. Chapter 7 is liquidation: the firm is shut down and its assets are sold. Chapter 11 is reorganization: the firm is insolvent now but still has enough going-concern value that continuation is worth preserving. GM is treated as a Chapter 11 case because the operating business is deemed salvageable.

The ownership outcome is then summarized by the lecture's headline shares:
\begin{equation}
s_{\text{U.S. govt in GM}}=60.8\%,
\qquad
s_{\text{Canada}}=12\%,
\qquad
s_{\text{UAW}}=17.5\%.
\end{equation}
At this point the lecture is no longer speaking metaphorically. A canonical American private company becomes, for a time, a government-owned organization.

Before moving on, the lecture briefly returns to incentives inside the nonprofit sector. The cited statistic is that
\begin{equation}
0.42 \text{ of nonprofits pay bonuses to executives.}
\end{equation}
The lesson is not that nonprofits have abandoned their missions. It is that incentive design remains necessary across institutional forms. The same course theme persists: people respond to money, but not only to money.

\section{State and Local Finance}

Shiller now moves from public involvement in firms to public finance in its own right. State and local governments do a very large share of the practical work of the social order: schools, police, fire departments, parks, hospitals, and infrastructure. The lecture-time claim is that state and local spending is on the order of twice federal spending. The point is not a modern budget fact-check. The point is that local public finance is analytically central.

The lecture then raises a puzzle. Every state in the United States, we are told, has a balanced-budget rule. Yet municipalities and states plainly borrow. How can both claims be true?

\subsection{Question \& Answer}

\paragraph{Question.} How can a government have a balanced-budget rule and still borrow heavily?

\paragraph{Answer.} Because the rule applies primarily to the operating budget, not to long-lived capital projects. The lecture resolves the apparent contradiction by distinguishing two budgets: one for current operations and one for durable investment. The first is constrained to balance; the second may be financed with debt.

In symbols, the lecture's structure can be written as
\begin{align}
T_t &\approx E_t^{\mathrm{op}},\\
E_t^{\mathrm{cap}} &> 0 \quad \text{for long-lived public investment},\\
\Delta B_t &\approx E_t^{\mathrm{cap}} \quad \text{in the simple case of debt finance}.
\end{align}
Here \(T_t\) is current tax revenue, \(E_t^{\mathrm{op}}\) current operating expenditure, \(E_t^{\mathrm{cap}}\) capital expenditure, and \(B_t\) debt.

The new-town example is the lecture's worked public-finance derivation. We imagine a very small town today, a much larger town tomorrow, and a lumpy infrastructure project that must be built before the population arrives:
\begin{equation}
n_{\text{current}} \approx 20\text{ to }30,
\qquad
n_{\text{planned}} \approx 20{,}000,
\qquad
C_{\text{sewer}} \approx \$100\text{ million}.
\end{equation}
The logic then unfolds step by step. The sewer system is indivisible at the relevant scale; it should be built for the anticipated future city rather than piecemeal for a tiny current population. The present residents cannot pay \(C_{\text{sewer}}\) out of current taxes. Debt therefore shifts repayment toward those future residents who will actually benefit from the completed infrastructure. In that sense borrowing is not fiscal indiscipline; it is intertemporal fairness.

But the lecture immediately adds the darker side. What if the population does not arrive? What if the forecast is wrong? Then the municipality has borrowed against a future tax base that never appears, and the city can be pushed toward financial distress.

That is where Chapter 9 enters. Municipal bankruptcy differs from corporate bankruptcy because a city has no shareholders. What it does have is taxing power. Yet that taxing power is not unlimited, because taxes that rise too far can induce residents to leave. Municipal solvency is therefore tied to political legitimacy and local economic survival at the same time.

The lecture notes that municipal bankruptcies have historically been rare, and suggests several reasons. One is bailout. New York City in the 1970s is the leading example: distress did not culminate in a formal bankruptcy filing because higher levels of government intervened. Another is self-insurance through rainy day funds. A city may tax somewhat more in better times and retain the surplus as a reserve. Yet the lecture places us after the financial crisis, at a moment when many such funds had already been exhausted. That is why municipal bankruptcy risk is not just a legal curiosity in the lecture; it becomes a live pricing question.

At this point the blackboard itself records the lecture's movement: first Chapter 9, then rainy day funds, then a tax point. The board is not giving us a derivation. It is showing the order of thought. The municipal borrower is risky enough that bankruptcy must be discussed; reserve accumulation partly offsets that risk; and then tax treatment changes the pricing of the debt.

The lecture's central statement is:
\begin{equation}
\text{Interest on municipal bonds is exempt from federal income tax.}
\end{equation}
That is the mathematical core of the municipal-bond discussion. The interest is exempt, not the default risk. Hence a municipal bond may offer a lower nominal yield than a comparable taxable bond and still be attractive.

We can formalize the investor's indifference condition in the standard way. If a taxable bond yields \(y_{\text{taxable}}\), then its after-tax return is \((1-\tau_{\text{inv}})y_{\text{taxable}}\). If a municipal bond yields \(y_{\text{muni}}\) and is exempt from federal income tax, then indifference requires
\begin{equation}
y_{\text{muni}}=(1-\tau_{\text{inv}})y_{\text{taxable-equivalent}},
\end{equation}
or equivalently
\begin{equation}
y_{\text{taxable-equivalent}}=\frac{y_{\text{muni}}}{1-\tau_{\text{inv}}}.
\end{equation}
This makes precise the lecture's claim that high-income investors are especially drawn to municipal bonds: a higher marginal tax rate increases the taxable-equivalent yield of any given municipal yield.

The lecture also notes that yields on municipal bonds had risen in the crisis period. That is not a contradiction. Tax exemption lowers required nominal yield, but increased default or restructuring risk raises it. The market price of a municipal bond is therefore balancing at least two distinct forces:
\begin{itemize}
\item tax privilege,
\item credit risk.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Question \& Answer}

\paragraph{Question.} Why would Yale borrow money if it already has a very large endowment?

\paragraph{Answer.} The lecture's immediate answer is that Yale can borrow on tax-subsidized terms. Tax exemption lowers the borrowing rate, and so debt can be attractive even for a wealthy institution. That observation then opens a more subtle question about whether one could exploit the spread between cheap tax-exempt borrowing and higher-yield assets.

The lecture's arithmetic is:
\begin{equation}
A_{\text{Yale}} \approx \$16\text{ billion},
\qquad
D_{\text{Yale}} \approx \$2.5\text{ billion},
\qquad
A_{\text{Yale}}-D_{\text{Yale}} \approx \$13.5\text{ billion}.
\end{equation}
At first glance one might think that an institution with \(A_{\text{Yale}}\) this large should have no reason to issue debt. But if tax-exempt debt can be issued at a low yield, then the institution faces an apparent spread:
\begin{equation}
\text{apparent spread}=r_{\text{taxable asset}}-r_{\text{tax-exempt debt}}.
\end{equation}
This is the lecture's ``arbitrage game.'' A tax-exempt borrower could seem to issue low-yield debt and invest in higher-yield assets. The lecture immediately adds the necessary caveat: this is not an unrestricted free lunch. The use of proceeds is constrained. Debt still makes sense, but not as an unlimited machine for extracting tax arbitrage.

The municipal-bond section closes by returning to institutional design. State governments have operating and capital budgets; the federal government, in the lecture's telling, does not use the same distinction in a clean way. This is one reason state and local finance can appear, in this narrow respect, more structurally explicit than federal budgeting.

\section{Social Insurance and the Technology of Administration}

The lecture now moves to its broadest public-finance application. If finance is about risk management, then one of its largest modern embodiments is social insurance.

\begin{definition}
Social insurance is government-provided insurance against major adverse states of life or income, especially in domains where private insurance is incomplete, too expensive, or structurally difficult to provide on socially acceptable terms.
\end{definition}

The lecture begins with taxes rather than pensions. Properly stated, progressive taxation means that higher income is taxed at a higher marginal rate, not a lower one. The lecture's substantive point, however, is about the addition of the Earned Income Tax Credit. For sufficiently low earned income the net tax schedule may turn negative:
\begin{equation}
T(y) < 0 \qquad \text{for part of the low-income range.}
\end{equation}
This is not merely redistribution in a moral sense. It is also smoothing. A shock to pretax earnings is only partly transmitted to after-tax income.

The same logic extends to public services. Education and other publicly funded goods insure people against very bad economic states by guaranteeing access to certain foundational goods even when private income is low. The lecture then lists the major U.S.\ objects of social insurance: old-age pensions, survivors' insurance, disability insurance, health insurance, and workers' compensation.

A compact way to summarize the structure is
\begin{equation}
\text{current contributions} \longrightarrow \text{future pension / survivor / disability benefits}.
\end{equation}
The lecture wants us to see that all of these are financial devices. They are long-term contingent claims organized by the state.

There is also a market-completion point. Old-age income, life insurance, and disability insurance can all in principle be purchased privately. But private markets are not enough. Disability insurance, in particular, is vulnerable to adverse selection: people who know they are at greater risk will seek coverage more aggressively than those who do not. The lecture therefore treats social insurance as a domain in which government organization is not accidental but structurally connected to the information problem.

\subsection{Question \& Answer}

\paragraph{Question.} Why did social insurance emerge so late in history if the underlying human risks were always present?

\paragraph{Answer.} Because large-scale social insurance requires administrative technology. It is not enough to have compassion or political will. One must be able to identify people, record contributions, keep long-lived accounts, transmit documents, copy forms, and enforce standardized procedures across a large territory.

The lecture's historical focal point is Bismarckian Germany in the 1880s. The system is described as covering on the order of
\begin{equation}
\text{workers in the Bismarck system} \approx 11\text{ million},
\qquad
H_{\text{record-keeping}} \approx 30\text{ to }40\text{ years}.
\end{equation}
That horizon matters. A retirement system is not a one-year transfer program. It is a mechanism in which the state must preserve a contribution history over decades and then honor claims far in the future.

This is why the lecture ends with paper, carbon paper, typewriters, filing cabinets, management schools, bureaucracy, and the postal system. These are not digressions. They are the infrastructure of financial administration. An early English attempt at wage supplementation failed largely because income could not be observed and verified well enough. Bismarckian Germany succeeded not simply because the idea was noble, but because the information technology had matured enough to make the idea operable.

In the lecture's final compression, technology drives finance. New financial forms become feasible when the administrative machinery required to support them becomes cheap and reliable enough.

\section{Summary}

The chapter has moved through several institutional forms, but the analytical thread has remained stable. Nonprofits solve incentive and trust problems when mission must be made visible. Corporate taxation, regulation, limited liability, and bankruptcy law show that apparently private firms already live inside public structures. State and local governments borrow because infrastructure is lumpy and because repayment should often be spread across future beneficiaries. Municipal bonds are priced through the joint action of tax privilege and credit risk. Social insurance extends the same logic of risk management to the scale of an entire society.

The lecture therefore leaves us with a broader definition of finance than the one with which many courses begin. Finance is a set of arrangements by which we induce cooperation, allocate burdens across time, and protect people and institutions against bad states of the world. Once that is understood, the difference between nonprofit, for-profit, and government activity becomes less absolute than it first appears, and more like a family of related mechanisms for organizing social purpose.
