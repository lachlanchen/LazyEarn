## Derivation Steps

Dry-cleaning scale model
1. Start with one central factory \(F\) that does the real cleaning and pressing.
2. Reinterpret each storefront \(S_i\) as a tagging-and-bagging node, not a full production plant.
3. Let vans move garments from \(S_i\) to \(F\) and back.
4. Then throughput aggregates as \(Q=\sum_i q_i\) instead of each site carrying a separate full cost stack.
5. This lowers required storefront footprint and makes many small nodes feasible.
6. If outsiders also send garments to \(F\), competitors become wholesale customers.
7. The scaling claim is therefore: own the core process, distribute the intake points.

Store-level unit economics
1. Use the spoken price point \(p_{\text{shirt}}\approx \$1.99\).
2. Use the spoken rough unit-cost figure \(c_{\text{unit}}\approx \$0.40\) cautiously.
3. Write revenue as \(R=pQ\).
4. Subtract processing, storefront, transport, and factory costs to get \(\Pi\).
5. Treat the \(\$50{,}000/\text{month}\) claim as an asserted outcome, not a derived theorem.

From first million to sustainable flow
1. Distinguish one-year wealth from recurring wealth.
2. Ask what keeps the cash flow recurring after the first big year.
3. The lecture answers behaviorally first: reduce lifestyle inflation and status consumption.
4. Therefore wealth preservation is modeled as a control on spending drift before it is modeled as an investment-return problem.

Service-quality sales model
1. The surgeon denies “selling” in the ordinary sense.
2. He substitutes care quality and trust for aggressive salesmanship.
3. This implies a causal chain: quality \(\to\) trust \(\to\) demand/referrals.
4. The chapter can formalize this as a service-driven acquisition mechanism, not a hard-close funnel.

Rights-window leverage
1. Control a scarce distribution channel.
2. Deliver content directly into that channel with owned tech.
3. Aggregate attention at scale, here stated as \(54\) million travelers per month.
4. Turn that attention into leverage with leagues, partners, advertisers, and even competitors.
5. Therefore category power comes from controlled access, not just content ownership.

Private-equity and bank-spread logic
1. Prefer businesses with actual cash generation.
2. Treat EBITDA-only stories as weaker than debt-serviceable cash flow.
3. Use \(D_{\max}=f(CF_{\text{actual}})\) as the underwriting intuition.
4. For banks, profit comes from the rate spread \(s_{\text{bank}}=r_{\text{loan}}-r_{\text{funding}}\).
5. The final guest uses this to shift discussion from glamour to boring but financeable systems.

Persistence state transition
1. Initial approach often produces “no.”
2. The host recodes “no” as “not yet.”
3. That preserves the search process instead of terminating it.
4. In chapter diagrams, treat this as a state transition, not as a literal algebraic equality.

- Use \(\Pi\) for profit, not \(P\), to avoid conflict with price and probability.
- Use \(p\) for unit selling price and \(c_{\text{unit}}\) for unit processing cost.
- Use \(S_i\) for storefront nodes and \(F\) for the factory.
- Use \(q_i\) for local throughput and \(Q\) for total throughput.
- Use \(CF_{\text{actual}}\) for real cash flow and \(E_{\text{EBITDA}}\) for EBITDA.
- Use \(D_{\max}\) for debt capacity; do not introduce a full leverage model beyond the lecture’s level.
- Use \(r_{\text{loan}}\) and \(r_{\text{funding}}\) for the spread formula.
- Use \(N_{\text{travel}}\) for monthly audience scale in the Reach TV case.
- Treat “no \to not yet” as state-transition notation, not \( \text{no} = \text{not yet} \).
- Keep dollar values inline as \(\$\) amounts since that matches the transcript’s idiom.
