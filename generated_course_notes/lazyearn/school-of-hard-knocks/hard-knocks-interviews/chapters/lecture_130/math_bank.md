## Definitions And Objects
- \(M\): money level; use as a broad scalar for available financial resources, not as a precise balance-sheet quantity.
- \(H(M)\): happiness or well-being as a function of money; editorial notation only.
- \(M_*\): the “base level” or sufficiency threshold where extra money stops adding much happiness.
- \(\Delta H / \Delta M\): marginal happiness gain from additional money; use finite-difference language rather than heavy calculus unless needed.
- Poverty: the low-\(M\) regime described by childhood scarcity and labor.
- Bill-paying sufficiency: the upper bound of the verbally identified transition zone.

## Derivation Steps
Threshold claim from the transcript
1. Start with the global question: does money buy happiness?
2. Record the immediate rejection of the unrestricted claim.
3. Reintroduce a restricted claim: money does help at some levels.
4. Use the poverty autobiography as the empirical reason that low-\(M\) gains matter.
5. Infer a regime change: the effect of money is positive in deprivation, not uniformly positive at all wealth levels.

Saturation claim from the luxury examples
1. Move from necessity to surplus.
2. Test the surplus regime with repeated examples: champagne, women, beaches.
3. Read these examples as repeated claims that qualitative experience is no longer improving with added money.
4. Generalize to “just making more and more money” as the variable being increased.
5. Conclude that marginal happiness gains are small or negligible beyond sufficiency.

Base-level localization
1. Do not assign a number to the threshold.
2. Keep the threshold as an interval-like verbal bound.
3. Place it between poverty and paying all your bills.
4. Preserve that this is a probabilistic estimate, not a proved cutoff.

## Notation Choices
- Use \(M\) for money, not \(W\) or \(I\), because the transcript repeatedly says “money.”
- Use \(H\) for happiness; avoid importing utility notation \(u(c)\) unless a later chapter makes that framework explicit.
- Use \(M_*\) for the base level; avoid \(M_c\), \(M_0\), or “critical wealth” because the speaker’s phrasing is practical, not technical.
- Prefer \(\Delta H / \Delta M\) over \(dH/dM\) unless the prose explicitly needs a smooth-curve interpretation.
- Keep inequality language qualitative: “positive below threshold,” “approximately flat above threshold.”
- Do not introduce expectation operators, optimization symbols, or welfare-maximization notation.
