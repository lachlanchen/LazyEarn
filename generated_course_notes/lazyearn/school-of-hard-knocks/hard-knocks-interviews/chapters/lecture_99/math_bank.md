## Derivation Steps

Capacity bound to institutional scale

1. Start with the single-operator case: one doctor with one medical bag, or one founder doing everything.
2. Direct impact is then capped by personal hours and attention.
3. If more output is required, the founder must stop treating every task as non-transferable.
4. Transfer requires process capture: write down what is said and done.
5. Process capture alone is insufficient; people must also be selected and trained.
6. Even after delegation, the founder must supervise because a new CEO or manager can still drive the firm downward.
7. Therefore scaling comes from institution-building, not from merely working harder.

Income to deployable capital

1. Reported high income is not identical to spendable wealth.
2. Taxes remove a first slice.
3. Reinvestment removes another slice because growth consumes capital.
4. The relevant variable for future moves is therefore deployable cash, not gross earnings.
5. This is why AK refuses a naive reading of “a couple million” as pure discretionary money.

First-business threshold

1. The first business creates the initial capital base.
2. That base creates comfort and optionality.
3. Optionality makes later investments easier to initiate.
4. The “first million is the hardest” is therefore functioning as a threshold claim about capital accumulation.
5. The lecture treats that threshold as practical, not as a precise theorem.

Self-improvement feedback loop

1. A mistake, failure, or bad hire occurs.
2. Do not stop at external blame.
3. Ask what should have been seen earlier.
4. Convert that answer into better selection, better training, better background checking, or better preparation.
5. Repeated self-audit is the mechanism by which growth occurs.

Entrepreneurial phase transition

1. Early on, the problem is getting started: knowledge, money, and personal readiness.
2. Once started, the problem becomes scale: systems, people, and delegation.
3. After scale, the problem becomes diversification: larger bets with larger downside.
4. After enough success, the problem becomes balance: family, time, and identity.
5. The lecture’s claim is that “entrepreneurship” is not one fixed optimization problem but a sequence of changing ones.

## Notation Choices

- Use \(H\) for hours or effective attention, not abstract time \(t\), unless an iterative process is explicitly being described.
- Use \(O\) for output or organizational throughput.
- Use Roman caps \(\mathrm{SOP}\) for standard operating procedure.
- Use \(R,T,I,C_{\text{deploy}}\) for finance: gross earnings, taxes, reinvestment, deployable cash.
- Use \(K_{\text{base}}\) for the first durable capital base created by the first successful business.
- Use \(S_0,S_1,S_2,S_3\) for entrepreneurial phases, with arrows \(\to\) indicating phase progression rather than causal certainty.
- Reserve \(\alpha_f,\alpha_i\) for editorial productivity coefficients only; do not overinterpret them as measurable data from the source.
- Keep inequalities qualitative: \(\lesssim\) for capacity bounds, \(\approx\) for rough numerical claims, and exact equality only for bookkeeping identities like \(C_{\text{deploy}} = R - T - I\).
- Do not introduce hats, operators, expectation symbols, or probabilistic notation unless a later drafting step truly needs them.
