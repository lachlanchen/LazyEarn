## Derivation Steps

Spending replacement rule:
1. Let \(S\) be the amount spent.
2. The private-equity operator treats the spend as more costly than its face value.
3. He states the replacement burden as “spend one, make two.”
4. Preserve this as the heuristic \(E_{\text{replace}}(S)=2S\), not as strict accounting identity.

Capital deployment rule:
1. The yacht owner contrasts money seen in the bank with money put to use.
2. Idle cash is treated as non-productive.
3. Deployed cash is attached to businesses, boats, and other operating assets.
4. Encode only the ordering \(r_{\text{deploy}} > r_{\text{idle}}\).

Giving-growth ladder:
1. Scarcity at age \(11\) creates the operative belief that strangers care.
2. Robbins sets the first concrete target at \(2\) families.
3. The sequence expands to \(4\) and then \(8\).
4. Later the unit changes to \(Q_{\text{fed/year}}=4\times10^6\) people.
5. The later target changes again to \(M_{\text{target}}=10^9\) meals.
6. Keep the ladder, but do not merge the units.

Marketplace inequality:
1. Hold the time interval fixed.
2. The lecture asks whether some people make \(2\times\), \(10\times\), or \(50\times\) more in that same interval.
3. Jim Rohn’s answer is not innate superiority but greater value delivered to others.
4. Preserve the schematic relation \(Y \propto V_{\text{market}}\).

Access-through-value:
1. Early access to high-value people is initially weak.
2. Robbins solves this by working near them and adding value first.
3. Added value creates more trust and more access.
4. Preserve the process as \(\text{access} \to \text{add value} \to \text{more access}\).

Fear and courage:
1. Fear is present.
2. “Fearless” is redefined as fearing less, not having zero fear.
3. Action is still taken under fear.
4. Preserve the result as \(\text{courage} = \text{fear present} + \text{action anyway}\).

Owner leverage:
1. Early diversification without mastery spreads the entrepreneur thin.
2. First acquire mastery in one business.
3. Then move from operator to owner.
4. Owner-scale requires leaders, strategy, and leverage.
5. Preserve the chain \(\text{operator} \to \text{owner} \to \text{leaders} \to \text{scale}\).

Net accounting:
1. Jake Paul gives a current-year figure only after the net/gross distinction is raised.
2. He explicitly says to count net, not gross.
3. Therefore income variables should default to \(Y_{\text{net}}\) when the lecture is making a real wealth claim.

## Notation Choices

- Use Roman letters with descriptive subscripts; avoid abstract physics-style notation.
- Use \(P\) for price, \(N\) for counts, \(R\) for annual revenue scale, and \(G\) for “generated amount” when the transcript does not specify valuation.
- Use \(Y\) for income / earnings and always distinguish \(Y_{\text{gross}}\) from \(Y_{\text{net}}\) when that issue matters.
- Use \(S\) only for the spending amount in the spend-one / make-two heuristic.
- Use \(E_{\text{replace}}(S)\) for replacement earnings burden.
- Use \(V_{\text{market}}\) for value delivered to others; do not rename this as utility or productivity unless the final chapter explicitly explains the shift.
- Use \(F_n\), \(Q_{\text{fed/year}}\), and \(M_{\text{target}}\) to keep families, people, and meals separate.
- Use arrows \(\to\) only for process chains or business-state transitions.
- Use \(\propto\) only when explicitly labeled as note-writer reconstruction.
- Keep currencies exactly as spoken; do not silently convert euros to dollars.
- Do not introduce compound-interest formulas, discount factors, expectations, or optimization operators. The lecture mentions compounding, but does not write a formula.
