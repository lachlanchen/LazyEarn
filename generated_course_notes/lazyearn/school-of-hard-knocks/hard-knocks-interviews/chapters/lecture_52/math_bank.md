## Derivation Steps

Sales yes-ladder
1. Begin with repeated attempts: \(N_{\text{doors}}=100\).
2. Treat each rejection as information rather than terminal failure.
3. Replace direct pitch-first behavior with a sequence of easy affirmative questions.
4. Accumulate local agreement: \(\text{yes},\text{ yes},\text{ yes}\).
5. Use that agreement to soften resistance to the actual ask.
6. State the outcome cautiously as higher \(P(\text{yes on ask }4\text{ or }5)\), not as a measured theorem.

Scarcity to operating discipline
1. Anchor the broke phase with \(R_{\text{rent}} \approx \$1{,}300\).
2. Add the survival detail: sale of wedding jewelry to cover rent.
3. Infer a no-subscription / save-first discipline from the transcript.
4. Convert the anecdote into a threshold rule: sacrifice first, reward later.
5. Keep the broken subscription sub-passage qualitative rather than algebraic.

Fast-unicorn timeline
1. Start company in April 2021.
2. Measure early scale window as \(\Delta t_{\text{raise}} \approx 11\) months.
3. Insert capital event: \(M_{\text{raised}} > \$100\) million.
4. Insert valuation event: \(V_{\text{company}} \approx \$1.5\) billion.
5. Conclude that “rich quick” is being framed as timed industry selection plus capital access, not pure speed alone.

Crypto friction and liquidity comparison
1. State the speaker’s anti-bank premise: bank intermediation extracts value from deposited money.
2. Translate the crypto transfer claim into \(c_{\text{crypto}}(\$10\,\text{million}) \approx \$1\).
3. Add the liquidity contrast: \(T_{\text{crypto exit}}(\$15\,\text{million}) \approx 1\) minute.
4. Keep the real-estate side schematic because no precise liquidation time is given.
5. Preserve all of this as speaker doctrine, not settled market law.

Networking to business formation
1. Remove transactional intent from the opening interaction.
2. Begin with person-first contact rather than work-first contact.
3. Build shared affinity.
4. Let affinity become trust.
5. Let trust become business possibility.
6. Record the mechanism as a pipeline, not a strict causal proof.

Execution over idea quality
1. Introduce the comparator example: \(S_{\text{Snuggie}} \approx \$100\) million.
2. Note that the product is rhetorically presented as a “terrible idea.”
3. Attribute success to execution rather than idea elegance.
4. Encode the thesis as
   \[
   \text{bad idea executed well}
   \;>\;
   \text{great idea executed poorly}.
   \]

Tech scale and go-to-market
1. Contrast digital goods with physical goods.
2. Compress that contrast into \(MC_{\text{digital}} \ll MC_{\text{physical}}\).
3. Add the speaker’s scale claim: software can replicate without physical production bottlenecks.
4. Shift from product to distribution: go-to-market determines reach.
5. Conclude that scale is global once the product is digital and the channel is online.

Future-state investing
1. Refuse present trend-chasing.
2. Project the world forward by five years.
3. Ask what problems must be solved in that future state.
4. Build now for those future conditions.
5. Encode the rule as \(\text{build at } t \text{ for the world at } t+5\).
6. Attach examples only as illustrations: AI, automation, batteries, synthetic data, AI-avatar media.

Stage-dependent capital allocation
1. Start in scarcity: no money available, so concentration dominates.
2. Express that as \(K_{\text{early}} \to \text{all-in concentration}\).
3. Move to post-scale wealth: capital preservation and balance become relevant.
4. Express that as \(K_{\text{late}} \to \text{diversification}\).
5. Add the narrative bridge: losses teach knowledge, knowledge can be redeployed, teams extend scale.

## Notation Choices

- Use \(t\) for time durations and entrepreneurial age-in-business measures.
- Use \(Y\) for annual income or annual turnover claims; qualify with superscripts or subscripts to avoid mixing personal income and business revenue.
- Use \(W\) for net worth.
- Use \(R\) only for rent in this lecture; do not overload it with “return.”
- Use \(M\) for money raised and \(V\) for company valuation.
- Use \(N\) for countable attempts such as sales doors knocked.
- Use \(n_{\text{ask}}\) for ask index inside a persuasion sequence.
- Use \(P(\text{yes on ask }k)\) only as an informal probability notation; do not imply empirical calibration.
- Use \(c_{\text{crypto}}(x)\) for transaction-cost claims and \(T_{\text{crypto exit}}(x)\) for liquidity-time claims.
- Use \(MC_{\text{digital}}\) and \(MC_{\text{physical}}\) for marginal-cost comparison; do not introduce fixed-cost notation unless later lectures require it.
- Use arrow notation \(\to\) for process logic, pipelines, stage changes, and historical sector ladders.
- Use \(\approx\) for spoken approximate figures, \(>\) for explicitly stated lower bounds, and avoid exact equality when the transcript is conversational.
- Keep money units in dollars inside the formulas, since the transcript itself speaks in dollar-denominated benchmarks even when the scene is Dubai.
- Treat “Web3,” “AI,” “blockchain,” “social media,” and “internet” as sector labels, not variables.
- Do not introduce operators, expectation brackets, optimization notation, or formal balance-sheet symbols absent from the lecture.
