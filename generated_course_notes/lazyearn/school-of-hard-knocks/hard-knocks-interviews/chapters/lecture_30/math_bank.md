## Derivation Steps

### 1. Sales volume to expected closes
1. Treat each qualified meeting as one sales trial.
2. Let \(n\) be the number of qualified encounters.
3. Let \(p\) be the chance that one encounter closes.
4. Expected closes are then
   \[
   \mathbb{E}[S] = np.
   \]
5. This is the compact form of “the more people you see, the more chances you have at making a sale.”

### 2. Talking past the sale as a confusion penalty
1. Buyer intent is already live.
2. Extra options are introduced after the buyer is ready to buy.
3. Let \(c\) measure confusion from those extra options.
4. Model the close probability as \(p_{\text{close}}(c)\).
5. As \(c\) increases, \(p_{\text{close}}(c)\) falls.
6. Therefore the lecture’s rule is: wrap the sale instead of widening the choice set.

### 3. Walk-away willingness to leverage
1. A negotiator with no outside option is structurally cornered.
2. Let \(O\) encode outside options and \(w\) encode willingness to walk.
3. Define leverage as \(L=L(O,w)\).
4. Increasing either \(O\) or \(w\) increases \(L\).
5. Ross’s verbal claim becomes: the person most willing to walk away has the most leverage.

### 4. Attention into monetization
1. First acquire attention \(A\).
2. Convert attention into a loyal audience base \(B\).
3. Loyal audience becomes monetizable demand.
4. Revenue \(R\) is then generated through several channels.
5. Hence the compact mechanism:
   \[
   A \to B \to R.
   \]

### 5. Clip funnel to live-stream demand
1. Start with a long live stream of duration \(T_{\text{live}}\).
2. Extract a short clip of duration \(t_{\text{clip}}\).
3. Use the clip as a viral unit.
4. Viral exposure redirects viewers to the main live stream.
5. Monetization happens downstream of that redirected live audience.
6. The funnel is therefore
   \[
   \text{live stream} \to \text{clip} \to \text{viral reach} \to \text{live viewers} \to R.
   \]

### 6. Rough June stream arithmetic
1. Take the lecture’s June numbers:
   \[
   N_{\text{streams}}=18,\qquad R_{\text{June}}=\$623{,}000.
   \]
2. Divide total June revenue by number of streams.
3. Obtain
   \[
   \bar R_{\text{stream}} \approx \frac{623{,}000}{18}\approx \$34{,}611.
   \]
4. Keep this as an editorial average only, not a native platform metric.

### 7. Equity dominating quick cash
1. A cash deal gives fixed immediate value \(C\).
2. An equity deal gives ownership fraction \(\alpha\) in firm value \(V\).
3. If \(V\) scales sufficiently, the payoff becomes \(\alpha V\).
4. When \(\alpha V > C\), equity dominates immediate cash.
5. This captures Ross’s “ownership is king” logic.

- Use \(N_{\text{host}}\) for the host’s followers, not \(F\), to avoid collision with fan-base notation.

- Reserve \(p\) for close probability in the sales model.

- Use \(R\) for total revenue and subscript it by source:
  \(R_{\text{subs}}, R_{\text{tips}}, R_{\text{KCIP}}, R_{\text{brand}}, R_{\text{equity}}\).
- Use \(r_h(v)\) only for hourly platform pay as a function of viewers; this keeps the Kik heuristic separate from total monthly revenue.
- Use arrows \(\to\) for process / mechanism flows, not for identities.
- Use \(\approx\) for speaker-level numerical claims and rough arithmetic.
- Use strict \(>\) only where the transcript clearly gives a lower bound, such as “a little more than \$30M.”
- Keep dollar-denominated variables visibly financial:
  \(P_{\text{house}}, R_{\text{June}}, \bar R_{\text{stream}}, C\).
- Treat rhetorical rules such as “2 ears, 1 mouth” and “1.5x or 2x is enough” as heuristics, not theorem-like equalities.
