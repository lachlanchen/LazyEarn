## Derivation Steps
Opportunity allocation
1. Start with complaint: others are receiving more chances.
2. Introduce the correction: the actionable set is not others' opportunities but one's own.
3. Replace grievance about \(O_{\text{others}}\) with action on \(O_{\text{given}}\).
4. Brady's “three opportunities in practice” example supplies the local unit of action.
5. Accountability then becomes the rule that closes the loop: use what is actually given.

Self-belief construction
1. Early high school and college do not supply immediate recognition.
2. Repeated adversity forces strategy revision rather than passive waiting.
3. Trial and error plus failure generate learned resilience.
4. Resilience accumulated in obscurity later appears as self-confidence at the pro level.
5. Hence \(B_t\) is not assumed at the start; it is updated through survived difficulty.

Pressure programming
1. Pressure is not treated as an external shock but as something we can stage in advance.
2. Brady makes practice emotionally resemble the game.
3. He assigns concrete decisive-game semantics to ordinary reps: fourth down, fourth and seven, one shot, win-or-don't.
4. Repeated exposure reduces novelty and increases familiarity.
5. Later game pressure is then processed as already-known experience rather than unprecedented crisis.

Super Bowl 51 deficit logic
1. Define deficit \(D=\text{opponent} - \text{own}\).
2. The lecture explicitly marks a \(21\text{-}3\) state and then a \(28\text{-}3\) state.
3. The pressure problem worsens numerically, so the anti-quit principle is tested under increasing \(D\).
4. Belichick's “21 points won't be enough” statement functions as a refusal to let present deficit determine future outcome.
5. Brady's line about maybe 28 being enough marks the moment of maximum doubt.
6. The comeback is presented as proof that fight must persist even when the state worsens.

Effort gradient across season outcomes
1. Reject the idea that “playing one game” captures the real problem.
2. Compare season records rather than isolated appearances.
3. Order the records from weak to elite: \(4\text{-}13\), \(9\text{-}8\), \(12\text{-}5\), \(15\text{-}2\).
4. Attach increasing required discipline, habits, and actions to that ordering.
5. Conclude that excellence is sustained through repeated daily choice, not occasional performance.

## Notation Choices
- Use \(O\) for opportunities, \(B\) for belief, \(T\) for time horizon, \(S\) for state/stakes, \(D\) for score deficit, \(E\) for effort, and \(J\) for objective.
- Use subscripts with descriptive labels rather than dense symbolic indices: \(O_{\text{given}}, O_{\text{others}}, S_{\text{practice}}, S_{\text{game}}\).
- Use arrows \(\to\) for causal or training-direction claims, not for strict logical implication or equality.
- Use \(\sim\) only for approximate state matching, never to claim literal identity.
- Keep score strings exactly in transcript form as \(21\text{-}3\) and \(28\text{-}3\); define the deficit separately as \(D\).
- Keep season records as text-formatted subscripts: \(E_{4\text{-}13}\), not \(E_{4-13}\) if hyphen ambiguity would be visually messy in LaTeX.
- Do not introduce neuroscience symbols, utility functions, or optimization machinery beyond the minimal \(J_{\text{self}}\) shorthand.
- Treat “fourth and seven” as a concrete rehearsal state, not as full football-play notation.
- Keep any reference to “programmed” or “body knows” schematic and metaphor-sensitive; no biological formalism.
