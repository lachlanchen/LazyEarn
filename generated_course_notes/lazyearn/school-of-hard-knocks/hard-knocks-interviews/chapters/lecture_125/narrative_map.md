# Narrative Map
## Opening Setup
The opening is practical and targeted. We are not dropped into a general sermon about wealth; we are first given a career-choice frame, then an engineering identity, and only then the broader wealth question. That sequencing matters because it tells the reader how to hear the answer: as guidance for builders deciding where to aim their effort.

The companion text should preserve that rhythm. We begin from a concrete question asked by someone with technical training, then let the speaker widen the scope from career selection to sector-level opportunity.

## Beat Sequence
1. Career-direction prompt  
The interviewer opens by asking what industry one should pursue, which establishes the conversation as a decision problem rather than a biography. It appears first to anchor the discussion in action, and it leads naturally to the interviewer’s self-positioning as an engineer.

2. Engineer’s self-location  
The line “I’m an engineer” narrows the frame and tells us the question comes from a technically minded listener who wants a usable map of opportunity. It appears here to qualify the kind of answer being requested, and it leads into the more explicit wealth question.

3. [Question & Answer] Wealth puzzle stated  
The interviewer then sharpens the issue into a direct puzzle: how can someone become wealthy in 2022? This is the clip’s cleanest tension point, and it should later survive as a standalone `Question & Answer` subsection because the next move is a compact resolution.

4. Immediate thesis before proof  
The speaker answers first: the next 1,000 unicorns will be in green tech. This appears immediately after the question to give the clip force and direction, and it leads into the need for justification.

5. Present-scale baseline  
The speaker begins defending the thesis by quantifying the present world: about $90 trillion in economic activity, with roughly 80% dependence on fossil fuels. This appears here because the thesis needs a structural base, and it leads into the future horizon where that base is stressed.

6. Future-scale pivot  
The lecture then jumps to 2050 and adds two linked conditions: the world economy will be about twice as large, and it will be net zero. This appears at this moment to convert a static baseline into a transition problem, and it leads directly to the entrepreneurial inference.

7. Recap into commercial conclusion  
The final lines restate the thesis in light of the new scale-and-constraint picture: that is why the next 1,000 unicorns will be in green tech, and that is where one should go. This closing beat appears as a recap rather than a new idea, sealing the argument by returning to the opening career question.

## Transition Cues
- The setup moves by narrowing the audience: “What industry…” becomes “I’m an engineer,” which tells us whose problem is being solved.
- The key pedagogical pivot is the adversative turn into the central problem: “but how can someone become wealthy in 2022?”
- The speaker then uses an answer-first cue rather than a slow build: the unicorn claim arrives before the evidence.
- Justification begins with a grounding pivot: “because today…” This signals that we are about to get baseline conditions.
- The temporal jump is explicit: “and in 2050…” This moves us from present description to future transformation.
- The conclusion uses a recap cue: “so that’s why…” This tells the writer not to treat the ending as a separate argument, but as the closing synthesis of what came before.

## Recurring Motifs
- Builder’s-eye framing: the question is asked from the standpoint of an engineer deciding where to work and build.
- Thesis first, reasons second: the clip states the commercial conclusion before unpacking the quantitative basis.
- Scale as destiny: the argument repeatedly relies on the size of the world economy rather than on niche examples.
- Constraint as opportunity: fossil dependence plus net-zero transition is framed not as a burden alone, but as a generator of entrepreneurial openings.
- Present-to-future contrast: the rhythm depends on moving from what the system is now to what it must become.

## Pacing Risks
- A draft writer may compress the opening too aggressively and lose the fact that the wealth question is carefully staged through career choice and engineering identity.
- The standalone `Question & Answer` structure can easily disappear if the thesis and its proof are merged into one flat paragraph.
- The present baseline and future constraint should not be collapsed into a single summary sentence; the force of the clip comes from hearing present scale first and then feeling the 2050 shift land on top of it.
- There is a risk of turning the argument into abstract climate commentary. The source is narrower: it is a commercial inference about where unicorn-scale companies are likely to emerge.
- A writer may over-formalize the numbers and lose the spoken cadence. The notes should be deliberate and quantitative, but they should still feel like a guided unfolding of the speaker’s logic rather than a reconstructed textbook proof.
