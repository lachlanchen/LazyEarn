## Derivation Steps
Cash-gap arithmetic:
1. Add the next-day card bill and payroll to form total obligations.
2. Approximate payroll as \(200{,}000\) because the transcript says “200 something thousand.”
3. Subtract the stated account balance \(B \approx 300{,}000\).
4. Record the resulting liquidity gap as roughly \(450{,}000\).
5. Compare the later sales figure \(S_{\text{new}} \approx 800{,}000\) against that gap to show why the speaker says sales solved the immediate crisis.

Revenue-to-profit estimate:
1. Take the revenue claim \(R \approx 240\,\text{M}\).
2. Introduce a margin variable \(m\).
3. Use the standard identity \(\Pi = Rm\).
4. Insert the speaker’s “over 20%” language only as \(m > 0.20\), not as a fixed audited value.
5. Infer \(\Pi > 48\,\text{M}\) only conditionally.

Compounding metaphor:
1. Start from one cent.
2. Double once per day.
3. Represent the day-\(n\) amount as \(a_n = 0.01\cdot 2^{n-1}\).
4. Treat this as a rhetorical model for nonlinear growth, not a literal proof of the business forecast.

Family-capital model:
1. Let each recurring contribution be \(c\).
2. Let the account grow by factor \((1+r)\) each period.
3. Accumulate all contributions into one future-value sum \(FV = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} c(1+r)^k\).
4. Use \(G=\$25\) from the visible screenshot as the concrete one-time contribution example.
5. Use the transcript’s “\$1 a day” as the recurring-input example only in prose or a separate numeric illustration.

Scale-buying mechanism:
1. Introduce purchase volume \(q\).
2. Assume larger \(q\) improves purchasing terms.
3. Encode that relation as falling unit cost \(c_{\text{unit}}(q)\).
4. Keep the relation qualitative because the lecture gives no numeric schedule.

Sales-choice rule:
1. Compare sellers by expected result \(E_i\), not by likability first.
2. Rank the seller with the higher expected result above the other.
3. Use personal liking only if the expected result is effectively tied.
4. Mark this as a formal shorthand for the transcript’s verbal correction.

Time-buyback logic:
1. Introduce recovered hours \(T_{\text{freed}}\).
2. Add those hours to founder strategic time.
3. Interpret the result as more deployable capacity, not literally more hours in a week.
4. Keep “compound their time” as metaphor unless a later chapter needs a more formal productivity model.
