## Definitions And Objects

- \(T_{\mathrm{fed},t}, T_{\mathrm{state},t}, T_{\mathrm{total},t}\): claimed federal, state, and total tax liabilities.
- \(\Pi\): business profit before owner-compensation split.
- \(S\): owner salary under S-corporation treatment.
- \(\tau_{\mathrm{SE}}\): self-employment tax rate discussed in the lecture.
- `STR`: short-term rental; keep this exact abbreviation when echoing the board.
- \(P_{\mathrm{prop}}\): property purchase price.
- \(E_{\mathrm{down}}\): equity / down payment.
- \(L_{\mathrm{loan}}\): borrowed amount used to acquire the property.
- \(W_1\): year-one write-off.
- \(L_{\mathrm{paper}}\): paper loss created by depreciation.
- \(I_{\mathrm{W2}}, I_{\mathrm{1099}}\): income streams the lecture says can be offset.
- \(u_{\mathrm{biz}}\): business-use share of the vehicle.
- \(\mathrm{GVWR}\): gross vehicle weight rating.

- \(\Delta T\): tax savings.
- \(\Delta CF\): added cash flow after reinvestment.
- \(d_{\mathrm{Augusta}}\): number of rental days under the Augusta Rule.
- `Cost segregation study`: the mechanism Dennis says accelerates depreciation and creates a large year-one paper deduction.

## Derivation Steps
S-corporation self-employment-tax logic
1. Start with self-employed business profit \(\Pi\).
2. Lecture claim: as a sole proprietorship or single-member setup, the \(15.3\%\) self-employment tax hits all profits.
3. Cross roughly \(\$50{,}000\)-\(\$60{,}000\) of income.
4. Switch to S-corporation treatment.
5. Pay yourself salary \(S\).
6. Lecture conclusion: the \(15.3\%\) burden is attached to \(S\), not to the full remaining profit pool.

Short-term-rental cost-segregation example
1. Start with \(I_{\mathrm{W2}}=\$100{,}000\).
2. Acquire a property with \(P_{\mathrm{prop}}=\$500{,}000\).
3. Use \(E_{\mathrm{down}}=\$100{,}000\) and \(L_{\mathrm{loan}}=\$400{,}000\).
4. Put the property into short-term-rental use.
5. Require tenant stays of seven days or less.
6. Require management time of \(100\) hours.
7. Perform a cost segregation study.
8. Use the lecture’s rule of thumb \(W_1=20\%\cdot P_{\mathrm{prop}}\).
9. Compute \(W_1=0.20\times \$500{,}000=\$100{,}000\).
10. Match the \(\$100{,}000\) paper loss against the \(\$100{,}000\) income.
11. Lecture conclusion: tax-free example.

Vehicle write-off and reinvestment example
1. Start with \(I=\$200{,}000\) of business income.
2. Put down \(E_{\mathrm{down}}=\$20{,}000\) on a vehicle priced at about \(\$200{,}000\).
3. Use the vehicle deduction to create \(W\approx \$200{,}000\) of write-off.
4. Lecture estimate: someone at that income might otherwise owe about \(\$50{,}000\) in taxes.
5. Compare tax savings to down payment.
6. Compute \(\$50{,}000-\$20{,}000=\$30{,}000\).
7. Reinvest that delta into cash-flow-producing assets or back into the business.

Heavy-vehicle eligibility logic
1. Check that \(\mathrm{GVWR}>6{,}000\) lb.
2. Check that business use exceeds \(50\%\).
3. Route the deduction through Section \(179\) plus Section \(168\mathrm{K}\).
4. Apply the relevant year’s bonus-depreciation rate \(b_t\).

G-Wagon tax-savings reinvestment logic
1. Lecture claim: the vehicle generated about \(\$92{,}000\) of tax savings.
2. Reinvest that \(\$92{,}000\) into a multifamily property in Texas.
3. Resulting cash flow is about \(\$1{,}400\) per month.
4. Lecture conclusion: a depreciating car can still fit a profitable operator strategy if the tax savings are redeployed.

Augusta Rule loop
1. Start with a primary residence.
2. Rent it for at most \(14\) days.
3. Lecture claim: the first \(14\) days of rental income are not taxed to the owner.
4. If the owner has an S corporation, have the S corporation rent the residence.
5. Take the rent deduction at the S-corporation level.
6. Receive the rent tax-free at the individual level.
7. Lecture conclusion: this behaves like a tax-free distribution back to the owner.

Deduction-defense logic in the yacht story
1. IRS disallows a \(\$1{,}000{,}000\) claimed vehicle deduction.
2. Taxpayer supplies supporting records: captain’s log, photos, and P\&L evidence.
3. Dennis invokes the standard “ordinary, necessary, and reasonable.”
4. Audit is resolved in the taxpayer’s favor.
5. Lesson for notes: documentation is part of the operator identity, not an afterthought.

## Notation Choices
- Use \(Y_t\) for annual income and \(T_t\) for taxes.
- Use \(P_{\mathrm{prop}}\) for property purchase price and \(P_{\mathrm{veh}}\) only if a separate vehicle price symbol is needed.
- Use \(E_{\mathrm{down}}\) for down payment / equity and \(L_{\mathrm{loan}}\) for borrowed funds; do not reuse \(D\) for both debt and deduction.
- Use \(W\) or \(W_1\) for write-off amount and \(L_{\mathrm{paper}}\) for paper loss.
- Use \(\tau_{\mathrm{SE}}\) for the \(15.3\%\) self-employment-tax rate.
- Keep `STR` exactly when reproducing the board phrase; use \(d_{\mathrm{stay}}\le 7\) only if the later draft wants a cleaner symbolic version.
- Use \(h_{\mathrm{mgmt}}\) only if an explicit hours variable is needed; otherwise keep the board phrase “manage for \(100\) hours.”
- Use \(u_{\mathrm{biz}}\) for business-use percentage of a vehicle.
- Use \(b_t\) for bonus-depreciation rate by year.
- Use \(\Delta T\) for tax savings and \(\Delta CF\) for extra cash flow.
- Use \(d_{\mathrm{Augusta}}\) for Augusta-rule rental days.
- Use \(\to\) for lecture-style causal or strategic flow and \(\Rightarrow\) for the chapter writer’s cleaned consequence step.
- Use \(\approx\) for spoken rounded values and `<` only where the lecture states a bound, as in \(T_{\mathrm{total},2024}<\$92{,}000\).
- Keep dollar formatting explicit inside math mode: `\$\dots` with commas.
