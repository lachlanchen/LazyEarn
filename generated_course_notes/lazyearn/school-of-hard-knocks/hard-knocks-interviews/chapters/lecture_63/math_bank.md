## Derivation Steps
1. UnitedHealthcare stock multiple.
   1. Take the chiropractor’s stated start price \(P_{\text{UNH},2010}=\$33\).
   2. Take the stated later price \(P_{\text{UNH},\text{now}}=\$213\).
   3. Form the ratio \(213/33\).
   4. Record the implied multiple \(\approx 6.45\).

2. Insurer consolidation summary.
   1. Start from `20-something` HMOs/PPOs.
   2. Normalize cautiously as \(N_{\text{payors,early}}>20\).
   3. Take the final count as \(N_{\text{payors,late}}=5\).
   4. Pair this with the later claim that four of those five are public.

3. Chiropractor doctrine.
   1. Separate `wants or needs` from `problems`.
   2. Map the first to success.
   3. Map the second to wealth.
   4. Keep the arrows directional and speaker-attributed.

4. Real-estate filter from the digital-marketing founder.
   1. Start from `rent where you live` and `rent what you own`.
   2. Extract the stated focus on building equity.
   3. Add the explicit screening conditions: cash flow and good investment quality.
   4. Encode this as a buy-only-if-cash-flows rule, not a universal anti-buy doctrine.

5. Sponsor arithmetic.
   1. Read the search-volume claim as \(V_{\text{search}}>500\,\text{million/month}\).
   2. Read the offer as \(C_{\text{domain,year 1}}=\$0.99\).
   3. Keep this as commercial pitch arithmetic, not as a deeper model.

6. Recycling survival count.
   1. Take the operator’s experience horizon \(T_{\text{recycling}}=30\ \text{years}\).
   2. Take the near-broke count as roughly four.
   3. Compress the lesson as repeated near-failure plus continued improvisation.
   4. Do not force a probability model unless needed later.

7. Positioning lesson from `number two`.
   1. Start from the advisor’s line: why talk to number two when number one exists?
   2. Treat this as a messaging rule, not a market-share theorem.
   3. Encode \(\text{rank}=2\) as weak positioning.
   4. Encode \(\text{rank}=1\) as stronger positioning.

8. Lean-overhead ratio.
   1. Take \(R_{\text{monthly}} \approx \$30{,}000\).
   2. Take \(O_{\text{monthly}} \approx \$3{,}000\text{--}\$4{,}000\).
   3. Compute \(3{,}000/30{,}000=0.10\).
   4. Compute \(4{,}000/30{,}000\approx 0.133\).
   5. Report the implied overhead share as \(10\%\text{--}13\%\).

9. Host-stated paycheck-to-paycheck fraction.
   1. Start from `one in four`.
   2. Convert to \(1/4\).
   3. Optionally convert to \(25\%\).
   4. Keep it host-attributed.

## Notation Choices
- Use \(N\) for counts of people, firms, or events.
- Use \(Y\) for yearly income or money made in a year when the speaker clearly means annual personal or business earnings.
- Use \(R\) for monthly revenue when the final entrepreneur gives a monthly flow example.
- Use \(O\) for monthly overhead or operating expense.
- Use \(P\) for stock price.
- Use \(T\) for time horizons in years.
- Use \(D\) for debt balances.
- Use \(V\) for search volume or aggregate activity volume.
- Use \(C\) for explicit cost in the sponsor segment.
- Use \(p\) for proportions or probabilities only when the transcript gives a clean fraction-like statement.
- Use text operators such as `success`, `wealth`, `cash flow`, `equity building`, `weak positioning`, `stronger positioning` in roman text inside math mode rather than inventing overly formal symbols.
- Use \(\Rightarrow\) for directional doctrines and compressed business logic.
- Use \(\approx\) for almost all spoken quantities unless the transcript gives an exact small integer count.
- Keep all non-frame mathematics labeled as transcript-backed or standard reconstruction; there is no visible-board notation to preserve.
