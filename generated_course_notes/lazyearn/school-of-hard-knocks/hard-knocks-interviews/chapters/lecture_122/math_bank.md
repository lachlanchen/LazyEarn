## Definitions And Objects
- \(W\): the wanted outcome, kept local when possible; “today,” “the next hour,” or the concrete target at hand.
- \(G\): what one will do or give up to get \(W\); the admissible cost, sacrifice, or constraint set.
- \(a_{\text{next}}\): the smallest reasonable next step; the action unit the lecture keeps returning to.
- \(R\): result or feedback obtained after action; not fully formalized in the transcript, but implied by “start getting results.”
- \(D\): desire; the kind of pull associated with becoming better, gaining energy, and meaningful pursuit.
- \(C\): craving; the kind of pull associated with vice, hollow satisfaction, and loss of enthusiasm.
- \(S\): space/silence; the condition needed for ideas to surface.
- \(O\): overstimulation; phone, Netflix, drinking, and similar consumption that crowds out labor and distorts diagnosis.
- \(P\): pain-avoidance motivation; the panic-driven mode that may produce effort early on.
- \(M_D\): desire-led motivation; the motive Terry treats as necessary for sustaining effort after success.
- \(E\): evidence set in the imposter-syndrome discussion; actual attention, outcomes, and response from the world.
- \(N\): next forward action after reading \(E\), replacing rumination about “deserving.”

## Derivation Steps
1. Three-step action loop:
   1. Set \(W\) at the smallest workable scale.
   2. Determine \(G\): what will be done or surrendered.
   3. Choose \(a_{\text{next}}\), the smallest reasonable move.
   4. Execute \(a_{\text{next}}\) with confidence.
   5. Read \(R\) and iterate rather than jumping back to life-scale abstraction.

2. Vice-to-desire correction:
   1. Pure suppression focuses on not doing bad things.
   2. Pure suppression removes enthusiasm and shrinks life.
   3. Therefore one must discriminate \(D\) from \(C\).
   4. Pursue \(D\), not merely abstain from \(C\).

3. Idea-generation sequence:
   1. Activity fills every gap.
   2. Scrolling prevents silence.
   3. Without \(S\), thoughts do not surface clearly.
   4. Therefore create space first; ideas appear after the break, not during nonstop input.

4. “All in” reconstruction:
   1. Reject the fantasy of total identity-merger at once.
   2. Maintain the life structure already in place.
   3. Commit fully for a bounded interval.
   4. Repeat the interval.
   5. The repeated interval builds the skill of full commitment.

5. Obstacle diagnostic inversion:
   1. A person reports loss of passion or stalled execution.
   2. Do not assume the project is the true problem.
   3. Check \(O\): stimulation, indulgence, consumption, avoidance.
   4. Reduce \(O\) before declaring the project dead.

6. Success-maintenance shift:
   1. Early action may be powered by panic or pain avoidance.
   2. Success removes some panic.
   3. Without a new motive, complacency appears.
   4. Replace \(P\) with \(M_D\): movement toward something genuinely wanted.

7. Imposter-syndrome update:
   1. Public response creates a mismatch between self-story and observed reality.
   2. Read \(E\): what attention and outcomes are actually present.
   3. Stop centering the question of deservingness.
   4. Convert the problem into \(N\): what to do moving forward.

8. Final tactical compression:
   1. Stabilize the body and state via breathwork.
   2. Stabilize baseline function via personally workable diet.
   3. Return to \(a_{\text{next}}\): the pathetic next best step.
   4. Small-step execution closes the lecture by restating the opening logic.

## Notation Choices
- Use \(W\) for “want,” not \(D\), because \(D\) is reserved for desire/craving contrast.
- Use \(G\) for “give up / do” rather than \(C\), because \(C\) is reserved for craving.
- Use \(a_{\text{next}}\) for the small action unit; avoid \(a^\*\) or optimization notation unless explicitly labeled as editorial reconstruction.
- Use \(R\) for result/feedback; keep it qualitative rather than numerical.
- Use \(D\) and \(C\) consistently as a binary conceptual contrast: \(D\) for desire, \(C\) for craving.
- Use \(S\) for silence/space and \(O\) for overstimulation only in schematic tables or compact formal summaries.
- Use \(\rightarrow\) for procedural progression, not strict causation.
- Use \(\neq\) only for the lecture’s strong conceptual contrasts.
- Use \(\approx\) only where the transcript implies a loose model rather than a stated identity.
- Avoid heavy optimization symbols such as \(\arg\max\), utility functions, gradients, or control-theoretic notation; the lecture does not justify that level of formalization.
