## Derivation Steps

Startup capital stack

1. Banks reject restaurant lending.
2. Founder raises personal equity through labor income rather than immediate bank financing.
3. Once \(E_0\) is large enough, SBA-backed borrowing becomes available.
4. First-store capital becomes \(K_0 = E_0 + L_0\).
5. In transcript terms, that becomes roughly \$50k + \$50k.

Projection-band bank logic

1. Present a low case where the business still works.
2. Interpret “works” at the low end as approximately break-even.
3. Present a medium case with ordinary profitability.
4. Present a high case with stronger upside and quicker repayment.
5. Bank underwriting is then tied to downside survival plus upside repayment capacity.

Leverage-shock logic

1. Debt-financed growth makes the business feel strong in normal conditions.
2. Katrina removes operating capacity when 21 to 28 restaurants go down.
3. Bank obligations do not disappear when stores stop operating.
4. Effective operating base falls from \(N\) to \(N_{\mathrm{eff}} = N - N_{\mathrm{down}}\).
5. The lesson extracted is to reduce leverage and grow with more equity.

Focus-to-scale logic

1. Choose one offer and make it highly craveable.
2. Avoid branching into many products or menu items.
3. Repeat the same operating unit with high execution quality.
4. Scale then shows up as \(1 \to 10 \to 100 \to 900+\), not as diversification first.

Ownership-to-control logic

1. High founder ownership keeps decision rights concentrated.
2. Concentrated control reduces pressure for short-term cosmetic fixes.
3. Short-term actions like wage cuts or defensive price changes can be deferred or avoided.
4. Editorially, this is a move from maximizing \(\Pi_0\) to maximizing long-run discounted profit.

Thin-margin volume logic

1. Mature restaurant margins are described as \(\mu \in [0.05,0.10]\).
2. Profit therefore satisfies \(\Pi = \mu R\).
3. If \(\mu\) is small, a target profit \(\Pi^{*}\) requires large revenue \(R^{*} = \Pi^{*}/\mu\).
4. Hence the lecture’s repeated emphasis on pushing the top line while staying profit-smart.

Progress-versus-perfection logic

1. Waiting for perfection blocks release.
2. Release can begin at version \(v=1\).
3. Improvement can continue through repeated iteration.
4. The lecture’s example explicitly contrasts version 1 with version 100.
5. Therefore progress is modeled as iterative sequence, not one perfect terminal object.
