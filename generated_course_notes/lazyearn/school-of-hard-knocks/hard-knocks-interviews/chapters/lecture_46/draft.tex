\chapter{I Was Broke, Found God, and Now I Make \$15 Million per Year}

This chapter comes from an interview rather than a blackboard lecture, but the argument has a recognizable mathematical spine. It proceeds by a sequence of compact relations: public attention can become revenue, intention must be separated from distraction, income follows assets, low consumption creates robustness against shocks, each economic era has a dominant wealth lever, and sales is best understood as the revelation of value rather than the application of pressure. We shall keep very close to the spoken order, formalizing only what the lecture itself makes sufficiently clear.

\section{Prologue: A Viral Interview Becomes a Blueprint}

The opening is deliberately dramatic. We are first reminded of the earlier street encounter: the man with the Rolls Royce is a consultant, definitely an entrepreneur, and the largest amount he has made in a single year is \$15 million. He has also been broke, then wealthy, then broke again, and now, in his own words, he has set things up so that this collapse does not happen anymore. The same opening also places faith near the center of the story: he does not merely believe in God, he says he trusts God.

Only after this data of scale is fixed do we move to the present interview. The host reminds us that the earlier clip drew more than 150 million views across social media, names Myron Golden explicitly, and then turns the spectacle into a promise: we are now on the golf course to get a blueprint for becoming a multimillionaire and for selling and marketing like a \$15 million per year entrepreneur.

The bridge is one sentence long and it is one of the governing equations of the chapter:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Attention} \to \mathrm{Revenue}.
\end{equation}
This is not meant as a theorem. It is a transcript-backed maxim. But it does important structural work. It tells us, before any technical discussion begins, that public notice is not merely vanity in this lecture; it is an economic input.

\section{The First Reversal: From Job to Enterprise}

When the conversation settles, the first substantive answer is unexpectedly simple. Golden says he has been a business owner for 47 years, but he did not grow up expecting to build an empire. The first decisive shift came when he was sixteen and saw his father quit his job, refuse to work for anybody else, and then do for himself what he had previously done for other people.

That moment is important because it gives the lecture its primitive model of enterprise. The labor does not change first. Ownership changes first. One continues to solve a problem, but now the surplus is no longer assigned upward to an employer.

We may summarize the move schematically as
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Same\ skill} + \mathrm{Different\ ownership} \to \mathrm{Different\ economics}.
\end{equation}
This formula is our reconstruction, not Golden's notation, but it captures the conceptual turn the interview wants us to notice.

A second numerical boundary condition is then supplied. Asked how much money he made last year, Golden answers: eight figures. The chapter is careful to establish scale before it begins to generalize.

\section{Intention, Distraction, and the Purpose of Money}

The first major conceptual question arrives naturally: what do the most successful people do differently from everybody else? Golden's answer is compact: they hyper-focus on intention and ignore distraction.

We can formalize his distinction with a minimal notation. Let \(N\) denote the favorable movement of the needle toward the desired objective. Then:
\begin{align}
a \in I &\iff \Delta N(a) > 0,\\
a \in D &\iff \Delta N(a) \le 0.
\end{align}
Here \(I\) stands for intention and \(D\) for distraction. This notation is ours, but it is faithful to the spoken criterion: intention moves the needle in our favor, distraction does not.

The lecture immediately widens the frame. Wealthy people, he says, do not regard the primary purpose of money as paying bills. The transcript gives a three-level taxonomy:
\begin{itemize}
\item poor people use money primarily to pay bills;
\item middle-class people use money to maintain good credit so that they can buy things they cannot afford to impress people they do not know or like;
\item rich people use money to turn money into more money.
\end{itemize}
This is not offered as a sociology paper. It is a practical classification of attitudes toward money.

\subsection{Question \& Answer}

The lecture now raises its first local obstacle.

Question: What do wealthy people do differently?

Answer: They do not begin with diversification, branding, or even tactics. They begin by classifying attention correctly, and they refuse to define money as a fluid whose purpose is merely to leave the account in order to satisfy present obligations. Once that shift is made, the next step follows naturally. Money grows, he says, when we perceive what is valuable to other people.

This is why the discussion moves immediately to problem solving. Whether one trades stocks, invests in real estate, or sells insurance, the decisive principle is the same:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Solve\ their\ problem} \to \mathrm{Solve\ your\ money\ problem}.
\end{equation}
The lecture insists on the direction of this arrow. We do not solve our financial problem by staring at it. We solve it by identifying and addressing a problem that belongs to someone else.

\section{Loss, Fragility, and the Discipline of Retained Capital}

The interview then introduces a necessary correction to any triumphalist reading. Golden says he made his first million at 45, after first correcting himself from 43. He also says that he made a million dollars a year for a couple of years in a row and then lost all his money. Why? A series of tragedies, yes, but also a more ordinary mechanism: he upgraded his lifestyle too soon.

This is the point at which the chapter becomes formally interesting. A wealth trajectory is not determined only by income. It is determined by income, consumption, return on assets, and shocks. A cautious reconstruction is
\begin{equation}
W_{t+1} = W_t + r_t K_t + (Y_t - C_t) - S_t.
\end{equation}
Here \(W_t\) is wealth at time \(t\), \(K_t\) is the asset base, \(r_t\) its rate of return, \(Y_t\) income or revenue, \(C_t\) consumption, and \(S_t\) the shock term. This equation does not appear on screen; it is the smallest model that faithfully captures the logic of the conversation.

Golden's practical rule is then sharp: if one can live on roughly \(10\%\) of revenue or income, then when the bottom falls out one can remain okay for a couple of years. In notation,
\begin{equation}
\frac{C}{Y} \le 0.1.
\end{equation}
Again, this is not a theorem. It is a heuristic of resilience.

\paragraph{Worked example.}
Suppose monthly income is
\[
Y = 100{,}000,
\]
monthly living cost is
\[
C = 10{,}000,
\]
and current reserve is
\[
W_0 = 240{,}000.
\]
Then:
\begin{enumerate}
\item the retained amount per month is
\begin{equation}
Y - C = 90{,}000;
\end{equation}
\item the spending ratio is
\begin{equation}
\frac{C}{Y} = \frac{10{,}000}{100{,}000} = 0.1;
\end{equation}
\item if revenue temporarily falls to zero, the number of survivable months is
\begin{equation}
R = \frac{W_0}{C} = \frac{240{,}000}{10{,}000} = 24.
\end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
Thus \(R=24\) months reproduces the lecture's intuition that a modest burn rate can buy two years of survival even under harsh conditions.

The associated slogan, ``stay small enough, long enough, and you'll be big enough soon enough,'' is therefore not merely moral advice. It is a control strategy for keeping \(C\) small while \(W\) and \(K\) are still being built.

\section{Economic Eras and the Error of Deadlines}

The next move widens the temporal scale. Golden argues that every time wealth changes sectors or categories, a new economic era is introduced. The transcript gives a historical chain:
\begin{align}
L(E_{\mathrm{agri}}) &= \mathrm{Land},\\
L(E_{\mathrm{ind}}) &= \mathrm{Machines},\\
L(E_{\mathrm{dist}}) &= \mathrm{Outlets},\\
L(E_{\mathrm{now}}) &= \mathrm{AI}.
\end{align}
This is the lecture's own periodization. We preserve it as source claim, not as independently established economic history.

The conclusion drawn from it is immediate: if one were entering an industry right now, it should be something to do with AI, because current wealth formation is concentrated around the dominant lever of the present era.

\subsection{Question \& Answer}

Question: If the lecture has already identified the current lever of wealth, why not simply set the target ``\$1 million in 90 days'' and pursue it with maximum force?

Answer: Because, Golden says, the problem is not ambition but control. He rejects goals precisely insofar as they add artificial deadlines to objectives. His definition is explicit:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Goal} = \mathrm{Objective} + \mathrm{Deadline}.
\end{equation}
He does not claim control over the times and seasons, and he uses the farming analogy to make the point vivid: no farmer can produce 7 million ears of corn by next Wednesday just by willing it.

The cleaned-up lesson is therefore this. We may control inputs. We may choose outcomes. But we do not control the time at which the harvest arrives. In that sense, the lecture is not anti-planning; it is anti-false-control. The point of replacing the goal with an objective is to prevent the person from confusing delayed harvest with failure.

\section{Family Trajectory, Assets, and the Work of Mentorship}

The interview then turns from markets to family history. The host asks how Golden changed his family's trajectory and how he escaped the cycle of poverty. Golden answers by first correcting the logic of the familiar slogan. The rich get richer and the poor get poorer, he says, but that is not a causal chain. It is correlation, not causation.

\subsection{Question \& Answer}

Question: How did he change his family's trajectory, and why is ``rich get richer'' not already an explanation?

Answer: Because a description of simultaneous motion is not yet a mechanism. Golden's answer is that he hated being poor, learned the things rich people did, and then began to do those things instead of the things poor people did. The first formal principle he gives is
\begin{equation}
Y = f(K),
\end{equation}
or in the lecture's own words, income follows assets. The function \(f\) is not specified by the transcript, and we should not pretend that it is. The point is structural. One stops staring at wages and starts building an asset column.

This is why the lecture distinguishes between buying assets and building assets. A book, he says, is an asset because it is written once and then continues to pay monthly. The chapter should preserve that example because it shows the precise kind of object he has in mind: something whose output persists after the initial labor is complete.

The next mechanism is mentorship. Golden says he has spent roughly \$1.7 million on books, courses, coaching, and programs, and the main gain is not just information but compression. Another person's lifetime of figuring things out is bought and transferred in abbreviated form. He therefore insists that the deepest barrier is often not ignorance of a known thing but ignorance of what one does not yet know one does not know.

This is the natural place for the Robert G. Allen story. Golden reports telling Allen that he had gone from earning \$6.25 an hour as a trash man to earning \$30,000 a month. Allen's reply is decisive: you are doing well, but you are only on the path to really big money. The lecture uses this as a re-scaling moment. A number that once looked immense is suddenly reclassified as small relative to a higher regime.

So the movement here is:
\begin{enumerate}
\item from wage-thinking to asset-thinking;
\item from asset-thinking to mentorship;
\item from mentorship to larger perception;
\item from larger perception to awareness of abundance.
\end{enumerate}
The transcript at this point is slightly garbled, but the intended claim is plain enough: the problem is not simply lack of access to abundance; it is lack of awareness of abundance already present.

\section{Return to Market: Access, Value, Trust, Faith}

At this point the video pauses for a channel interlude. For note-writing purposes we suppress that interruption and resume when the interview resumes.

The return begins not with metaphysics but with access. How does someone with little credibility get into the room? Golden's answer is blunt: one pays to be in the room. One buys the way in and, if one learns properly, gets paid on the way out. He then narrates a concrete case: he joined a mastermind as the poorest person in it, discovered that the people in that room valued his ideas far more than he had expected, and realized that he had been selling to the wrong people all along.

The lecture's market-selection principle is therefore straightforward: if one wants to get rich, one should sell to rich people. Why? Not because they are morally better, but because they have the capacity to buy and because the energy required to solve a large problem is not necessarily greater than the energy required to solve a small one.

\subsection{Question \& Answer}

Question: How do we get money from rich people without reducing sales to pressure, manipulation, or extraction?

Answer: By refusing that description of sales in the first place. Golden explicitly says that he does not see selling as getting money from people. He sees it as delivering value to them. Payment comes because a real problem has been solved.

A cautious reconstruction is
\begin{equation}
P_{\mathrm{payment}} = g(V,T,M_b),
\end{equation}
where \(V\) is revealed value, \(T\) is trust or relationship capital, and \(M_b\) is the buyer's capacity to pay. The transcript does not provide this notation, but it closely follows the spoken mechanism: the right buyer, trusting the right person, having seen enough value, gladly exchanges money for the offer.

This is why the lecture opposes price-cutting as the first reflex. If the answer to ``no'' is immediately to lower price, then value has not yet been sufficiently revealed. The correction is not always to cheapen the offer; often it is to increase value and improve fit.

The lecture also insists on temporal structure. One does not walk up to a stranger and ask for marriage; one builds rapport and trust over time. So too in commerce. Public value first buys attention. Attention matures into relationship. Relationship matures into trust. Trust eventually matures into purchase or investment.

This same logic explains the claim that the most money he ever made in a single day was about \$5.5 million. The act of sale may be concentrated in one day; the trust that makes the day possible is not.

Only after all of this does the lecture return fully to the earlier theological thread. Why trust God? Because, Golden says, God has never lied to him, and what God says in the Bible is not only true but truth. The financial lesson is then folded into a moral one. The blessing of the Lord maketh rich and addeth no sorrow with it; and the one who would be greatest must be servant of all.

So the final commercial principle is no longer merely economic. Greatness, in the lecture's closing vocabulary, grows with scale of service. This is the same movement we have already seen several times in secular language: solve other people's problems, create value for them, and abundance becomes accessible. The difference now is that the lecture places that mechanism under a theological sky and ends with gratitude rather than conquest.

\section{Summary}

The chapter has the form of a chain of reductions. First, spectacle is reduced to data: \$15 million in a year, eight figures recently, poverty, collapse, recovery, faith. Then wealth is reduced to attention properly classified, money properly understood, and problems properly located. Fragility is reduced to a bad ratio of consumption to income. Economic timing is reduced to the difference between leverage and deadline. Sales is reduced to value revelation in the presence of trust. Poverty escape is reduced to asset accumulation, mentorship, and enlarged perception. Finally, business itself is reduced to service.

If we compress the lecture to its most durable relations, we obtain the following list:
\begin{align}
\mathrm{Attention} &\to \mathrm{Revenue},\\
\mathrm{Intention} &\neq \mathrm{Distraction},\\
\mathrm{Income} &\mathrm{\ follows\ assets},\\
\frac{C}{Y} &\le 0.1 \ \mathrm{supports\ robustness},\\
L(E_{\mathrm{now}}) &= \mathrm{AI},\\
\mathrm{Value} + \mathrm{Trust} &\to \mathrm{Payment},\\
\mathrm{Service} &\to \mathrm{Greatness}.
\end{align}
These are not axioms of economics. They are the lecture's own compact working relations. Our task in the later draft will be to refine their wording without losing the order, motivation, and pressure by which they were first introduced.
