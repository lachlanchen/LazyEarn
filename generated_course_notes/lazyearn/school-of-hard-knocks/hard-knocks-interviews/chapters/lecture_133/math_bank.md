## Derivation Steps
1. Transaction and timescale facts
   1. Glenn states the company went public in 1999.
   2. He states the later merger valued the whole company at about \$1.03 billion.
   3. Later he states that even successful companies usually take at least \(3\text{--}5\) years.
   4. These become the chapter’s two main hard numeric anchors.

2. Low-capital startup heuristic
   1. The interviewer asks what to do with \( \$100{,}000 \).
   2. Glenn says that level of capital is insufficient for immediate capital-heavy high-tech buildout.
   3. He gives two feasible paths: start in services to generate income, or, if technically capable, take time off and build the product yourself.
   4. The implied constraint is that founder labor substitutes for missing outside capital.

3. Web3 causal chain
   1. Glenn begins with blockchains having a real purpose.
   2. He shifts to proof-of-stake versus proof-of-work only as a brief example of changing implementation.
   3. He then identifies the underlying social pressure: frustration with advertising-based platforms and rising privacy concern.
   4. He concludes that decentralized applications and decentralized social interaction are attractive because they do not rely as directly on monetizing personal information.
   5. The chapter should preserve the causal chain, not inflate it into technical blockchain theory.

4. Cybersecurity demand chain
   1. The interviewer raises recent hacks as motivating examples.
   2. Glenn answers that security is “massive” and frames it as structural rather than temporary.
   3. He argues that security is often absent at design time, using the “buildings without locks” analogy.
   4. He adds the historical shift from paper files to computers, then to cloud-based interconnection.
   5. More digital interdependence plus poor security design implies larger attack surface and stronger long-run demand for security work.

5. Work-hours and creativity
   1. Glenn describes remote work producing isolated stretches of \(8\), \(10\), or \(12\) hours.
   2. He says too many such days in a row deplete creativity.
   3. He therefore breaks up work with physical movement and changes of workspace.
   4. The usable note version is a qualitative relation between continuous work intensity and creative depletion.

6. Recession deployment logic
   1. Glenn treats recession as painful but temporary.
   2. He says labor and certain services become cheaper.
   3. He therefore treats downturns as opportunities to start companies, hire people, or undertake delayed spending if capital is available.
   4. Savings matter because they provide optionality while conditions reset.

7. Domain-depth investing / career rule
   1. The interviewer asks how to know when to buy into an industry.
   2. Glenn rejects scattered chasing of whatever is newly popular.
   3. He recommends staying with what one knows or learning one field deeply.
   4. The implied mechanism is informational advantage through depth rather than breadth.
