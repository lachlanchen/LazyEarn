## Derivation Steps

Catalog ownership to durable income

1. Each owned master contributes an income stream \(I_i\).
2. One-song dependence makes wealth hinge on a single outlier.
3. A large owned catalog replaces one-hit dependence with a sum over many tracks.
4. Therefore the relevant object is \(I_{\mathrm{catalog}}=\sum I_i\), not one isolated hit.

Advance to recoupment to payment timing

1. The label advances \(A=\$2\,\mathrm{M}\).
2. Revenue first pays back that advance.
3. If \(R_{\mathrm{music}}<A\), the artist remains unrecouped.
4. If unrecouped, later label checks do not arrive.
5. If \(R_{\mathrm{music}}\ge A\), later revenue is split and the artist begins receiving semiannual checks in Russ’s example.

“There is money in music” from ownership structure

1. Labels make billions while owning music rights.
2. Touring is not the source of those label profits.
3. Therefore the claim “there is no money in music” cannot be right as stated.
4. The corrected claim is that money sits in music ownership, not merely in music labor.

Song-level ROI anecdote

1. Russ reports a distribution/upload cost near \$9.99.
2. He reports doing the beat and execution himself, with no marketing spend in the anecdote.
3. He reports about \$2 million earned on that master.
4. The crude ratio \(\frac{R_{\mathrm{song}}}{C_{\mathrm{song}}}\) is therefore enormous.
5. The lecture uses this as evidence for music as the highest-ROI reinvestment vehicle in his own business.

99-cent pricing under zero debt

1. Unit profit is \(\Pi=(p-c)q\).
2. Holding \(p=\$0.99\) fixed requires controlling \(c\).
3. Ownership of buildings, factories, and equipment lowers recurring cost pressure.
4. Zero debt sets \(c_{\mathrm{interest}}=0\).
5. Lower cost pressure allows the company to survive at a low price point.
6. Volume \(q\) can then compensate for tighter unit margin.

Reach over margin

1. Lower price compresses per-unit margin \(p-c\).
2. Lower price can raise sales volume \(q\).
3. If the increase in \(q\) is large enough, total profit can remain acceptable.
4. This is the lecture’s stated logic behind choosing reach with disciplined cost control.

Packaging as the acquisition surface

1. Beverage incumbents can dominate paid advertising.
2. Shelf choice happens at the cooler, not in an abstract spreadsheet.
3. Packaging therefore acts as the primary customer-acquisition interface.
4. The product must attract attention before any large media budget exists.

Rebuild-from-zero operator

1. Study continuously.
2. Identify a market void.
3. Move fast once the void is found.
4. Add the right people because the build cannot be done alone.
5. If the attempt fails, restart immediately.

Failure as input to later success

1. Early failure does not end the sequence.
2. Each loss supplies information for the next move.
3. A long run of losses can still terminate in one large success.
4. The lecture treats fear of failure as the main blocker to iteration.

One-day-at-a-time recovery

1. Begin with the smallest executable step.
2. Repeat with another concrete step on the same day.
3. Let repetition make the first steps automatic.
4. Larger forward motion appears only after the small steps are stabilized.
5. The process fails only when the next step is not taken.

- Use \(A\) only for advance; do not reuse it for assets or advertising.
- Use “recouped” and “unrecouped” as state labels, not more legalistic contract terms.

- Use \(\approx\) for rounded speaker-reported figures, \(>\) only where the transcript explicitly says “over,” and \(\sim\) for rough scale ratios.
