## Derivation Steps
1. Pivot mechanism
   1. Start with a LAN-security product that logs and analyzes internal network traffic.
   2. Observe that the internet makes the whole world networked, so the addressable domain changes.
   3. Keep the core analytics technology \(A\) fixed.
   4. Replace \(T_{\mathrm{LAN}}\) with \(T_{\mathrm{web}}\).
   5. Replace \(\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{sec}}\) with \(\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{mkt}}\).
   6. Validate the new mapping with actual customers and early revenue.

2. Sports Illustrated counting problem
   1. Raw traffic totals alone do not identify audience structure.
   2. One possibility is \(U=100{,}000\) and \(f=1\).
   3. Another is \(U=10{,}000\) and \(f=10\).
   4. Both produce the same total \(V\), but they imply different marketing realities.
   5. Therefore the valuable object is not just total hits but inferred audience composition and interest.

3. Funding logic
   1. An idea by itself is weak evidence.
   2. A demo or sample product is stronger evidence.
   3. Early adoption is stronger still.
   4. Investor confidence rises when actual usage is observable.
   5. Therefore the path to funding runs through proof, not just persuasion.

4. Dog-food test
   1. Advertising polish does not rescue a product users do not want.
   2. The relevant binary is whether people actually use the thing.
   3. If users adopt it early, the company can infer product pull.
   4. That product pull becomes the basis for growth and financing.

5. VC pass to founder ownership
   1. The company is profitable and growing.
   2. The founders seek VC to accelerate expansion.
   3. VC does not invest.
   4. The company is forced into frugality rather than subsidized spending.
   5. Lower dilution leaves founders with roughly \(95\%\) ownership at IPO.

6. Scaling-cost logic
   1. Growth increases the business scale \(s\).
   2. Expense load \(E(s)\) rises with scale.
   3. A bad shock at larger \(s\) hits a larger cost base.
   4. Therefore expense control is part of scaling, not separate from it.

7. Management overload model
   1. An employee can succeed at a smaller managerial load \(n\).
   2. Company growth raises \(n\) over time.
   3. Once \(n\) crosses a threshold \(n^\*\), performance can collapse.
   4. The fix is to stop adding load, reassign responsibilities, or hire someone already above that scale.

8. Crossing-the-chasm logic
   1. A product can secure early adopters first.
   2. Early-adopter success does not automatically imply mainstream adoption.
   3. There is a gap between those markets.
   4. Marketing and positioning must bridge that gap for broader scale.
