## Definitions And Objects
- \(W\): wealth or accumulated economic position
- \(W_{\text{labor}}\): wealth built primarily from wages/salary/cash compensation
- \(W_{\text{ownership}}\): wealth built from owning equity, a company, properties, or other scalable claims
- \(y\): labor income flow
- \(s\): savings rate
- \(c\): consumption outflow
- \(S(t)\): savings stock or financial runway
- \(O\): optionality; the feasible set of career/business moves available to a person
- \(T_{\text{investment horizon}}\): time one can leave capital invested without needing liquidity
- \(D\): local demographics
- \(F\): local foot traffic / demand flow
- \(P\): product format or business model format
- \(M\): market fit between business concept and location
- \(R\): reputation capital
- \(p_{\text{successful interview}}\): fraction of door knocks producing a usable interview
- \(X_{\text{company sale}}\): one-time exit proceeds from selling a company
- \(C_{\text{cash flow}}\): annual operating cash flow
- \(\Rightarrow\): use only for causal or decision-chain summaries, not as a strict theorem arrow

## Derivation Steps
1. Ownership and optionality chain
   1. Begin with labor income \(y\).
   2. Save a fraction \(s\) of that income to build \(S(t)\).
   3. Larger \(S(t)\) increases runway and therefore optionality \(O\).
   4. Higher \(O\) makes it feasible to leave pure employment and attempt ownership.
   5. Ownership creates exposure to \(W_{\text{ownership}}\), which is the lecture’s preferred long-run upside.

2. Long-horizon investing constraint
   1. Investment timing is said to be difficult.
   2. The interviewee therefore shifts the rule from timing to horizon.
   3. Capital should be money “you won’t need for maybe ten years.”
   4. So the relevant condition is not short-term forecast accuracy but \(T_{\text{investment horizon}}\) being long enough.

3. Market-fit failure test
   1. Start with a proposed business format \(P\) such as pizza by the slice.
   2. Check local demographics \(D\).
   3. Check foot traffic \(F\).
   4. If \(D\) and \(F\) do not support \(P\), then market fit \(M\) is weak.
   5. Weak \(M\) explains likely business failure without needing a more exotic cause.

4. Persistence loop
   1. Attempt action.
   2. Receive rejection or poor response.
   3. Reevaluate rather than stop.
   4. Continue with the next attempt.
   5. This same loop appears both in filming the interviews and in the sales advice.

5. Partner-selection logic
   1. Observe candidate history.
   2. Do not treat failure as automatic disqualification.
   3. Ask whether the person recovered and how they behaved after failure.
   4. Use that recovery signal as evidence about character and operating resilience.

6. Reputation-capital rule in real estate
   1. Move quickly and with conviction.
   2. But keep a good name and treat investors properly.
   3. Therefore speed is constrained by reputation maintenance rather than replacing it.
   4. In shorthand: high-conviction action must preserve \(R\).
