## Definitions And Objects

- \(V_{\text{exit}}\): realized cash value at business sale.
- \(V_{\text{early}}\): earlier rejected or unrealized offer value.
- \(V_{\text{late}}\): later realized sale value.
- \(C_0\): initial capital / initial loan.
- \(M_{\text{stated}}\): buyer’s stated preference or declared reason.
- \(M_{\text{true}}\): actual motivation driving the deal.
- \(C_{\text{owner,net}}\): effective net housing cost to the owner-occupant.
- \(C_{\text{mortgage}}\): mortgage burden.
- \(C_{\text{tax}}\): property-tax burden.
- \(C_{\text{upkeep}}\): maintenance / operating burden.
- \(R_{\text{second unit}}\): rent collected from the tenant-occupied unit.
- \(R_{\text{scheduled}}\): paper rent roll / nominal rent expected.
- \(R_{\text{collected}}\): real rent actually received.
- \(\rho_{\text{collection}}\): rent-collection rate.
- \(N_{\text{units}}\): number of motel units.
- \(\Pi_{\text{commission}}\): disputed commission value.
- \(Q_{\text{investment}}\): Barbara’s effective investment-quality judgment.
- \(\Delta t_{\text{lost}}\): years of missed market participation.
- \(\text{interest}, \text{hesitation}, \text{urgency}, \text{commitment}\): negotiation states rather than numeric variables.

## Derivation Steps

1. Timing comparison
   1. The transcript states an earlier near-sale at \(\$2\) million.
   2. It later states an actual sale at \(\$66\) million.
   3. Use these as paired values for a timing comparison.
   4. Compute only the scale ratio \(66/2=33\).
   5. Do not treat that as a return model or valuation theorem.

2. Negotiation mechanism
   1. Begin from a prospect who is on the fence or leaning no.
   2. Barbara says she sometimes takes the deal away.
   3. She also introduces competing offers / scarcity.
   4. Scarcity raises urgency.
   5. Urgency increases the chance of commitment.

3. Listening-to-motivation mechanism
   1. Buyer speech gives surface preference.
   2. Barbara says buyers often do not tell the full truth.
   3. Therefore surface speech is not the true motive.
   4. Listening is used to infer the hidden motivation.
   5. Once motivation is known, one can structure the deal correctly.

4. Two-family-house cash-flow logic
   1. Buy a two-family house.
   2. Live in one unit.
   3. Rent the other unit.
   4. Use that rent to offset mortgage burden.
   5. Net owner housing cost becomes total carrying cost minus rental income.

5. Paper-rent-roll correction
   1. The motel was presented as having a strong rent roll.
   2. Barbara discovered tenants had not paid in two years.
   3. Therefore paper scheduled rent was not actual collected rent.
   4. Introduce a collection-rate factor.
   5. Real cash flow must be based on collected rent, not the brochure.

6. Entry-delay cost
   1. Barbara says she tried to buy early and backed out.
   2. She then says it took about eight years to catch up.
   3. Interpret this as the cost of delayed market participation.
   4. Use it to support the early-entry rule.
   5. Do not infer an exact appreciation rate.

7. Founder-screening heuristic
   1. Barbara rejects diversification as a simple rule for her own practice.
   2. She says she judges the entrepreneur.
   3. She names resilience as the key separator.
   4. She later adds sales ability and business judgment as necessary traits.
   5. Model investment judgment as founder-weighted rather than sector-weighted.

8. Doubt-to-motivation logic
   1. Rejection or doubt appears.
   2. Barbara treats it as fuel rather than endpoint.
   3. She responds with assertion or renewed effort.
   4. The response creates a new opportunity.
   5. Hence doubt is modeled as an input to action, not merely a negative state.

9. Apples bootstrap logic
   1. The host asks what to do from zero with one year to make a million.
   2. Barbara rejects the million-in-a-year framing.
   3. She instead starts with a small selling activity.
   4. She attaches a first-month revenue number of about \(\$50{,}000\).
   5. The implied rule is: start with immediate cash generation and build.

## Notation Choices

- Use \(V\) for valuation / sale values and reserve subscripts `early`, `late`, `exit` for timing distinctions.
- Use \(C_0\) for initial capital or startup loan.
- Use \(C_{\text{owner,net}}\) rather than \(P\) for profit in the housing example, because the transcript’s point is owner cost offset, not a full investment P\&L.
- Use \(R\) for rent or revenue-type inflows; distinguish \(R_{\text{scheduled}}\) from \(R_{\text{collected}}\).
- Use \(\rho_{\text{collection}}\) for collection rate rather than an English word in equations.
- Use \(M_{\text{stated}}\) and \(M_{\text{true}}\) for buyer motivation rather than overloaded \(x\) or \(y\).
- Use \(\Pi_{\text{commission}}\) for the commission dispute, keeping \(\Pi\) as a payoff-like quantity only where the transcript names a specific money outcome.
- Treat negotiation stages as labeled states in prose or diagrams, not as numeric variables unless needed.
- Keep dollar units explicit in the prose around equations; do not overload equations with unit clutter unless necessary.
- Avoid finance-heavy notation such as DCF, IRR, or cap-rate symbols; the lecture does not support them directly.
- Keep all reconstructed equations clearly marked as business heuristics, not canonical laws.
