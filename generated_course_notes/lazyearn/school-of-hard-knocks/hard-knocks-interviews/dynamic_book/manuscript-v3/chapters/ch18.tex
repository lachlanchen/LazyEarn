\chapter{Compounding Is Slow; Risk Is Fast}
\chaptermark{Compounding Is Slow; Risk Is Fast}

A retired dentist in Philadelphia describes investing as a long wait followed
by an apparent acceleration. Saving alone, he says, is not enough; money placed
regularly in broad public-market funds seems to do very little at first and
then begins to grow geometrically. His point is easy to admire because the
curve rises. The more important part of the story is the quiet left-hand side
of the curve, when progress is small, uncertain, and easy to interrupt.

Compounding requires a viable asset, continued ownership, and time. A reader
who must sell during a decline, who borrows against a volatile position, who
pays away too much of each gain, or who concentrates everything in one failure
does not receive the return suggested by an uninterrupted illustration. Time
helps only a position whose owner can survive the path.

That is why risk moves faster than compounding. A portfolio may need decades
to earn its expected result and one afternoon to lose the liquidity that made
waiting possible. The task is not to find an asset that never falls. It is to
build a life and a portfolio that do not require prediction, luck, or permanent
good conditions merely to remain standing.

\section{Buy the right to remain invested}

When Ryan Serhant arrived in New York to work in theater, he calculated that he
needed about \$2,000 a month to stay in the city. He entered real estate on the
day Lehman Brothers filed for bankruptcy. The later scale of his business can
obscure the practical intelligence of that first calculation. Before a long
horizon could matter, he needed a monthly floor.

The floor differs by household. It includes food, housing, health, transport,
care obligations, tax, insurance, and every payment that cannot be postponed
without harm. It also includes the irregular costs that a monthly average
hides. Money needed on a known date does not belong in a position whose value
may be depressed on that date.

This makes liquidity part of return rather than an enemy of it. Cash may lose
purchasing power and may earn less than a risky asset. It can still buy the
time needed to avoid selling that asset under pressure. The relevant reserve
is not a ceremonial percentage. It is the cash required by the household's
obligations, income volatility, dependants, health, and recovery time.

Von Miller applies this logic to an unusually short and uncertain earning
career. Speaking while he was a professional football player, he repeatedly
urges athletes to preserve their earnings, wait until they understand an
opportunity, and protect their health and longevity. The numbers attached to a
star athlete are exceptional; the mechanism is not. When income depends on a
body, a cycle, one employer, or a brief public moment, today's high flow should
not finance commitments that require it forever.

A physician interviewed in Houston gives the same warning from a less
cinematic setting: do not overextend; keep fixed obligations low enough to
survive a bad year. Another physician remembers contributing through an
employer retirement plan as soon as she began work. The specific account rules
depend on plan and jurisdiction. The durable sequence is broader: create the
margin, preserve near-term liquidity, make regular long-horizon contributions,
and refuse to let a strong year make the weak year unaffordable.

\section{Concentration creates fortunes and fragility}

Most founders begin concentrated. Their skill, reputation, labor, private
equity, and future income may all depend on the same company and industry.
That concentration can be rational during creation: attention is scarce, the
founder may possess unusual knowledge, and a small position cannot change the
life even if it succeeds. It is still risk, not proof of conviction.

Reid Hoffman describes taking a risk that could fail while preserving the
ability to play again. As an investor, he says a venture capitalist may review
hundreds of opportunities in a year and choose almost none. Another venture
speaker gives a portfolio heuristic in which most of thirty companies go to
zero and a few winners return many times the original investment. These are
accounts from highly selected winners, not expected results for a reader.
They do reveal the structure of early-stage investing: outcomes may be
power-law distributed, a total loss is ordinary, and one isolated deal lacks
the portfolio logic used by a fund.

Diversification reduces dependence on any one company, borrower, tenant,
sector, geography, or story. It does not abolish market declines, inflation,
illiquidity, fraud, institutional failure, or a household's need for cash. Nor
does owning many positions guarantee meaningful diversification if all respond
to the same shock.

An Austin cybersecurity executive credits long-held Intel and Microsoft shares
with helping her financially, then advises saving, living within one's means,
and holding a diverse portfolio. The first statement is a history of winners
known after the fact. It contains the survivors but not the comparable
companies that disappeared, stagnated, or were sold. The second statement is a
policy for not requiring the next stock to repeat the first two. A useful plan
learns from both without confusing testimony with selection.

The decision also changes across stages. During accumulation, a person may
accept a bounded concentration in a business or craft that she understands and
can influence. As the claim grows, or as work, home, and investment become
correlated, preservation becomes more important. Diversification can then
protect a life already built. It may reduce the highest possible outcome. That
is often the price of making one outcome unnecessary.

\section{Small drags also compound}

The clean compounding formula assumes that every period's result remains in
the account:

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
W_T={}&W_0\prod_{t=1}^{T}(1+r_t-d_t)\\
&+\sum_{k=1}^{T}C_k\prod_{t=k+1}^{T}(1+r_t-d_t),
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

where \(r_t\) is the asset return, \(d_t\) is the total proportional drag, and
\(C_k\) is a contribution made during the path. The notation is a planning aid,
not a forecast. Returns vary; some costs are fixed rather than proportional;
tax depends on instrument, account, transaction, owner, and law.

A Dubai interview offers an exaggerated but useful arithmetic exercise. Start
with one dollar and double it twenty times. With no drag, the result is

\begin{equation}
2^{20}=1{,}048{,}576.
\end{equation}

Now suppose, artificially, that one quarter of each period's gain disappears
before the next doubling. Each round multiplies wealth by (1.75), producing

\begin{equation}
1.75^{20}\approx72{,}571.
\end{equation}

The result is about 93.1 percent below the undisturbed example, not the 96
percent stated in the conversation. This is not a model of an actual tax bill
or bank fee. It is an illustration of path dependence. A recurring drag is
paid not only once; it also removes the future return that the removed amount
could have earned.

Fees, tax, turnover, bid--ask spread, borrowing cost, dilution, vacancy,
maintenance, and idle cash are not interchangeable. Record each where it
actually enters. A low headline fee may sit beside expensive advice or trading.
A tax deferral can preserve capital for reinvestment without making the
underlying purchase sound. A dividend is a distribution from company value,
not an additional return independent of price. A property can produce rent and
still consume cash through vacancy, repair, management, insurance, tax, and
debt service. Accurate friction is more useful than optimistic precision.

\section{A river still needs a watershed}

Eric Spofford contrasts a pile of earned money with a river of recurring cash.
He describes resisting lifestyle growth, putting operating income into
cash-flowing property, and allowing the property income to support later
spending. Anthony Powell offers a similar ladder: earn, contribute regularly
to public-market assets, and consider multifamily property only after capital
has accumulated. A former model who became a Los Angeles home builder reports
reinvesting profits into progressively larger projects and using a tax-deferred
property exchange. These stories share a sound question: can today's labor
create an asset that continues to produce value tomorrow?

Their asset answers are personal. A river can dry. Tenants leave, customers
churn, dividends are cut, borrowers default, regulation changes, repairs arrive,
and leverage converts a temporary decline into a forced sale. A private
business can produce recurring cash while remaining dependent on one founder,
platform, supplier, or fashion. The test is the system beneath the flow.

Todd Nepola distinguishes the home one inhabits from property bought for
income, and favors long holding, self-management, refinancing, and repeated
acquisition. A home does not have to maximize cash flow to be valuable. It may
provide shelter, stability, control, community, and a form of committed saving.
An income property does not become free because other occupants pay scheduled
rent. Collection, operating expense, capital work, management labor, financing,
and legal duties remain. Refinancing releases borrowed cash and enlarges the
fixed claim against the asset. Chapter 17's survival test still applies.

The Los Angeles builder's tax deferral belongs in the same category. Deferring
a liability may keep more capital working for a period. It cannot improve poor
land, weak demand, an excessive price, or dangerous leverage. Current rules are
jurisdiction- and fact-specific and require qualified advice. Tax should be
modeled as one consequence of an investment, not used as its reason.

\section{Do not borrow against the patience you need}

After selling her company, Anne Mahlum describes favoring public equities and
holding many positions rather than selling frequently. She also describes a
securities-backed credit line and borrowing when she expects another investment
to earn more than the loan costs. One stated rate is not intelligible enough to
use, so no precise borrowing term can be trusted. More important, the apparent
spread between borrowing cost and expected return is not guaranteed.

A loan secured by volatile securities can demand collateral precisely when the
portfolio has fallen. Interest can rise, the new investment can be illiquid,
and the lender's remedies can force the sale of the asset intended to compound.
This joins several risks: market decline, leverage, rate movement, collateral
calls, and the uncertain return of the new position. It may be manageable for
a highly liquid household with expert advice and abundant unpledged reserves.
It is not a general shortcut around realizing gains.

The rule is simple enough to write and hard enough to honor:

\begin{center}
\emph{Do not fund a long horizon with a claim that can end it early.}
\end{center}

Debt maturity, collateral rights, withdrawal restrictions, margin rules, and
known spending dates must all fit the investment horizon. Expected return is
not cash available for a required payment.

\section{Replace borrowed rules with a portfolio policy}

The interviews contain many memorable allocations. One Miami operator was
told to place ten percent of income in an index fund. Another speaker saved
seventy percent and lived on thirty. A Calabasas entrepreneur divided each
check into thirds, including a reserve account he treated as untouchable. A
technology salesperson kept only a small exposure to cryptocurrency and used
mutual funds for much of the rest. A retired consultant held energy shares; a
retired dentist described equities, bonds, annuity contracts, and pension
income. Other speakers preferred cash, property, dividends, or venture stakes.

These allocations conflict because the lives behind them differ. Income,
country, age, dependants, pension rights, debt, health, tax, employment,
knowledge, temperament, and the price of a mistake differ too. Even a sensible
rule can become dangerous when copied without its conditions.

Another Miami speaker puts the habit before the asset: pay yourself first, then
look for evidence that the underlying activity has real traction. Both parts
need qualification. A contribution made automatically is easier to sustain, but
it must not strand money needed for food, care, tax, or high-cost debt. Visible
traction may indicate durable demand, paid promotion, a crowded trade, or an
overpriced asset. Ask what produced it and what would disprove it.

Write a portfolio policy before choosing the next product:

\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=0.13em]
\item Name the life the capital serves and the dates on which money will be
  needed. Separate survival, near-term commitments, and long-horizon growth.
\item State the reserve in months and dollars, the obligations it protects,
  where it is held, and the event that permits its use.
\item List human-capital and business exposures beside financial holdings.
  Employment in technology plus technology shares is one larger exposure.
\item Set a loss capacity: the decline the household can withstand without
  selling, borrowing, missing an obligation, or abandoning an essential goal.
\item Set concentration limits by company, sector, asset type, geography,
  counterparty, platform, and illiquidity. Name any intentional exception and
  its maximum loss.
\item Choose a contribution and rebalancing rule that can continue through
  ordinary volatility. Record fees, tax consequences, spreads, financing,
  dilution, maintenance, and adviser compensation.
\item Define reasons to sell: the thesis or governing terms have changed, a
  dated obligation is approaching, concentration exceeds its limit, the
  position no longer fits the life, harm has become unacceptable, or a
  superior use remains superior after cost and tax. A frightening headline is
  not a rule.
\item For advice, establish duty, credentials, scope, custody, fees, referral
  payments, product incentives, conflicts, and the conditions for leaving.
  Warmth is valuable in a relationship; it does not establish fiduciary duty.
\end{enumerate}

The technology salesperson offers the most useful test because she turns away
from products and toward values. She spends more on a gym and healthy food;
another person might choose travel. A portfolio exists to support a particular
life, not to win a montage of strangers' holdings. Benchmarks can reveal cost,
risk, and performance. Status comparison merely imports someone else's ends.

Eric Spofford calls for urgency in daily work and patience about the long
result. Keep the order. Act promptly on contributions, costs, documentation,
and risks within one's control. Refuse urgency about outcomes that require
years. Compounding is slow because value, trust, retained earnings, and
investment returns need time. Risk is fast because one obligation can demand
cash now.

The bridge between those clocks is a reserve. It looks idle until the day it
buys another year of good decisions. The next chapter will build that reserve
and the recovery plan around it.
