\chapter{Know What the Number Means}
\chaptermark{Know What the Number Means}

A lawyer in Austin remembers the largest financial number ever attached to
him: \$17.5 million. The number appeared during the dot-com boom, when stock
options in companies going public acquired a quoted value. He could not sell
the shares immediately. By the end of the required holding period, he recalls,
the position had fallen to about \$750,000. It was eventually sold for roughly
\$350,000.

All three figures described the same stake at different moments, but none meant
the same thing. The first was a market value attached to restricted shares. The
second was a later market value. The third was reported sale proceeds before
the tax and other consequences needed to determine usable personal cash. To
call the first figure \emph{money made in a year} would place an unrealized
change in value beside salary, business profit, and cash received as if they
were one substance.

They are not. A number becomes economically useful only after we know what it
measures, who owns it, when it applies, and what condition it is in. Until then,
size creates excitement without information.

\section{Give every number a complete address}

Financial language often loses its nouns. Someone says a company \emph{does
twenty million}, a founder \emph{made five million}, or a transaction \emph{was
worth a billion}. The listener supplies the most flattering interpretation.
Revenue becomes personal income; annual recurring revenue becomes cash in the
bank; an enterprise value becomes seller proceeds; a private-company estimate
becomes spendable wealth.

Before using a number, give it four parts:

\begin{description}[leftmargin=2.65em,style=nextline]
\item[Category] What is being measured: revenue, gross profit, operating
  profit, net income, cash flow, salary, distribution, transaction value,
  equity value, debt, or an asset estimate?
\item[Owner] Which person or legal entity has the claim? Company cash can fund
  company obligations; it does not automatically belong in an owner's wallet.
\item[Period] Is the figure for a month, a year, one closing date, or an
  estimate as of today? Is it historical, annualized, forecast, or cumulative?
\item[State] Is it collected or receivable, gross or net, realized or
  unrealized, restricted or transferable, before or after tax, certain or
  contingent, and measured or merely claimed?
\end{description}

Then add the source and date. Audited accounts, a bank balance, a signed term
sheet, an appraisal, a platform dashboard, and a person's recollection are not
equivalent evidence. A range may be more honest than a precise figure when the
underlying value is uncertain. Precision in the answer cannot exceed precision
in the evidence.

This discipline is not pedantry. It changes decisions. A company with strong
revenue and weak collection may need working capital. A profitable owner with
no distribution may need personal liquidity. A valuable but concentrated
shareholding may need a survival plan rather than a celebration.

\section{Follow revenue until it becomes available cash}

In Scottsdale, the founder of a healthcare company reports about \$100 million
in annual revenue, complete ownership, and an estimated personal net-worth
range of \$500 million to \$700 million. In the same set of interviews, an
online fitness founder reports more than \$20 million of annual recurring
revenue. An industrial distributor reports about \$250 million of revenue in
strong years. These accounts indicate businesses of substantial scale. They do
not reveal how much cash any owner may safely spend.

The operating path has several gates:

\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
\text{revenue}\\
-\text{refunds, discounts, and direct costs}\\
=\text{gross profit},\\[0.5ex]
\text{gross profit}-\text{operating expenses}\\
=\text{operating profit}.
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
\text{operating profit}\\[-0.35ex]
\xrightarrow{\text{collection and working capital}}\\[-0.35ex]
\text{operating cash}\\[-0.35ex]
\xrightarrow{\text{capital spending, debt, and tax}}\\[-0.35ex]
\text{change in cash}.
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}

These lines are a reasoning bridge, not a substitute for the applicable
accounts. Revenue recognition, depreciation, financing, tax, and entity rules
depend on the business and jurisdiction. Their purpose here is simpler: do not
jump from the top line to the owner's life.

Annual recurring revenue needs its own qualifiers. Is it contracted or merely
annualized from a recent month? How much has been collected? What percentage of
customers renew voluntarily? What does delivery, support, refund, and recovery
cost? Does one customer or channel dominate? A recurring invoice can support a
durable company when continued value earns continued payment. It can also hide
churn, discounts, service burden, and fragile concentration.

The healthcare founder says he retained cash in the company to survive a bad
month. A later operator recommends maintaining an eighteen-month cash-flow
model. Those are related ideas, but they are not the same quantity. A model is
a forecast horizon; a runway is the time liquid resources can support a stated
net outflow. Neither proves resilience unless its assumptions include delayed
collection, obligations, growth commitments, and a bad case severe enough to
matter.

\section{Reconcile before you calculate}

A Houston restaurant owner offers three measurements of the same operation. He
reports a 4,600-square-foot restaurant, a one-million-dollar December, and just
under \$10 million of annual revenue. He also gives revenue density of roughly
\$2,300 to \$2,400 per square foot.

The figures do not reconcile exactly. Ten million dollars divided by 4,600
square feet is about \$2,174 per square foot per year; a December run rate
continued for twelve months would be \$12 million, not just under \$10 million.
The discrepancy may come from rounding, a different area definition, a
different period, or imperfect recollection. Without records, choosing the
explanation would be invention.

The right response is not to discard the whole account. The restaurant may
indeed produce unusually high sales from a small footprint. Preserve the
reported figures, label the mismatch, and ask for the denominator and period
before using the ratio. Then ask what the ratio omits: food and labor cost,
rent, waste, payment fees, insurance, taxes, equipment, owner labor, employee
bonuses, and the service intensity required to know and attend to customers.

This is a general reconciliation rule. When a monthly number and an annual
number disagree, do not silently annualize the more dramatic one. When a ratio
is quoted, recover both numerator and denominator. When a total spans several
companies, do not assign it to one company. When someone answers a profit
question with revenue, retain the answer but correct the category.

\section{Treat a valuation as a proposition}

A private-equity operator in Houston is asked about the most money he made in a
year. He redirects the answer from dividends or company earnings to a reported
portfolio value of about \$420 million. That mismatch is more instructive than
the headline. Annual realized income is a flow during a period; portfolio value
is a stock estimated at a point in time.

For a public security, a visible market price still leaves selling restrictions,
tax, concentration, bid depth, and price movement between quotation and sale.
For a private security, the mark may also depend on an old financing round, a
different class of shares, liquidation preferences, dilution, debt, transfer
limits, sparse transactions, and assumptions about a future exit. A portfolio
total can combine positions measured on incompatible dates and terms.

Record marked value separately from realized proceeds. For each important
holding, note the valuation date and method, the precise right owned, ownership
on a fully diluted basis, senior claims, restrictions, obligations, and the
range that could plausibly be realized after time and friction. Do not sum the
optimistic endpoint of every range and call the result liquidity.

For an immediate decision by person or entity \(p\), a more useful quantity is

\begin{equation}
A_{p,t}=C^{\mathrm{free}}_{p,t}+L^{\mathrm{net}}_{p,t}
       -O^{\mathrm{due}}_{p,t}-R^{\mathrm{protected}}_{p,t},
\end{equation}

where unrestricted cash and reliably realizable liquid assets are reduced by
obligations due and reserves that have already been assigned to tax, payroll,
health, repair, or survival. Restricted and illiquid holdings remain visible
on a separate balance sheet; they do not enter \(A_{p,t}\) merely because a
dashboard gives them a price. The equation is a decision aid, not a legal or
accounting definition.

The Austin lawyer's experience does not prove that every owner should sell at
the first opportunity. A sale can surrender future value, create tax, or
violate a sound long-term plan. His case demonstrates concentration joined to
forced waiting: a quoted fortune can fall while its owner lacks the right or
practical ability to convert it. A plan should be made before the lockup ends,
with qualified advice, rather than derived from the peak after it vanishes.

\section{Do not turn a good year into a permanent burden}

At the opening of another Houston interview, a luxury concierge answers a
question about his best year with a range of \$2.5 million to \$5 million. The
brief exchange does not establish whether this was personal income, business
revenue, profit, or cash after tax. His more durable point is that earning and
retaining are different abilities. A person can generate a large flow and
still build little resilience.

An anonymous commercial-real-estate broker makes the same distinction through
a purchase. He reports a seven-figure year and warns his younger self against
immediately answering it with a conspicuous sports car. The car is not the
problem by definition. The danger is allowing an exceptional flow to create a
recurring standard of housing, transport, debt service, insurance, maintenance,
and social expectation that an ordinary year cannot carry.

Before adding a fixed commitment after a strong year, calculate from cash that
is personal, collected, after tax, and free of prior obligations. Ask how much
of the income is repeatable without damaging health, family, workers, or the
business that produced it. Price the continuing cost, not only the purchase.
Then rerun the household under a weak year, an interruption, and a delayed
recovery. A purchase may still be worth making; it should be chosen as part of
the desired life rather than used to prove that the peak happened.

Visible restraint is not proof of wealth either. A person may spend little
because of fear, scarcity, debt, inaccessible assets, or responsibilities that
remain unseen. The useful measure is whether resources, commitments, and the
life they support fit together over time.

\section{Use the number card}

Take every number large enough to affect a decision and write one line for each
field below. If an answer is unknown, write \emph{unknown}; do not replace it
with the most convenient assumption.

\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=0.13em]
\item State the figure exactly as reported, together with its source and date.
\item Name the category, owner or entity, and period.
\item Mark cash or accrual; gross or net; historical, annualized, or forecast.
\item Mark realized or estimated; liquid, restricted, pledged, or illiquid.
\item List debt, tax, senior claims, reserves, contingencies, and delivery
  obligations that stand ahead of use.
\item Reconcile it with the related statements: month to year, revenue to
  profit, profit to cash, enterprise value to equity, and portfolio mark to
  realizable proceeds.
\item Record a range and confidence level appropriate to the evidence.
\item Name the decision the figure is supposed to support and the consequence
  if it is wrong.
\end{enumerate}

Do this before admiration, comparison, borrowing, spending, or advice. The
exercise will make some fortunes smaller, some businesses stronger, and some
apparently modest positions safer than they first appeared.

Once a number has an address, capital can be judged by what it must carry.
Debt, in particular, should not be praised for boldness or rejected from fear.
It should be tested against the asset, cash flow, promises, and downside placed
behind it.
