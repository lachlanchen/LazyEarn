# How You Got Rich Architecture

Status: architecture accepted for drafting. The 135-source evidence map is
complete. `chapter_contracts.json` is the executable source of chapter titles,
primary evidence, recurring cases, contradictions, reader payoffs, and
transitions.

## Narrative Spine

A well-lived life is the end. Wealth is infrastructure: it can buy time,
resilience, choice, care, and room to act, but it cannot decide what deserves a
life. Leadership is stewardship of the people and systems that make durable
business, freedom, and happiness possible.

The book therefore moves in one direction:

1. Choose what money is for.
2. Make something another person genuinely values.
3. Turn repeated value into responsible ownership.
4. Make capital survive risk and time.
5. Use freedom for a life worth living.

## Opening Contract

The introduction begins with the seduction of the visible number, then reveals
the categories hidden inside it: income, revenue, profit, company value, paper
wealth, liquidity, spending, security, and freedom. It introduces the School of
Hard Knocks interviews as reported experience rather than a referendum won by
successful people. It names selection, memory, incentives, starting conditions,
and luck without draining the stories of practical value.

The introduction must credit School of Hard Knocks, its host, and interviewees;
explain that stories are paraphrased unless a checked quotation is marked; and
state that inclusion does not imply endorsement.

## Part I: What Money Is For

### 1. A Number Cannot Tell You What Rich Means

Separate status, income, assets, liquidity, security, autonomy, and happiness.
End with a capability-based definition of enough. Primary sources: 7, 20, 102,
130.

### 2. The Unequal Starting Line

Place agency inside geography, family, migration, health, education, networks,
timing, and luck. Resourcefulness matters without making access equal. Primary
sources: 81, 115.

### 3. Skills, Work, and the First Margin

Connect practiced capability to earned income, household alignment, high-cost
debt, reserves, and the first surplus available for choice. Primary sources:
36, 124, 135.

### 4. Choose the Life Before the Income

Design the desired allocation of time, place, family, health, work, uncertainty,
and contribution before choosing the income machine. Primary sources: 120, 129.

## Part II: Make Something Valuable

### 5. Find the Expensive Problem

Identify painful, frequent, urgent, or costly problems in reachable markets.
Novelty and enthusiasm do not replace buyer evidence. Primary sources: 8, 46,
58, 63, 112.

### 6. Listen Before You Build

Use observation, interviews, complaints, bad reviews, substitutes, and small
tests. Customers reveal needs without dictating the final design. Primary
sources: 10, 52, 98, 114, 125, 140.

### 7. Price the Outcome

Price saved time, avoided loss, changed capability, fit, and credible future
use rather than only input cost or prestige. Primary sources: 13, 45, 54, 78,
94, 118.

### 8. Sales Is a Test of Reality

Treat selling as diagnosis, direct asking, truthful explanation, respectful
follow-up, and learning under rejection. A clear no ends the pursuit. Primary
sources: 23, 72, 77, 108, 137.

### 9. Distribution Opens the Door; Trust Keeps It Open

Follow attention through trust, transaction, delivery, return, referral, and
recovery. Recognition, audience, and reputation are not synonyms. Primary
sources: 17, 26, 30, 33, 60, 70, 91, 92, 95, 104, 126.

## Part III: Own the Machine

### 10. From Skill to Ownership

Show the ladder from labor and professional skill to equity, practices,
products, rights, and claims on future value. Ownership creates responsibility
as well as upside. Primary sources: 15, 34, 64, 71, 100, 103.

### 11. A Business That Can Leave the Room

Develop process, standards, delegation, recurring revenue, documented judgment,
retention, and founder-time release. Primary sources: 9, 29, 57, 67, 85, 87,
96.

### 12. Working Capital Is the Price of Growth

Explain the delay between selling, delivering, collecting, paying, hiring, and
financing. Growth changes the job and can consume cash before it reports profit.
Primary sources: 35, 39, 41, 42, 65, 68, 73, 75, 76, 131, 132.

### 13. The Company Is Other People

Treat hiring, partnership, culture, dissent, accountability, safety, and
leadership as human obligations, not motivational leverage. Primary sources: 5,
38, 43, 109, 113, 127, 138.

### 14. Own the Rights and Critical Links

Connect platforms, intellectual property, contracts, licenses, distribution,
bottlenecks, acquisitions, and integration as forms of durable control. Primary
sources: 6, 11, 22, 25, 27, 31, 47, 51, 53, 55.

### 15. Build Something That Can Outlive You

Bring exit design, transferability, succession, portable reputation, and
post-founder continuity together without making sale the only purpose of a
company. Primary sources: 2, 134.

## Part IV: Capital, Risk, and Time

### 16. Know What the Number Means

Give the reader a clean grammar for revenue, profit, cash flow, distributions,
deal size, equity value, paper wealth, liquidity, and annual spending. Primary
sources: 61, 69, 106, 117, 128.

### 17. Debt Must Be Carried by an Asset

Judge leverage through realistic cash flow, rates, collateral, covenants,
vacancy or demand loss, refinancing, and the survival case. Primary sources: 12,
44, 48, 49, 74, 110, 121.

### 18. Compounding Is Slow; Risk Is Fast

Reconcile concentration and diversification, reinvestment and liquidity, public
markets and productive assets, patience and reasons to sell. Primary sources:
14, 24, 32, 40, 50, 62, 66, 80, 84, 89, 93, 97, 105, 111, 136, 139.

### 19. Cash Buys the Right to Survive

Separate household reserve, operating runway, committed project cash, and
opportunity capital. Liquidity preserves time and bargaining power. Primary
sources: 21, 59, 82, 101, 119.

### 20. The Shell Around the Risk

Place taxes, entities, contracts, disclosure, insurance, advisers, and
professional duties around the risks they are meant to contain. Primary
sources: 1, 56.

## Part V: Enough

### 21. The Cost of the Game

Confront pressure, health, relationships, identity, leadership burden, ethical
compromise, and regret. Sacrifice is a cost, not proof of seriousness. Primary
sources: 28, 37, 79, 88, 99, 107, 122.

### 22. Freedom Needs a Use

Follow leaving, retirement, post-exit boredom, family, travel, chosen work,
service, and renewed creation. Freedom from compulsion needs a positive use.
Primary sources: 83, 123, 133.

### 23. Enough

Return to scarcity, diminishing returns, happiness, meaning, faith, family,
service, and stewardship without imposing one answer. Primary sources: 3, 4,
90.

## Ending Contract

The ending does not recap twenty-three lessons. It returns to a person deciding
what to do with a finite day. The final movement joins household resilience,
useful work, fair leadership, capital that can wait, and freedom that can be
shared. The closing sentence must leave the reader with agency and duty rather
than a wealth promise.

## Drafting Rules

- A chapter opens with a scene or decision only when it advances the argument.
- A recurring person returns only when a later decision changes what the first
  appearance meant.
- Reported numbers are labeled by category and attribution; none is decorative.
- Contradictions appear where the attractive claim appears, not in a disclaimer
  appendix.
- Practical tools grow from the chapter's evidence and remain proportionate to
  it.
- No chapter ends with a mechanical summary. Its last paragraph changes the
  question and hands the reader to the next chapter.
- Source acceptance follows prose, attribution, and timestamp review. Mapping
  alone is not acceptance.
