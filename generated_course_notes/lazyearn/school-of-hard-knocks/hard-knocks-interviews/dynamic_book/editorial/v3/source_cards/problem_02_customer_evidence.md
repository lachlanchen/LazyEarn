# Problem 02: Customer Evidence, Commitment, and Honest Experiments

Status: focused close read accepted for V3 architecture; manuscript change
deferred until the adjacent business-model, sales, and working-capital cards are
reconciled.

## Question

How can a founder learn whether a problem matters enough to support a business
without mistaking compliments, forecasts, traffic, pilot interest, or one sale
for proof? What can a deliberately cheap experiment establish, and what remains
untested when the real product is costly, regulated, operationally difficult,
or dependent on several decision-makers?

## Synthesis

Customer evidence is not a binary state. It is a ladder in which each rung
answers a different question:

1. **A claim or compliment** shows what a person is willing to say in the
   conversation. It may reveal language, but it establishes neither need nor
   intent.
2. **Observed past behavior** shows the workaround, inconvenience, budget,
   switching cost, and people already involved. It establishes that something
   happens, not that this offer should exist.
3. **A costly commitment** gives up time, reputation, access, data, or money to
   advance the decision. It is stronger than praise, but a ceremonial pilot or
   effortless signup can remain cheap theater.
4. **A transaction under real terms** shows that one buyer chose this exchange
   at this price, through this channel, under these conditions. It does not yet
   prove benefit, retention, margin, or a market.
5. **Use, reorder, renewal, or referral** shows that value survived contact
   with ordinary life. Support burden, refunds, non-use, and transferred work
   belong in the same observation.
6. **Repeatable acquisition and delivery** show whether appropriate customers
   can be reached and served without consuming more cash, labor, trust, or risk
   than the exchange creates.
7. **Durable economics and safeguards** test whether the business can survive
   variation, growth, regulation, failure, and the departure of unusually
   committed early participants.

No rung is worthless. The error is asking one rung to answer a later question.
A video demonstration can test whether an audience requests access; it cannot
prove the technology works. A concierge service can test experienced value; it
cannot prove software economics. A paid pilot can test a buyer's commitment;
it may not test procurement at scale or everyday user adoption. A first sale
can defeat polite indifference and still produce a bad business.

The experiment should therefore be the smallest **faithful** test, not merely
the cheapest artifact. Before running it, record:

- the decision the result will inform;
- the single risky assumption under test;
- the behavior that would count as evidence;
- the threshold and time window chosen before seeing the result;
- which parts of the real exchange the test reproduces;
- which parts it deliberately does not test;
- the safety, privacy, legal, and ethical floor that cannot be suspended; and
- the next action for a positive, negative, or ambiguous result.

This contract prevents a founder from moving the goalposts after an attractive
number appears. It also makes a negative result narrower and more useful: the
test may reject a channel, message, segment, price, or workflow without proving
that the underlying human problem is imaginary.

## Contradictions and Limits

- Meroff's claim that sales are the only indicator is too strong. A sale is a
  powerful early commitment, but can be induced by novelty, discount, personal
  loyalty, pressure, subsidy, or a promise the company cannot profitably keep.
- A thousand biased conversations do not become reliable through volume.
  Selection, leading questions, role confusion, and politeness can repeat the
  same error at scale.
- Jones's Regain case is retrospective testimony from one operator. Its value
  is the visible mismatch among clinician, patient, payer, user, and retailer,
  not a universal healthcare launch formula.
- Early adopters tolerate burdens that mainstream customers will not. Their
  behavior can establish a beachhead while overstating the eventual market.
- A smoke test may measure curiosity rather than purchase. A free trial may
  measure low-friction signup rather than sustained use. Aggregate traffic can
  grow while every customer cohort deteriorates.
- Cheap simulations cannot validate technical feasibility, reliability,
  capacity, unit cost, regulated performance, or rare catastrophic failure
  unless those conditions are actually represented.
- An MVP is not permission to weaken informed consent, accessibility, security,
  clinical care, financial suitability, labor protection, or any other duty
  whose failure would be borne by someone used as an experiment.
- Correlation after a product change is not automatically attribution.
  Seasonality, sales effort, pricing, staffing, policy, and concurrent changes
  must be considered before declaring a mechanism proven.

## V3 Decisions

1. Keep the five-part, twenty-three-chapter architecture. This evidence
   strengthens Chapters 5--8 rather than requiring a startup-method chapter.
2. Retain Abraham, Meroff, Hill, and the other interviews as the narrative
   center. Do not replace them with imported Zappos, Groupon, Dropbox, or IMVU
   stories.
3. Add a compact evidence ladder to Chapter 6 and let it recur briefly in
   Chapters 8, 11, and 12 as the business moves from conversation to sale,
   repeat use, repeatable acquisition, and viable delivery.
4. Preserve Chapter 8's existing sentence that one sale is one observed
   decision. Make explicit that a sale validates only the exchange it actually
   reproduces.
5. Add the original experiment contract above as a practical reader tool,
   avoiding trademarked startup jargon where ordinary language is clearer.
6. Keep decision roles visible. Evidence from a user, payer, approver,
   champion, retailer, or regulator must be labeled by the decision that role
   can actually make.
7. Reserve claims about scale, margin, working capital, and business-model
   durability for the next source card; do not let early demand evidence settle
   those questions in advance.

## Continuity Test

The first card defined what money is for. This card protects the route toward
it from self-deception: start with another person's real burden, observe the
existing life around it, ask for progressively stronger evidence, and never
claim that a test proved more than it reproduced. The next card must follow the
customer decision into the full operating system: price, channel, capacity,
cash timing, and the conditions under which apparent growth destroys freedom.
