# Narrative Map

## Opening Setup

The opening is a rapid thesis preview: do not diversify before the main business has real scale, take risks while life still gives the risk time to matter, and trust repeatable skill more than current circumstance. After that, the interview resets formally with Grant Mitt introduced as founder of Mitt Group and Mitt Blockchain, so the chapter should move from preview claims into the structured question sequence.

The real spine is not mathematics in the physics sense; it is a practical model of business judgment under base rates, concentration, distribution, risk, skill, scaling, energy, industry choice, and hiring. The writing should keep that sequence rather than turning the interview into generic advice.

## Beat Sequence

1. **Preview: the hard theses arrive before the introduction.**  
   Mitt’s claims about diversification after \(>\$10\) million revenue, not wanting delayed wealth at 75, and being able to rebuild from nothing establish the lecture’s emotional and commercial stakes. This preview prepares the reader to hear the later answers as mechanisms, not isolated slogans.

2. **Confidence as market exposure, not age denial.**  
   The first formal question asks how a young founder stays confident among older operators. Mitt answers by making age a market-irrelevant variable: if we make it a problem, others will; once we compete, customers choose. This leads naturally into the next question: if the market is that unforgiving, why choose entrepreneurship at all?

3. **Q&A subsection: If most businesses are bad economic bets, why start one?**  
   Mitt introduces the strongest obstacle himself: most businesses break even or lose money, only a tiny fraction reach over \( \$10\) million in revenue, and employees may average slightly more income than business operators. This tension should survive as a standalone `Question & Answer`, because the answer is not “business is easy” but “necessity plus preparation made it unavoidable.”

4. **Necessity, preparation, and acceptable loss.**  
   He explains that people depended on him, he had time, resources, capital, and the willingness to lose every penny rather than continue on the wrong path. This beat converts entrepreneurship from romance into a threshold decision: the trigger was not optimism alone but readiness under pressure.

5. **Q&A subsection: Why reject diversification when millionaires supposedly have many income streams?**  
   The interviewer pivots to diversification, and Mitt answers against the common slogan. He argues that the large pile usually comes from one thing first, then money gets allocated into buckets later. This should be a second `Question & Answer` subsection because it resolves an apparent contradiction between “seven streams” and “one thing made them wealthy.”

6. **Attention becomes distribution, hiring, and credibility.**  
   The social-media question moves from focus to reach. Mitt gives concrete figures: TikTok, Instagram, 30% to 40% of a new-start group following him first, and Fox Business appearances. The important transition is that content is not treated as vanity; it becomes recruiting, trust, and opportunity flow.

7. **Risk becomes rational when skill is repeatable.**  
   The risk-taking section ties the Ferrari line and Sahara Desert claim to self-trust. It is followed immediately by the sales section, where “you can’t Google experience” explains how that self-trust was earned: rejection, cold calls, Walmart DirecTV sales, and repeated contact with different kinds of people.

8. **Q&A subsection: What actually scales a business?**  
   The interviewer asks about operating in 17 states and moving from six or seven figures toward eight. Mitt rejects the easy answers: CRM, technology, and a single smart hire may help, but they do not double or quadruple revenue by themselves. The answer is people executing fundamentals at scale, so this deserves a standalone `Question & Answer`.

9. **Energy, industry selection, and team filters close the loop.**  
   Burnout is framed as leaking emotional energy from appointment to appointment; solar is framed as an emerging-industry bet made against crowded conventional paths; hiring is reduced to coachability, humility, persistence, and no easy fallback. The close returns to the opening motif: winners are built by pressure, focus, and repeatable execution.

## Transition Cues

- “Two answers” signals a compressed framework answer rather than a story.
- “Once you’re in the NFL…” shifts from age insecurity to competitive markets.
- “If you look at the numbers…” pivots from motivation to base-rate reality.
- “So you got to figure out…” turns the business-starting question into entrepreneur versus intrapreneur.
- “So think about it” introduces the diversification correction.
- “We all heard the saying…” sets up a common belief before reversing it.
- “But just that” shifts social media from audience size to practical leverage.
- “At the end of the day…” repeatedly marks Mitt’s move from examples to operating rule.
- “All scaling is…” introduces the cleanest definition-like moment in the interview.
- “The word no is way more important” marks the burnout section’s practical pivot.

## Recurring Motifs

- Market selection: customers, not age or credentials, decide.
- One thing first: wealth begins with concentrated competence before diversification.
- Skill as portable capital: sales experience and self-trust can travel even if circumstances reset.
- Numbers as pressure points: \(86\%\), \(0.5\%\), \(>\$10\) million, \(17\) states, \(145{,}000\), \(30\%-40\%\).
- People over tools: scaling, hiring, and intrapreneurship all depend on capable operators.
- Energy control: emotional discipline is treated as an operating resource, not merely personal wellness.

## Pacing Risks

A draft may compress the entrepreneurship section too quickly and lose the statistical obstacle that makes Mitt’s choice interesting. Keep the tension: he is not saying everyone should start a business.

The diversification section can easily become generic “focus advice.” Preserve the sequence: one big pile, then buckets, with the \(>\$10\) million threshold and projected revenue claims attributed as interview claims.

The social media section should not become a content-creator aside. Its narrative function is distribution, recruiting, credibility, and opportunity.

The risk and sales sections should stay adjacent. The sales stories explain why the later “bet on yourself” claim is not just confidence language.

The final chapter should acknowledge implicitly through style that this is a business interview, despite the prompt’s Susskind label. Do not import physics exposition or invented formalism beyond light, clearly reconstructed models.
