# Narrative Map

## Opening Setup

The setup is a direct interview question: should entrepreneurs diversify beyond one or two things? The implied premise is that diversification is prudent, mature, and probably important. The chapter should let that assumption stand for a moment before the answer overturns it.

## Beat Sequence

1. The interviewer frames diversification as the topic.  
He asks how important diversification has been for the speaker and for entrepreneurs generally. This creates the chapter’s tension: spreading effort looks like a path to safety, and the answer will test that assumption.

2. The question narrows to “beyond just one or two things.”  
This matters because the issue is not portfolio theory in the abstract; it is founder attention and operating capacity. It leads naturally into a yes-or-no answer about early entrepreneurial behavior.

3. `Question & Answer` beat: the speaker rejects the premise.  
The answer begins bluntly: he does not think people should diversify. This should become the standalone Q&A because the lecture raises a clean obstacle and resolves it with a counterintuitive answer.

4. The speaker borrows an outside formulation, but cautiously.  
He recalls Mark Cuban “or someone” saying diversification is for people who are not strong enough at one thing. The writer should keep the uncertainty in the attribution while using it as a rhetorical pivot into the mechanism.

5. The mechanism is reframed as failure limitation.  
Diversification is described not as excellence multiplied, but as limiting failures because the person is not yet great at one thing. This shifts the argument from “diversification is bad” to “premature diversification can hide weak mastery.”

6. The speaker asks the listener to “think about it.”  
This is the practical transition from principle to example. It slows the pace and invites a sequence: before we talk about multiple businesses, look at how the first business actually developed.

7. The personal chronology appears: he did not start diversified.  
The point is not merely autobiographical; it establishes order. First came focus, then later diversification may have become possible.

8. The solar-company revenue claim anchors the sequence.  
He says the solar company was doing more than $10 million in revenue. This gives the chapter its concrete threshold-like evidence and leads into the broader book theme: diversification should follow proof, not substitute for it.

## Transition Cues

The opening cue is a broad interview prompt: “How important…” followed by the narrowing phrase “beyond just one or two things?” It sets up diversification as the expected virtue.

The first pivot is abrupt and corrective: “I don’t think people should diversify.” That sentence should remain close to the surface because it carries the reversal.

The next cue is borrowed authority: “I think Mark Cuban or someone…” Use it as a loose reference, not a verified citation.

The mechanism cue is explanatory: “So it’s limiting your failures…” This moves from slogan to reason.

The final cue is reflective and anecdotal: “So think about it…” It opens the transition from general claim to the speaker’s solar-company example.

## Recurring Motifs

Focus before optionality: the speaker treats concentrated competence as the condition that makes later diversification meaningful.

Diversification as risk language: the interview starts with risk reduction, but the answer reframes early diversification as a symptom of weak execution.

Proof before expansion: the $10 million revenue detail works as concrete evidence that the first engine had real commercial traction before expansion entered the story.

Attribution caution: the speaker’s uncertain Mark Cuban reference should be preserved as interview texture, not converted into a footnoted doctrine.

## Pacing Risks

A draft could compress the whole segment into “don’t diversify early,” losing the staged reversal from common assumption to counterargument.

A draft could overstate the answer as universal business law. The transcript supports a sharp opinion and one anecdotal sequence, not a complete theory of diversification.

A draft could treat the $10 million figure as a formal rule or benchmark. It should remain a concrete revenue claim from the speaker’s experience.

A draft could erase the uncertainty in “Mark Cuban or someone.” Keep that looseness, because it is part of the source-conscious standard.

A draft could invent diagrams, formulas, or finance theory. With no validated visuals and a short transcript, any model should be explicitly reconstructed from the interview’s logic.
