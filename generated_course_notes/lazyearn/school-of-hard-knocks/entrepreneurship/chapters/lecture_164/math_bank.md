## Derivation Steps
1. Bots and reported metrics. Step 1: bots can like, retweet, comment, and post. Step 2: those actions enter engagement counts. Step 3: bot accounts also elevate reported monthly active users. Step 4: higher reported numbers look favorable when presented to shareholders. Step 5: therefore the lecture links going private to the ability to clean up bots instead of defending inflated metrics.

2. Authentication as infrastructure. Step 1: once bot removal becomes the goal, the platform needs a way to distinguish real participants from fake ones. Step 2: authentication ties an account to a person, business, or accountable page. Step 3: this reduces spam/scam pressure on content and users. Step 4: the same layer later supports moderation consistency and creator payouts.

3. Equal-service moderation rule. Step 1: the lecture claims universal satisfaction is impossible. Step 2: it replaces that impossible target with equal platform service for authenticated users. Step 3: removals remain possible only for specific reasons set in advance. Step 4: moderation is thereby framed as rule application rather than favoritism.

4. Creator payment logic. Step 1: the lecture says Twitter currently leaves most monetization to creators’ own marketing or affiliate strategies. Step 2: rival platforms are described as paying on views and engagement. Step 3: the speaker imports the “play to earn” analogy. Step 4: this becomes a speculative “tweet to earn” model. Step 5: authentication is reintroduced so payouts go to the right person rather than to bots.

5. Open-source algorithm logic. Step 1: privatization is presented as creating freedom to change the algorithm. Step 2: open sourcing is said to increase transparency for creators and users. Step 3: the lecture then attaches operational benefits: lower maintenance cost, higher speed, more flexibility, more security. Step 4: these consequences should be recorded as claims, not as proved implications.

6. Edit button and authenticity. Step 1: adding an edit button looks attractive on the surface. Step 2: untracked edits threaten the integrity of the original stated tweet. Step 3: the lecture proposes a backlog/history of prior versions. Step 4: the current tweet may still be updated. Step 5: authenticity is preserved because earlier versions remain accessible.
