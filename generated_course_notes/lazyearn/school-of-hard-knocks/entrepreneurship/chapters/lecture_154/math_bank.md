## Definitions And Objects

- \(i\): interviewee index; use this to keep financial claims attached to the correct speaker.
- \(Y_t\): annual earned income in year \(t\).
- \(Y_{\max}^{(i)}\): peak annual income reported by speaker \(i\).
- \(P_{\text{sale}}^{(i)}\): reported sale price or exit value associated with speaker \(i\).
- \(T_{\text{hold}}\): holding period between acquisition/start and sale when the transcript gives it.
- \(C_t\): annual consumption or living expenses in year \(t\).
- \(S_t\): annual investable surplus or savings.
- \(A\): acquisition price or business purchase price.
- \(E_0\): initial cash equity check or down payment.
- \(L\): borrowed capital used in the purchase.
- \(d\): down-payment fraction, \(d=E_0/A\).
- \(r_{\text{no}}\): diagnosed reason a customer says no.
- \(Q_{\text{team}}\): team quality/fit; only a reconstruction variable for writing the scaling logic compactly.
- \(T\): truck count or deployed equipment count in the landscape-business example.
- \(M_{\text{business}}\): money generated by the business; leave unspecified as revenue/profit/cash generation unless the final draft can justify a sharper term.

## Derivation Steps

Lagged-consumption savings rule
1. Let current income be \(Y_t\).
2. Take the mentor rule literally as a spending rule: consume roughly what was earned five years earlier, so \(C_t \approx Y_{t-5}\).
3. Define investable surplus as \(S_t = Y_t - C_t\).
4. Substitute the spending rule to obtain \(S_t \approx Y_t - Y_{t-5}\).
5. Use this only as a compact formalization of the interview advice, not as a complete household model.

SBA-style acquisition arithmetic
1. Let acquisition price be \(A\) and cash down payment be \(E_0\).
2. Define borrowed capital as \(L = A - E_0\).
3. Define the equity fraction as \(d = E_0/A\).
4. For the transcript example \(A=\$3{,}000{,}000\), \(E_0=\$150{,}000\), compute \(d=0.05\).
5. For the second example \(A=\$1{,}000{,}000\), \(E_0=\$50{,}000\), compute \(d=0.05\).
6. Convert that to an asset-control multiple: \(A/E_0 = 20\).
7. State clearly later that these are speaker-supplied examples, not a universal SBA formula.

Sales-objection diagnostic
1. Represent the customer’s refusal as a categorized objection \(r_{\text{no}}\).
2. Use the transcript’s categories first: competition, pricing, or some other issue.
3. Treat the next move as a function of the diagnosis rather than as generic persuasion: \(\text{next action}=f(r_{\text{no}})\).
4. Preserve the logic that diagnosis improves the next attempt even when the present sale is lost.

Scale-through-reinvestment logic
1. Start with the speaker’s premise: choose growth as an operating stance rather than waiting for certainty.
2. Add productive assets when prior additions are observed to create more money, encoded cautiously as \(T \uparrow \Rightarrow M_{\text{business}} \uparrow\).
3. Add labor before overload becomes chronic.
4. Buy better gear and outsource service-side work so managerial attention remains on core business decisions.
5. In the software-company case, scale comes through better placement, development, alignment, and communication, not just through headcount.

## Notation Choices

- Use superscripts \((i)\) to index speakers so that sale prices and annual incomes are never merged across interviews.
- Use \(t\) for year index in the savings rule; do not reuse \(t\) for interview timestamps.
- Use \(\approx\) for “about,” “close to,” and any spoken quantity that is clearly approximate.
- Use \(\gtrsim\) for “a little over” and \(\ge\) for “seven digits.”
- Keep dollar units explicit inside equations, e.g. \(\$38\,\text{million}\), rather than using bare numerals.
- Use \(E_0\) for the cash equity check/down payment and \(L\) for borrowed capital.
- Use \(d\) for down-payment fraction and reserve leverage language for the derived ratio \(A/E_0\).
- Use arrows \(\uparrow,\downarrow,\Rightarrow\) only for directional business logic, not for exact accounting equalities.
- Avoid introducing \(M\) as a standalone symbol for “million”; write \(\text{million}\) in text form to avoid collision with money variables.
- Keep the rejection variable as \(r_{\text{no}}\) rather than inventing a more elaborate sales notation.

- The quoted dollar figures belong to different speakers and should never be combined into one synthetic case.
- \(P_{\text{sale}}^{(2)} \approx \$42\,\text{million}\) is safer than an exact equality because the transcript is interview speech, not audited reporting.
- “A little over a million,” “probably close to 1.5,” and “seven digits” are inherently approximate and should stay approximate.

- The SBA-style examples should be presented as the speaker’s stated financing examples; the final notes should not imply that 5% down is generally available in every case.
