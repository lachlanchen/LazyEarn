## Definitions And Objects

- \(C\): available venture capital pool, about $50 million.
- \(I\): individual investment check size.
- \(R\): company revenue.
- \(E\): EBITDA.
- \(V\): company valuation or sale value.
- \(S_0\): current business scale.
- \(S_n\): business scale after \(n\) doublings.
- \(m_R, m_E\): revenue or EBITDA valuation multiples; not specified numerically in the transcript.
- Investment filter: interesting idea, thoughtful management team, large target market, early revenue stage, and ability to focus/hire talent.
- Scaling objects: value proposition, total addressable market, buyer, key message, organizational structure, metrics, alignment, relationships, channels.
- Sales objects: buyer pain point, true need, credibility, differentiating proposition, mutual solution.
- Risk object: “risk meter,” qualitative appetite for uncertainty, not a formal metric.

## Derivation Steps

1. Investment filter:
   1. Start with an early-stage company.
   2. Check idea quality.
   3. Check management-team thoughtfulness.
   4. Check size of target market.
   5. Check current revenue stage.
   6. Add focus, organization, and talent help after investment.

2. Selling through consequence:
   1. A buyer is not moved by generic upside alone.
   2. Identify what failure would cost the buyer.
   3. Frame the product as preventing that failure.
   4. Add credibility when the seller is small and the buyer is large.
   5. Make the differentiating proposition explicit.

3. Solution selling:
   1. Do homework before the meeting.
   2. Ask questions before showing the product.
   3. Identify the real need.
   4. Name the pain point in the buyer’s language.
   5. Offer a mutual solution rather than a demo-first pitch.

4. Scaling by planned doublings:
   1. Establish the current business scale \(S_0\).
   2. Ask what structure is needed at \(2S_0\).
   3. Ask again for \(4S_0\).
   4. Pre-design organization, metrics, alignment, relationships, and channels.
   5. Use the plan to reduce wasted time when growth arrives.

5. Risk and due diligence:
   1. Accept that not every good investment succeeds.
   2. If every investment succeeds, risk appetite may be too low.
   3. Review the data available at decision time.
   4. Study the people beyond the demo or deck.
   5. Check character, mutual respect, and shared goals.
