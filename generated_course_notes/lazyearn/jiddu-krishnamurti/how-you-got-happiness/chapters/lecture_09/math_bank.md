## Definitions And Objects

- `fragmentation`: the fact of being broken into social, psychological, religious, national, professional, and personal fragments.

- `fragment`: a partial identity or movement, e.g. Hindu, Jew, Arab, communist, Catholic, businessman, artist, scientist, doctor, husband, professional role.

- `center`: the dominant fragment that appears to observe, organize, integrate, or control the other fragments.

- `examiner`: the apparent observer of fragments; must be treated cautiously because the lecture asks whether the examiner is itself another fragment.

- `contradiction`: opposition among desires, wishes, thoughts, identities, or movements.

- `conflict`: the felt disturbance arising from contradiction; this is the usual condition under which fragmentation becomes visible.

- `awareness`: not a settled technical term here; the transcript distinguishes awareness through conflict from a possible awareness without conflict.

- `knowledge`: what is known from the past; useful in its proper place, dangerous when it claims to know the present whole.

- `practical knowledge`: knowledge used for limited tasks such as driving a car or learning a language.

- `psychological knowledge`: knowledge used to define self, other, life, the whole, status, security, or metaphysical certainty.

- `biological security`: food, clothing, shelter, and basic physical protection.

- `psychological security`: safety sought in group, profession, idea, image, conclusion, status, knowledge, or the “me.”

- `me`: the possessive center: my position, my happiness, my money, my house, my wife, my country, my God.

## Derivation Steps

1. Fragmentation and awareness:
   1. Human activity is fragmented.
   2. We usually notice fragments one by one.
   3. One fragment may act as examiner.
   4. The examiner may itself be a fragment.
   5. Therefore awareness through a center is suspect.

2. Center and fragmentation:
   1. The center appears to organize fragments.
   2. The center claims authority over the fragments.
   3. The center is itself a fragment.
   4. Therefore the attempted organizer continues fragmentation.

3. Conflict as the usual doorway:
   1. Opposing desires, thoughts, or identities arise.
   2. Opposition becomes contradiction.
   3. Contradiction becomes conflict.
   4. Conflict produces pain or disturbance.
   5. Through that disturbance, one becomes aware of fragmentation.

4. Fragment claiming the whole:
   1. A partial identity appears: “I am this.”
   2. The identity is treated as the whole of me.
   3. Another person or group treats another fragment as whole.
   4. The claims oppose each other.
   5. Conflict follows.

5. Cause of fragmentation:
   1. Quick answer: fear.
   2. Correction: fragmentation may cause fear.
   3. Quick answer: childhood separation or holding on.
   4. Correction: look at the present action.
   5. Identity, conditioning, and security are then examined.

6. Knowledge:
   1. Knowledge is tied to the past.
   2. Practical knowledge has a legitimate limited place.
   3. Psychological knowledge claims to know self, other, or whole.
   4. This imposes a part on the whole.
   5. That misplacement becomes a factor of fragmentation.

7. Security:
   1. The organism needs biological security.
   2. The mind seeks psychological security.
   3. Psychological security is sought through belonging and dependence.
   4. Belonging creates division.
   5. Division prevents shared biological security.

8. The “me”:
   1. Psychological security gathers around the “me.”
   2. The “me” claims primary importance.
   3. The “me” extends into “my country,” “my God,” “my position.”
   4. The lecture stops with this as an unresolved fact.

- Prefer word labels in TikZ diagrams over symbolic abbreviations for pocket readability.

- Use arrows \(\to\) for transcript-backed movement or dependence, not strict causality unless the transcript explicitly frames it as cause.

- Use \(\rightleftarrows\) for reciprocal relations such as fragmentation and center.

- Use \(\widehat{=}\) only as an editorial marker for “is taken as,” not actual equality.

- Do not introduce operators, Hilbert-space notation, derivatives, probabilities, or physics-style formalism.
