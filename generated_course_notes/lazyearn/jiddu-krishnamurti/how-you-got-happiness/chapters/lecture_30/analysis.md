# Chapter Plan

## Lecture Arc

The lecture begins with the large question: what can bring about a radical transformation in human consciousness? Bohm immediately lowers the authority of science for this question, and Krishnamurti presses the discussion toward ordinary life, energy, and the viewer who feels that previous dialogues are too remote.

The first pivot is from transformation in general to relationship as the practical field where the inquiry must begin. Relationship is then shown to be structured by images: self-image, images of the other, images of what one is entitled to, and images of security or pleasure.

The dialogue next tightens the problem: if right relationship is essential, why does one not give it complete seriousness? This introduces energy in the ordinary sense: not a scientific quantity, but the vitality not wasted in drink, chatter, despair, dependence, and self-confirming habits.

The central movement then becomes the machinery of image-making. Consciousness is described as largely filled by interrelated images centered on the self; asking “how do I end it?” is exposed as another image because the “how” quietly reinstates the “me” who will perform the method.

The lecture then works through hurt and pleasure. Insult and flattery are registered, taken over by thought and memory, and become images; pleasure and hurt are treated as two sides of the same image-process, not as separate principles.

The decisive pivot comes with the observer and the observed. The question is no longer how to look at the image from outside, but whether the observer is different from the image. The dialogue reconstructs the observer as past, memory, image, and thought, then dissolves the assumed distance between a “me” who looks and the image being looked at.

The final turn opens toward meditation and the ending of time. If the image-maker is absent, thought has its right place but no longer drives consciousness as anxiety, fear, pleasure, division, and turmoil. The lecture ends deliberately unresolved: what takes place when thought, as image-making time, comes to an end?

## Section Outline

1. **From Transformation To Ordinary Life**  
   Begin with the question of radical transformation, then preserve the correction that scientific expertise is not the authority here. The section should move quickly to the ordinary viewer who asks how this inquiry touches work, family, poverty, tiredness, and misery.

2. **Relationship As The Field Of Inquiry**  
   Present relationship as the “brass tacks” starting point: office, factory, home, sex, children, insult, disappointment, hope, and desire for more. Include a standalone `Question & Answer` subsection: **Why begin with relationship rather than an abstract doctrine of consciousness?**

3. **Image As The Block In Relationship**  
   Develop the sequence in which one relates through images: what I am, what you should be, what I deserve, what makes me secure. Keep the core claim precise: any image about oneself or another prevents the beauty of relationship.

4. **Energy, Seriousness, And The Refusal To Delegate**  
   Treat energy only in the transcript’s ordinary sense: vitality, drive, seriousness. The movement here is from wasted energy to the deeper image that “someone else must do it for me,” and then to the responsibility implied by “you are the world.”

5. **Consciousness As Interrelated Images**  
   Show how consciousness is described as a web of images, mostly centered on the self. Include a standalone `Question & Answer` subsection: **Why does asking “how do I do it?” recreate the image-maker?**

6. **Pleasure, Hurt, And Registration**  
   Follow the transcript’s concrete example: insult or flattery is registered in the brain, thought and memory take it over, and the image becomes hurt or pleasure. Include a compact comparison table for flattery and insult, but keep it narrow for pocket layout.

7. **The Observer Is The Observed**  
   This is the conceptual center of the chapter. Preserve the steps: the observer appears separate; separation creates time and conflict; the observer is the past; the past is image; the image of the observer is not an actuality independent of thought.

8. **When Image-Making Ends**  
   Close with the lecture’s unresolved religious and meditative question: if the image-maker is absent and thought stops except in its proper place, what then? Include a standalone `Question & Answer` subsection: **Is this the death of the self, and what does the lecture leave open?**
