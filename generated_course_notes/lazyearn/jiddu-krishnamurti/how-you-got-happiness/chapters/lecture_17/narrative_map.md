# Narrative Map

## Opening Setup

The lecture begins by defining the form of inquiry before naming the topic. Krishnamurti rejects “discussion” as exchange of opinions and asks for dialogue about problems that actually touch daily living.

The opening student question supplies the concrete pressure: a vegetarian who does not kill asks whether war against communism can be justified as self-protection. Krishnamurti does not stay at the political level. He uses the question to shift from justified violence to violence itself: what is aggression in the human being, and can the mind be free of it?

## Beat Sequence

1. Dialogue Before Doctrine  
Krishnamurti first establishes that this is not an exchange of views or cleverness. This matters because the lecture will repeatedly refuse abstract positions and return to direct observation; it leads naturally into the first student’s concrete conflict.

2. Defensive War As The Doorway  
The student asks whether a “self-protective” war is justified. Krishnamurti notes that every war is called self-protective, then pivots from the justification of war to the deeper fact of violence. `Question & Answer` subsection: “Is war self-protection?”

3. The Whole Spectrum Of Violence  
He broadens violence beyond war: aggression, competition, becoming somebody, discipline according to a pattern, suppression, brutality toward oneself, killing. This expansion prevents the chapter from treating violence as only physical or political, and it prepares the question of structure rather than isolated examples.

4. The Source: The Me As Centre  
A student asks for the source of violence, and Krishnamurti names the me, ego, self as the centre from which divisions spread. The lake image gives the beat its shape: waves of division radiate from the centre. This leads to the next warning: naming a cause is not the same as ending violence.

5. Cause And Effect As A Trap  
Krishnamurti pauses over the assumption that finding the cause will remove the effect. He says cause becomes effect and effect becomes cause, so the chain can continue indefinitely. `Question & Answer` subsection: “Does Knowing The Cause End Violence?”

6. Self And Society Cannot Be Split  
The inquiry moves from the me/not-me division to the relation between self and culture. He refuses the clean separation between inner violence and social violence: we built the violent society and are shaped by it. This sets up the practical question: can violence end in oneself?

7. Violence As Energy, But Not Yet Transformation  
A student asks whether violence can be transformed rather than ended. Krishnamurti answers by calling violence a form of energy, but immediately warns against becoming trapped in words like transform, transcend, and end. This beat should lead into the actual method of inquiry: how do we look at violence?

8. Knowing, Recognition, And The Past  
Krishnamurti asks, “How do you know you are violent?” He unfolds recognition as memory: at the moment of anger there may be no recognition; a moment later the past names it. This is the lecture’s central pivot from moral problem to the mechanics of thought, image, and time.

9. Image, Relationship, And Attention  
The insult example shows how self-image responds as the old. Then the relationship example shows that people often relate image to image rather than directly. `Question & Answer` subsection: “How Is The Image Formed?” The answer: inattention leaves marks; attention prevents marking.

10. No Escape, No Will, No Formula  
The later lecture keeps returning to the fact: naming, condemning, justifying, explaining, seeking causes, and exercising will are all movements away. Questions about order, loneliness, frustration, animals, language, belief, freedom, purpose, and meeting violence in others are handled as tests of the same point. The closing warning is that a formula for non-violence itself breeds violence.

## Transition Cues

- “What shall we talk over together?” opens the whole dialogue as shared inquiry, not instruction.
- “Is that what you want to really discuss?” marks the pivot from the student’s political question to the deeper issue.
- “Could we go into the question of…” is the recurring invitation to move from example to structure.
- “Shall we go into each violence, or shall we take the whole structure?” shifts from list to whole.
- “You asked a question…” often signals that Krishnamurti will examine the assumption inside the question, not merely answer it.
- “So again we come to the question…” functions as recap and reset.
- “Now let’s proceed… otherwise we can go on verbalisation forever” marks the turn from verbal analysis to direct looking.
- “How do you know…?” introduces the recognition/time/image sequence.
- “Look, sir…” is the repeated cue for concrete examples: anger, insult, wife or husband, tree, loneliness, animal, neighbour.
- “Don’t let’s go off” returns the discussion from speculative side paths to the central inquiry: can violence end?
- “We dealt with that question just now” near the end explicitly closes the loop and refuses a formula.

## Recurring Motifs

- Opinion is not truth.
- Violence must be seen as a whole, not fragment by fragment.
- The “me” creates division, and division breeds violence.
- Cause-seeking can become another escape.
- Naming belongs to the past.
- Recognition is memory entering the present.
- Image prevents relationship.
- Inattention leaves marks; attention does not.
- Will is resistance, and resistance is violence.
- The word is not the thing.
- Seeing danger is different from arguing about danger.
- Freedom is not “from” or “to”; freedom is freedom.
- Any formula for non-violence becomes another form of violence.

## Pacing Risks

A draft may compress the opening war exchange too quickly. That exchange is not incidental; it gives the reader the initial moral tension that the rest of the lecture deepens.

The “source of violence is the me” should not be presented as a final doctrine too early. Krishnamurti immediately complicates it by warning that cause-hunting itself can be a diversion.

The recognition sequence needs room. If the chapter jumps from “the past interferes” to “look freshly,” it will lose the careful movement through anger, recognition, naming, insult, image, and response.

The image formation section should preserve the Q&A rhythm. The student’s “space between perception and response” helps introduce time and marking; flattening it would make the attention/inattention distinction feel asserted rather than discovered.

The late questions can look digressive, but they test the core inquiry under pressure. Animals, frustration, Aristotelian language, psychic experience, belief, freedom, and purpose should be handled briefly but in order, as returns to the central fact.

The “violence is energy” material is easy to overstate. Keep it as Krishnamurti’s spoken conceptual turn, not a formal theory.

The ending should not become inspirational. It should retain the severity of the warning: if one predetermines how to act non-violently, that formula is already breeding violence.
