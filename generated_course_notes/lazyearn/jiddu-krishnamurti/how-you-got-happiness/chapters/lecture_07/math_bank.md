- `D`: disorder. Use only in diagrams or compact tables, not as if it were a formal variable.

- `I`: imposed ideal/order. Possible diagram label for superimposed order.

- `M`: measurement. Define as comparison in this lecture’s usage.

- `C`: comparison. Closely linked to measurement, hierarchy, becoming, and self-evaluation.

- `F`: fragmentation. The division-making movement of thought.

- `A`: act or actuality. Use only if needed for Anderson’s contrast between concept and act.

- “What is”: the fact or actuality being observed, especially disorder, violence, or the dew-on-leaf example.

- “Abstraction”: an opposite or ideal made by thought out of what is, e.g. non-violence as abstraction from violence.

- “Observer”: not a neutral witness in this lecture; the observer is the past, division, and part of disorder.

- “Perceiver”: used near the end as parallel to observer; avoid multiplying distinctions unless the chapter needs them.

## Derivation Steps

1. **Imposed Order Produces Disorder**
   1. Begin with disorder.
   2. Thought conceives an ideal order.
   3. The ideal is imposed on the disorderly mind or situation.
   4. The imposition creates resistance, conformity, suppression, or revolt.
   5. The result is further disorder.

2. **Order Comes From Understanding Disorder**
   1. Do not start with an ideal of order.
   2. Look at disorder itself.
   3. Study its nature, structure, and movement.
   4. See the factors that produce it.
   5. Order appears only through that understanding.

3. **Why Disorder Must Be Studied As Movement**
   1. Disorder as a concept appears “unstudiable.”
   2. The objection holds only if disorder is treated as a static object.
   3. Disorder is instead a living movement.
   4. Movement can be observed as it acts, spreads, corrupts, and destroys.
   5. Therefore the inquiry is into the movement of disorder, not an abstract category.

4. **Thought As Fragmentation**
   1. Thought is the response of memory and experience.
   2. Memory and experience belong to the past.
   3. The past is partial, conditioned, and fragmentary.
   4. Thought therefore divides.
   5. Division produces opposition and conflict.
   6. Conflict is disorder.

5. **Opposites As Abstractions**
   1. Start with “what is,” e.g. violence.
   2. Thought creates an opposite, e.g. non-violence.
   3. The opposite is an abstraction from the fact.
   4. Conflict arises between fact and abstraction.
   5. The conflict is another form of disorder.

6. **Measurement And Comparison**
   1. Measurement is identified with comparison.
   2. Comparison structures education, success, religion, hierarchy, and self-image.
   3. Comparison creates becoming: more, less, higher, lower.
   4. Becoming sustains division and anxiety.
   5. Measurement/comparison becomes a major factor of disorder.

7. **Controller As Fragment**
   1. A tradition says measurement must end to find the immeasurable.
   2. It then proposes control of thought.
   3. The controller of thought is itself another movement of thought.
   4. Therefore control remains inside measurement.
   5. It cannot reach the immeasurable.

8. **Observer Is The Observed**
   1. The mind asks whether it can observe disorder.
   2. It introduces an “orderly observer” to inspect disorder.
   3. That observer is an opposite created by thought.
   4. The observer is the past and a factor of division.
   5. Division is disorder.
   6. Therefore the observer is part of the observed disorder.
   7. Observation without the observer is the new factor.

## Notation Choices

- Use `\longrightarrow` for conceptual movement, not strict causality.

- Avoid heavy symbolic compression in final prose. These formulas are note-writing aids, not lecture equations.

- Prefer textual labels inside diagrams: “thought as memory,” “fragmentation,” “comparison,” “observer as past.”

- Use `\neq` only for contrastive conceptual claims, such as `order \neq conformity`, and avoid making them look like formal definitions.

- Use `=` only when the transcript directly supports identity-like language: `measurement = comparison`, `observer = observed`, `perceiver = perceived`.

- Use “what is” rather than inventing a symbol like `W` unless a diagram truly needs it.

- If a two-column table is used, title columns “Imposed order” and “Order from understanding disorder.”

- For the integer aside, write `2.5 \neq 2` only in a parenthetical note; do not build additional number-line notation.
