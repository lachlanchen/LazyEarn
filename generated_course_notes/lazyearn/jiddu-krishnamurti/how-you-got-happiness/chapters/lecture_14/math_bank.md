## Definitions And Objects

- \(\text{beauty}\): not defined as an object, expression, artwork, museum-piece, poem, cathedral, or expert judgment; approached through humility and not-knowing.

- \(\text{sorrow}\): both personal sorrow and the sorrow of humanity; the “what is” from which the mind usually escapes.

- \(\text{passion}\): not lust, not cultivated intensity, not artificial effort; born from non-withdrawal from sorrow.

- \(\text{sensitivity}\): the inward delicacy through which beauty can be perceived; connected to conduct, language, voice, body, mind, heart, humility, and relationship.

- \(\text{escape}\): any movement away from sorrow, including belief, identification, rationalization, ritual, pleasure, karma-explanation, or delegation to another figure.

- \(\text{action}\): not merely activity; in the final section, true action is acting now, not according to past memory, future formula, ideology, belief, or conclusion.

- \(\text{idea/formula/pattern}\): the mediating structure that separates perception from doing and makes action mechanical.

- \(\text{observer}\): the past operating as formula, concept, belief, and division between perception and action.

- \(\text{freedom in action}\): the movement in which seeing and doing are not separated by idea, conclusion, or the observer.

## Derivation Steps

1. Sorrow to passion to beauty:
   1. Human beings suffer personally and collectively.
   2. The ordinary mind escapes, rationalizes, or seeks comfort.
   3. Escape is movement away from what is.
   4. Movement away dissipates energy.
   5. If there is no escape, the mind remains with sorrow.
   6. From non-withdrawal, passion arises.
   7. Passion brings sensitivity.
   8. Without that sensitivity, beauty remains only intellectual appreciation.

2. Escape structure:
   1. Begin with \(\text{what is} = \text{sorrow}\).
   2. The mind moves away through belief, ritual, identification, explanation, or pleasure.
   3. This movement divides attention.
   4. Divided attention dissipates energy.
   5. Dissipated energy prevents direct understanding of sorrow.

3. Mechanical action:
   1. Perception occurs.
   2. The mind draws an idea, belief, formula, or conclusion from perception.
   3. Action is then made to conform to that mediator.
   4. Action becomes removed from perception.
   5. Because it depends on formula or memory, it becomes mechanical.

4. Idea, interval, conflict:
   1. An idea is formed.
   2. Action follows after an interval.
   3. During the interval, division and other incidents enter.
   4. The action is no longer total or complete.
   5. Conflict is inevitable.

5. Seeing as doing:
   1. Perception is not translated into conclusion.
   2. The observer as past does not intervene.
   3. Seeing and doing are one movement.
   4. This is action without dependence on formula, belief, past, or future.
   5. Krishnamurti calls this freedom in action.

6. Precipice example:
   1. Danger is seen.
   2. There is no deliberative formula.
   3. The seeing of danger is instant action.
   4. This illustrates direct perception-action, though ordinary conditioning also operates around physical danger.
