## Definitions And Objects

- `Meditation`: not defined as a method; treated as an inquiry beginning from not-knowing and including the whole field of life.

- `Method/System/Practice`: inherited routine or discipline aimed at an imagined result; linked to mechanical mind and static goal.

- `Tradition`: carrying over others’ experiences, illuminations, systems, prayers, mantras, disciplines, and authorities.

- `Not-knowing`: the starting condition of humility and freedom from the established known.

- `Daily living`: conduct, desire, ambition, greed, envy, pleasure, sex, fear, sorrow, death, work, art, responsibility, relationship.

- `False`: not a judgment imposed by a standard; something seen directly when there is no prejudice or reaction.

- `Fragmentation`: division within human consciousness expressed outwardly as divided roles and fields.

- `Whole human being`: prior to specialized identity; includes understanding of life, death, love, beauty, relationship, responsibility, and nonviolence.

- `Awake`: not episodic stimulation; sensitive awareness of what is happening inwardly and outwardly.

- `Sleep`: explored in relation to meditation; includes rest, dreams, order, and regeneration of the brain in Krishnamurti’s terms.

- `Dream`: continuation of daily disorder when daily life has not been watched or understood.

- `Order`: comes from understanding disorder, not from blueprint, pattern, control, or imposed discipline.

- `Past`: necessary as knowledge, but destructive when it covers the present.

- `Control`: division in which one fragment attempts to dominate another.

- `Controller`: not separate from what is controlled; a creation of thought.

- `Desire`: arises through seeing, contact, sensation; must be watched through its movement rather than suppressed or indulged.

- `Choiceless watching`: sensitive awareness without yielding, resisting, suppressing, or selecting.

## Derivation Steps

1. Critique of method and fixed goal:
   1. Traditions offer methods, systems, disciplines, repetitions.
   2. A method is practiced in order to arrive somewhere.
   3. The destination must already be imagined.
   4. What is already imagined as a destination becomes fixed or static.
   5. Truth, if living, cannot be approached as a static endpoint.
   6. Therefore begin with “what is meditation?” rather than “how to meditate?”

2. Not-knowing as freedom:
   1. Accepting tradition means carrying others’ experiences.
   2. Carrying others’ answers burdens inquiry.
   3. Saying “I do not know” drops the authority of inherited answers.
   4. Dropping that burden gives freedom to inquire.
   5. That inquiry is already the beginning of meditation.

3. Meditation and daily living:
   1. Ask whether meditation is apart from daily life.
   2. Daily life includes desire, fear, ambition, pleasure, sorrow, death, relationship.
   3. If meditation is apart from these, it becomes escape.
   4. Escape from life has no serious meaning as meditation.
   5. Therefore meditation must include the whole field of existence.

4. Seeing the false:
   1. Do not judge a practice as false from doctrine.
   2. Look without prejudice and reaction.
   3. Direct perception reveals falseness or truth.
   4. If a practice does not include life or transform conduct, it is false.
   5. From seeing the false, the inquiry proceeds.

5. Fragmentation and the whole human being:
   1. Human beings are inwardly fragmented.
   2. Cultural roles express that fragmentation: artist, scholar, scientist, priest, businessperson.
   3. Treating the artist as separate or special preserves division.
   4. First there must be a total human being.
   5. Creation/action can be whole only from that wholeness.

6. Awake and past:
   1. Ask what it means to be awake.
   2. Stimulation, crisis, and shock are not wakefulness.
   3. Burden, fear, illusion, and neurosis indicate non-wakefulness.
   4. The past is necessary as knowledge.
   5. When the past dominates the present, one is asleep.
   6. The question is whether knowledge can function without covering the present.

7. Dreams and order:
   1. Ask what dreams are without relying on authorities.
   2. Daily disorder, if unwatched, continues into sleep.
   3. The brain seeks order through dreams or intimations.
   4. If there is order during waking, the brain need not work at night to establish order.
   5. Sleep can then be quiet, restful, regenerative.

8. Control and desire:
   1. Religions prescribe control.
   2. Ask who controls.
   3. The controller is created by thought.
   4. Thought controlling thought is one fragment controlling another.
   5. Fragment-control sustains conflict.
   6. Desire arises through seeing, contact, sensation.
   7. Suppressing desire is control; yielding fragments life into getting and losing.
   8. Let desire flower under full watchfulness.
   9. The flowering itself is the ending of that desire.
