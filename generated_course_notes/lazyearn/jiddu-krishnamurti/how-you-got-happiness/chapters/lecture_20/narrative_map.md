# Narrative Map

## Opening Setup

The lecture begins by refusing to define meditation too quickly. Krishnamurti announces the topic, then immediately says that before entering it we must understand what we are seeking and why we seek at all.

This opening delay is the main pedagogical move. It prevents meditation from becoming another object to acquire. The chapter should preserve that delay: the reader should feel that “What is meditation?” cannot be answered until search, method, control, experience, and authority have been examined.

## Beat Sequence

1. **Meditation Is Deferred Until Seeking Is Understood**  
   He establishes that the first question is not “How do we meditate?” but “What are we after?” This appears first because any answer to meditation can become another object of desire; it leads naturally into the structure of seeking.

2. **Seeking Contains Recognition And Therefore The Already Known**  
   He argues that to seek truth, God, or a perfect life already implies an image or contour of the thing sought. This beat exposes search as predetermined desire, and it leads to the first negation: in meditation every form of search must end.

3. **Order Must Be Founded On Understanding Disorder** `Question & Answer`  
   He recalls the previous talks and lays the foundation of order, virtue, and righteousness, not respectability. The natural question is “How can order come about without a blueprint, authority, or control?” The answer is that order comes through observing disorder without suppression, choice, or effort.

4. **Control Produces Division, Conflict, And Distortion**  
   He moves from disorder to control because control appears to be the ordinary solution to disorder. The lecture reverses that assumption: control divides the controller from the controlled, and this division produces conflict; this prepares the claim that the controller is the controlled.

5. **The Observer Is The Observed**  
   He sharpens the foundation with the example of anger: the one who tries to get rid of anger is not separate from anger. This beat matters because it collapses the inner dualism on which control depends, and it leads into the warning that without this foundation meditation becomes self-deception.

6. **Love, Learning, And Freedom From Authority Are Necessary Ground**  
   He adds love untouched by pleasure, desire, jealousy, competition, and sentimentality, then redefines discipline as learning by observing oneself. This broadens the foundation from order alone to the whole condition of mind, brain, emotion, and relationship; it leads into the rejection of groups, teachers, and systems.

7. **Observation Is Not Exploration Or Experience** `Question & Answer`  
   He distinguishes observation from analysis, exploration, accumulation, and experience. The natural question is “Why do we want deeper experiences?” His answer is that craving for experience often escapes the poverty of what is, while recognition binds experience to the past.

8. **Meditation Without Method Or Practice**  
   Only now does he return directly to meditation. He rejects formulas, systems, concentration on images, and the battle to control the mind; this leads to the positive turn toward listening, quietness, and attention.

9. **Attention Includes The Awareness Of Inattention** `Question & Answer`  
   He says the quiet mind is not cultivated in time but comes when one sees the necessity of clear observation. The natural question is “What if the mind wanders?” The answer is not to battle it but to know inattention choicelessly; the awareness of inattention is attention.

10. **Stillness Extends To Body, Harmony, Sleep, And The Unnameable** `Question & Answer`  
   He extends silence to bodily stillness, sensitivity, love as joy, and the harmony of body, brain, mind, and heart. He then asks “What is the point of all this?” and finally moves to attention in sleep, ending with the limit of description: one can point to the door, but cannot describe what is beyond it.

## Transition Cues

- “But before we go into that...” is the central delaying cue. It signals that the apparent topic cannot be entered directly.

- “So, in meditation...” marks the first return from the analysis of seeking to the announced topic.

- “And, as we said when we last met...” brings prior talks into the present argument, making order and disorder the required foundation.

- “Are we communicating with each other?” slows the pace and marks conceptual difficulty, especially around order without control.

- “Right?” and “Are you following?” function as checkpoints after compressed conceptual reversals: controller/controlled, observer/observed, attention/inattention.

- “Now, if this is clear...” marks a threshold. It does not mean a topic is finished intellectually; it means enough false movement has been cleared to ask the next question.

- “Then what is meditation?” is the delayed return, not the opening question. It should feel earned.

- “We are not saying that at all. We are saying on the contrary.” marks the pivot away from conventional meditation as control.

- “Now, that silence is not possible...” extends the argument from mind to body.

- “Then comes another point in meditation” introduces the final movement into waking, sleep, dreams, and total attention.

## Recurring Motifs

- **The false object:** seeking, hope, experience, method, and cultivated silence all become objects projected by desire.

- **Recognition as the past:** whether in seeking or experience, to recognize something is already to know it from before.

- **Division as conflict:** controller versus controlled, observer versus observed, practitioner versus practised, analyser versus analysed.

- **Observation without distortion:** the lecture repeatedly returns to seeing without choice, suppression, resentment, justification, or authority.

- **The word is not the thing:** description, system, formula, and method are repeatedly denied as substitutes for direct seeing.

- **Attention is not practice:** listening, observing, and awareness are not built by repetition; practice already introduces division and time.

- **Harmony rather than achievement:** body, brain, mind, and heart must be in harmony, but not as a goal manufactured by control.

- **The limit of speech:** the lecture ends by refusing metaphysical description; the companion chapter should preserve that restraint.

## Pacing Risks

- The largest risk is compressing the first five minutes into “do not seek.” The point is subtler: seeking implies image, recognition, prior knowledge, and therefore predetermined desire.

- Do not rush from order to meditation. The lecture spends real time on order, disorder, control, and observer/observed because these are the foundation, not preliminary moral advice.

- The love section can be flattened into sentiment if handled too quickly. It functions as a precise negation of pleasure, desire, jealousy, competition, division, and sentimentality.

- The passage on groups, teachers, and systems should not become a generic anti-institution aside. It prepares the rejection of method and formula in meditation.

- Observation, exploration, and experience need enough space. This is one of the lecture’s main discriminations, and the question of why we want experience should survive as a `Question & Answer` subsection.

- The attention/inattention passage is easy to turn into a technique. The draft must preserve the paradox: practising attention is already inattention.

- The body stillness passage should not be expanded into physiology. Keep it as part of the lecture’s movement from mental silence to bodily sensitivity and harmony.

- The sleep and dream section may feel abrupt if the preceding harmony question is compressed. It is the last extension of attention: from waking observation into sleep, and then to the whole mind being awake.

- The ending should not explain the unnameable. The chapter should close with restraint, not a doctrine.
