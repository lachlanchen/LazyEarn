# Narrative Map

## Opening Setup

The lecture opens as a continuation, not a fresh topic. Anderson recalls the previous unfinished distinction between “being responsible for my action” and “being responsible,” giving Krishnamurti a precise hinge: responsibility as directed will versus responsibility as a total ground of action.

The setup should feel conversational and resumed. The writer should not begin with an abstract definition of responsibility; begin with the return to a live distinction that both speakers already feel is unresolved.

## Beat Sequence

1. **Resuming The Distinction: Responsible For Versus Being Responsible**  
Krishnamurti establishes that “responsible for” points in a direction and carries the implication of will, whereas being responsible is whole, undirected, and not confined to one field. This appears first because the whole lecture depends on refusing a narrow, object-based idea of responsibility; it leads naturally to examples in education, politics, behavior, and daily life.

2. **Responsibility As The Ground Of Action**  
Anderson sharpens the issue by linking it to crisis and the danger of putting action “out there” as an object. Krishnamurti confirms the turn: responsibility is not responsibility for a separate action, because one is one’s action; this leads into the question of what action is based on.

3. **Action From Formula Versus Action Now**  
Krishnamurti tests responsibility through education and inherited social patterns, then asks whether action can be responsible when it comes from a handed-down formula. This is a natural `Question & Answer` point: the tension is whether inherited ideas can produce responsible action, and the resolution is that action means doing now, free from the past.

4. **Authority, Literature, And The Same Problem Without Books**  
Anderson brings in the I Ching and the language of negation, trying to locate a shared insight across traditions. Krishnamurti redirects the inquiry: even if there were no books or teachers, the problem would remain, and the mind would still have to be clear and unrooted in the past; this clears the ground for relationship as the next test.

5. **Relationship As The Basic Foundation Of Life**  
The lecture pivots from inward clarity to human relationship: if responsibility is total, how does it express itself with children, family, neighbors, nature, and the world? This appears because responsibility must become concrete, not remain a general feeling; it leads into the problem of decision and choice.

6. **Decision, Choice, And Freedom**  
Anderson proposes the phrase “clean decision,” and Krishnamurti interrupts the term by asking whether there is decision at all. This should become a `Question & Answer` subsection: “Is freedom the capacity to choose?” The answer unfolds as a precise inversion: choice belongs to confusion; a mind that sees clearly acts.

7. **Image As The Failure Of Relationship**  
Krishnamurti returns from choice to relationship and asks whether one is related to another person or only to the image one has built. This beat must keep the spoken force of “our images have a relationship”; it leads into Anderson’s Keats passage and Krishnamurti’s insistence that relationship without image is tied to freedom from the known.

8. **Abstraction As Escape From Fact**  
The dialogue shifts from “if” to “when,” making abstraction visible in ordinary speech. Krishnamurti then takes violence as the central example: the fact is violence, the ideal of nonviolence is a non-fact, and trying to live the non-fact produces conflict. This is another strong `Question & Answer` point: “Why does the mind invent an ideal instead of meeting the fact?”

9. **Care, Negation, And Education**  
The inquiry turns back to children, education, dependence, gurus, and the habit of treating negation as prohibition. Krishnamurti’s movement is cumulative: responsibility includes love, care, attention, and diligence, but not rule-bound self-command or dependence on authority; this prepares the later teacher-student discussion.

10. **The Responsible Among The Irresponsible, And The Next Question**  
The final stretch asks what a serious person does in a society that trains irresponsibility. Krishnamurti rejects attack, propaganda, and planned intervention, proposing care that does not wound; the lecture then gathers into no-image relationship, love, fear, pleasure, and the next unresolved inquiry: order in freedom.

## Transition Cues

Krishnamurti often pivots with a direct return: “So, to come back,” “So we come back,” or “Let’s come back to this.” These cues should be preserved as structural resets, especially after literary references or examples.

He frequently motivates the next move by asking what a word means in life: “What is your responsibility?”, “What is human relationship?”, “Is there a decision at all?”, “Why does the mind do it?” These questions are not ornamental; they are the engine of the chapter.

Anderson often introduces a bridge from language, literature, or tradition: the I Ching, Keats, the Gita, Simone Weil, biblical phrasing. Each bridge should be brief, because Krishnamurti repeatedly accepts the opening only insofar as it returns to direct observation.

The strongest conceptual pivots are contrastive: not responsibility in a direction, but total responsibility; not decision, but action; not image, but relationship; not abstraction, but fact; not prohibition, but care; not permissiveness, but freedom with responsibility.

## Recurring Motifs

Responsibility is repeatedly widened: from my action, to children, to education, to politics, to nature, to the earth, to relationship, to the irresponsible person.

The past appears as formula, tradition, book, authority, image, and the known. Each form is slightly different, so the chapter should not flatten them into one generic “conditioning” claim.

The present is treated as action, not as a static state. “Doing now,” “the act of now,” and “not betraying the present” should remain connected.

Relationship is the practical test of the inquiry. It is not merely interpersonal intimacy; it includes wife, child, neighbor, teacher, student, nature, and society.

Negation recurs as a subtle term: not prohibition, not will, not choice, but the seeing that denies what is false because it understands it.

Care gathers the late lecture: responsibility becomes care, affection, attention, diligence, and watchfulness not to hurt.

## Pacing Risks

A draft could compress the opening distinction too quickly and lose why “responsible for” is suspect. The point is not grammar alone; it is the movement from directed will to total responsibility.

The discussion of children and education appears early and late. Do not merge those passages into one education section without preserving their different roles: early education tests inherited formula; late education tests care, attention, and teacher-student relationship.

The choice/decision passage can be over-summarized as “choice is bad.” Keep the tension: Anderson offers “clean decision,” Krishnamurti questions the word, then the dialogue discovers conflict and confusion inside choice.

The literary references can sprawl if treated as independent commentary. They should remain transition material unless they sharpen the immediate distinction being made.

The violence/nonviolence passage is central and should not be reduced to a slogan. The sequence matters: fact, inability to resolve fact, ideal, non-fact, conflict, postponement, escape.

The “unconscious” passage near the end needs caution. It should preserve Krishnamurti’s spoken tentativeness and practical contrast with attack or propaganda, not become a formal theory of the unconscious.

The final handoff to fear, pleasure, love, and order in freedom should remain unresolved. The lecture does not answer those topics here; it prepares them as the next movement in the larger course.
