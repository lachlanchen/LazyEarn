# Narrative Map

## Opening Setup

The lecture begins as a continuation, not a fresh topic: human beings need to change, yet they do not. The new route into that problem is the question of the “unconscious,” introduced not as accepted terminology but as something to be interrogated.

The opening rhythm is deliberately destabilizing. Krishnamurti asks who invented the unconscious, insists that the word is not the thing, and forces the discussion away from inherited psychological categories toward direct observation of consciousness, hurt, image, and relationship.

## Beat Sequence

1. **Return to the problem of change, then shift angle.**  
   The lecturer reopens the prior concern: why human beings accept intolerable psychological conditions. This appears first to anchor the discussion in the course’s larger inquiry, and it leads directly into the new angle: whether “the unconscious” is a real structure or a verbal invention.

2. **Question the unconscious as a category. [Q&A candidate]**  
   The lecturer asks who invented the unconscious and whether one is actually aware of such a thing. This creates the first tension: conventional explanation versus direct perception, leading into examples of dreams, slips, incomplete awareness, and hidden motives.

3. **Move from hidden contents to fragmentation. [Q&A candidate]**  
   The discussion tests whether the unconscious is a separate storehouse or the result of avoided material within consciousness. This appears after the conventional account has been fairly stated, and it leads to the sharper claim that separation is already implied by the word “unconscious.”

4. **Name avoidance as the practical mechanism.**  
   The speakers converge on the fact that one may avoid facing disturbing facts until avoidance becomes habitual. This beat matters because it converts “hidden depth” into an observable movement: the wound remains, one forgets, and one forgets that one has forgotten.

5. **Turn from forgotten hurt to the image of oneself. [Q&A candidate]**  
   The lecturer asks whether the psychological brain must be hurt and whether hurt is inevitable. This is the first major conceptual narrowing: hurt is not treated as an abstract human condition but as tied to an image that can be wounded.

6. **Show that the same image gives pleasure and pain.**  
   The lecture establishes a symmetry: if the image can make “me” feel good, the same image can make “me” suffer. This appears after the hurt discussion so the chapter can show why the promise of pleasure cannot be separated from vulnerability to pain.

7. **Ask where image-making begins. [Q&A candidate]**  
   The inquiry moves backward into childhood, parent, security, neglect, and conditioning. This beat should keep its live tension: biological dependence is acknowledged, but the question remains whether psychological hurt requires an image.

8. **Reframe relationship as image-governed or real. [Q&A candidate]**  
   Parent-child and human relationship are tested through the claim that image meeting image may not be relationship at all. The rope analogy then gives the beat its force: moments of apparent freedom or relation are limited by a hidden mechanism that can yank one back.

9. **Identify image-making as the machinery of thought.**  
   The lecture collects the preceding movements into a larger claim: image-making is a major content of consciousness and a product of thought. This leads into the final question because if thought produces image, and image destroys relationship and love, the central issue becomes whether the machinery can stop.

10. **Refuse method and remain with the fact. [Q&A candidate]**  
   The closing movement asks whether image-making can stop, then refuses evidence, experience, method, system, and practice as further image-making. The resolution is not a technique but remaining with the fact without commentary; the lecture ends by opening the next inquiry: what is consciousness without image-making?

## Transition Cues

The lecture often pivots by returning to the larger inquiry: “we were talking about” change, then “approach the same thing from a different angle.”

Krishnamurti repeatedly uses direct interrogatives to shift the level of the discussion: “Who has invented it?”, “Have you an unconscious?”, “Is this inevitable?”, “Where does it start?”, “Can the machinery stop?”

When the discussion risks becoming theoretical, he narrows it back to direct fact: “I want to question,” “What I am trying to find out is,” “Look at it,” “Don’t verbalize it too quickly.”

Several transitions are recaps that reset the inquiry: practically everyone has an image; the image gets hurt; the content of consciousness includes image-making; the next question is whether one is aware of this.

The final pivot is a deliberate refusal of the expected “how”: asking how would turn the inquiry into method, practice, and another image.

## Recurring Motifs

The word is not the thing: terms like “unconscious” may smuggle in separation before observation begins.

Fragmentation: conscious/unconscious, hidden/open, parent/child, self/other, and image/fact all become forms of division.

The wound remains: hurt is not gone because it is forgotten; it continues through avoidance, response, and image.

Image as self: the image is not merely something one has, but something one identifies as “me.”

Pleasure and pain as paired effects: the image cannot be arranged to produce only pleasure.

Relationship versus image: where image governs, relationship becomes verbal, fanciful, or limited.

The rope: apparent freedom persists only within a range; beyond that, the image reasserts control.

Fact versus method: remaining with the fact is distinct from turning the fact into a system, explanation, or practice.

## Pacing Risks

A draft could compress the opening too quickly by treating “the unconscious” as merely a rejected concept. The spoken sequence matters: the conventional view is first explored, then questioned, then recast as fragmentation.

The hurt section should not be flattened into “hurt creates image” or “image creates hurt.” The lecture keeps both directions alive before sharpening the point that the self-image is what gets wounded.

The childhood section is easy to over-explain with external psychology. Keep the pace argumentative and provisional: the speakers are testing whether biological dependence is the same as psychological hurt.

The relationship section needs room. The claim “as long as you have an image, you have no relationship” lands only after the parent-child discussion, the rope analogy, and the admission of resentment.

The closing should not become a self-help instruction. “Can it stop?” is answered by remaining with the fact, but the lecture explicitly blocks turning this into a method, practice, or proof.

Avoid making the chapter sound more systematic than the transcript. The structure can be clear, but the inquiry should still feel like a live sequence of questions, objections, recaps, and sharpened distinctions.
