# Chapter Plan

## Lecture Arc

The lecture opens by returning to the previous question of why human beings do not change, then pivots immediately to a new angle: the invention of “the unconscious.” The first movement is diagnostic: Krishnamurti presses the distinction between the word and the thing, while the others offer conventional accounts of hidden motives, dreams, slips, and unresolved experience.

The next turn dissolves the conscious/unconscious split into fragmentation: what is called unconscious may be avoided, pushed aside, or made habitual, but it is not truly separate from consciousness. From there the discussion narrows to hurt: the wound remains, is forgotten, and organizes response.

The central pivot comes when hurt is traced to the image of oneself. The image can give pleasure and pain, so the same mechanism that promises self-confirmation also makes injury possible.

The inquiry then moves backward into origin: child, parent, security, neglect, conditioning, and society’s role in image-making. This prepares the stronger claim that relationship governed by images is not real relationship.

The final movement asks whether the machinery of image-making can stop. The answer is not a method, system, or practice, but remaining with the fact without thought turning it into another image; the lecture closes by leaving open the question of what consciousness is when image-making is absent.

## Section Outline

1. **The Unconscious As A Question, Not A Given**  
   Begin with the return to human change and the challenge, “Who has invented this unconscious?” Preserve the contrast between conventional psychology’s hidden storehouse and Krishnamurti’s suspicion that the word already divides the field.

2. **Fragmentation: The Hidden And The Open**  
   Present the movement from “unconscious content” to avoided fact: material is not in another chamber, but consciousness has organized itself fragmentarily. Include a standalone `Question & Answer` subsection: “Is the unconscious a separate layer, or a name for avoidance?”

3. **The Wound That Remains**  
   Track the transcript’s move from forgetting to hurt: the wound remains, and one may “remember to forget.” Keep the rhythm personal and exploratory, showing how hurt produces resistance, withdrawal, isolation, and the image of being hurt.

4. **The Image That Can Be Pleased Can Be Hurt**  
   Make this the chapter’s central conceptual section: the self-image is treated as “me,” so pleasure attributed to the image and pain inflicted on the image both feel real. Include a standalone `Question & Answer` subsection: “Can the psychological brain never be hurt?”

5. **Where Does Image-Making Begin?**  
   Follow the debate about childhood, parental approval, biological security, and conditioning without deciding more than the transcript decides. Preserve the tension between actual bodily dependence and the image formed around that dependence.

6. **Relationship Or No Relationship**  
   Develop the lecture’s sharpest relational claim: where images govern parent, child, partner, or society, relationship becomes fanciful or verbal rather than actual. Include the rope analogy: moments of apparent freedom are limited by the hidden cord.

7. **Can The Machinery Stop?**  
   Close with image-making as a product of thought and with the refusal of “how” as method. Include a standalone `Question & Answer` subsection: “How can it stop?” Answer only in the transcript’s terms: by seeing and remaining with the fact, not by system, evidence, practice, or future image.

- [transcript-backed] Pleasure and pain symmetry: if the image can give pleasure, it must also be able to give pain. This can be written as a paired implication rather than a formal theorem.

- [transcript-backed] Parent-child transmission: parent’s self-image conditions the child into an image; neglect itself can communicate an image. Do not turn this into developmental psychology beyond the discussion.
