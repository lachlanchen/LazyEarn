## Definitions And Objects

- \(\mathcal{C}\): consciousness, used only as shorthand for the transcript’s “content of consciousness makes up consciousness.”

- \(\mathcal{I}\): image or image-making; includes self-image, religious image, national image, social image, parental image.

- \(\mathcal{I}_{\text{self}}\): image about oneself, the main object of the lecture.

- \(H\): hurt or wound; use sparingly, because the lecture speaks in ordinary language.

- \(P_+\): pleasure from the image.

- \(P_-\): pain from the image.

- \(R_{\text{actual}}\): real relationship, not image-governed relation.

- \(R_{\mathcal{I}}\): relationship mediated by images.

- \(\mathcal{F}\): fact, especially the fact of image-making and its consequences.

- \(T\): thought, only when needed to mark “thought produces the image.”

- \(\mathcal{M}\): machinery of image-making; transcript-backed phrase, but the operator notation is a standard reconstruction.

- “Unconscious”: use in quotation marks when the chapter follows Krishnamurti’s provisional or critical use.

## Derivation Steps

1. **From unconscious to fragmentation**
   1. The word “unconscious” is introduced as a problem.
   2. The word implies a hidden layer or separate region.
   3. The speakers test whether hidden material is really separate.
   4. The alternative is avoidance within one movement of consciousness.
   5. Therefore the key issue becomes fragmentation, not a separate unconscious entity.

2. **From avoidance to remaining wound**
   1. A disturbing fact is avoided.
   2. Avoidance becomes habitual.
   3. One forgets the avoided fact.
   4. One also forgets that one has forgotten.
   5. The wound remains and continues to govern response.

3. **From hurt to self-image**
   1. Hurt produces resistance, withdrawal, and isolation.
   2. The discussion asks whether psychological hurt is inevitable.
   3. Hurt is said to occur “with reference to something.”
   4. That reference is the image of oneself.
   5. Therefore the image, not bare awareness, is what gets hurt.

4. **Pleasure-pain symmetry of the image**
   1. The self-image can be pleasant.
   2. The pleasure is attributed to “me.”
   3. The same image can be contradicted or attacked.
   4. The pain is also attributed to “me.”
   5. Therefore the image cannot be arranged to give pleasure only.

5. **Image and no relationship**
   1. Parent, child, partner, or society may relate through images.
   2. Image-governed relation appears as relationship.
   3. The rope analogy shows that apparent freedom has a limit.
   4. When the limit is reached, the image takes over.
   5. Therefore relation through image is not actual relationship.

6. **Origin of image in the child**
   1. The child needs biological and psychological security.
   2. The parent has an image about himself or herself.
   3. The parent cannot see the child wholly through that image.
   4. The child receives or forms an image in that field.
   5. Hurt has been prepared by the steady building of image.

7. **Stopping the machinery**
   1. Image-making is identified as a product of thought.
   2. Thought-made images destroy relationship and love.
   3. The question arises: can the machinery stop?
   4. Asking “how” would create method, practice, and another image.
   5. The proposed movement is to remain with the fact without commentary.

## Notation Choices

- Use \(\mathcal{I}\), not \(I\), for image, because \(I\) can be confused with the pronoun “I.”

- Use \(\mathcal{C}\) for consciousness only in diagrams or compact summaries; the prose should keep the ordinary word “consciousness.”

- Use \(\mathcal{F}\) for fact only in the final stopping/transformation diagram.

- Use arrows \(\to\) for transcript-backed movement or dependency, not strict causation.

- Use \(\Rightarrow\) only for strong claims explicitly made in the discussion, such as image implying vulnerability to hurt.

- Avoid formal operators unless a diagram requires them. If \(\mathcal{M}\) is used, define it as “machinery of image-making,” not as a mathematical operator.

- Keep “unconscious” in quotation marks when discussing Krishnamurti’s critique of the term.

- Prefer “image-making” over “ego,” “self-model,” “identity construct,” or other imported terminology.

- Prefer “fact” over “truth-claim” or “data,” because the lecture’s closing distinction depends on remaining with the fact.
