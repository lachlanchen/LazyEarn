## Definitions And Objects

- `\text{image}`: the psychologically constructed self-image; treated as fictitious yet felt as real.

- `\text{image-maker}`: thought as the process or structure that creates images.

- `\text{death}_{\mathrm{bio}}`: biological death of the organism; acknowledged but not the main topic.

- `\text{death}_{\mathrm{psych}}`: psychological ending of image, thought, memory, concepts, and time.

- `\text{known}`: accumulated psychological content: images, memories, concepts, past continuing as future.

- `\text{psychological time}`: past meeting the present and carrying on as future; distinct from clock time.

- `\text{stream}`: transcript metaphor for universal image-making and sorrow manifesting through people.

- `\text{sorrow}_{\mathrm{thought}}`: personal sorrow, self-pity, and sorrow made by thought/image.

- `\text{universal sorrow}`: deeper sorrow of mankind, ignorance, destruction, repeated wars, poverty, mistreatment.

- `\text{compassion}`: not pity or sentiment; described as tremendous energy when deeper sorrow ends.

- `\text{silence}`: absolute silence not produced by control, will, wish, premeditation, or projection.

- `\text{sacred}`: that which is beyond thought-created images and beyond compassion; not examinable as a dead object.

- `\text{intelligence}`: insight that is not personal cleverness; later called universal or global/cosmic intelligence.

## Derivation Steps

1. Death beyond image:
   1. Start with the claim: the self is image.
   2. Ask what dies if the image ends.
   3. Distinguish biological death from psychological ending.
   4. Reject mere image-ending as too shallow.
   5. Move to image-maker/thought as the next layer.
   6. Still reject thought-ending as not deep enough.
   7. Reframe death as the ending of the known and psychological time.

2. From private image to universal stream:
   1. If image remains at biological death, image-making continues.
   2. The image is not fundamentally private: “my image is your image.”
   3. Image-making is treated as a stream manifesting in people.
   4. This stream is not only one brain’s activity.
   5. The stream metaphor opens the deeper layer: sorrow.

3. Sorrow deeper than image:
   1. Image-making is surface wave activity.
   2. Sorrow is not merely pain, loss, feeling, or self-pity.
   3. Universal sorrow is part of the same stream but deeper.
   4. Disturbances in deeper sorrow appear at the surface as image-making.
   5. The inquiry must “go deeper” rather than remain with surface waves.

4. Compassion from the ending of sorrow:
   1. Thought-made sorrow/self-pity cannot be compassion.
   2. The enlightened one sees sorrow and destruction but is not personally in sorrow.
   3. The speaker resists the formula “one must suffer first.”
   4. Corrected formulation: when deeper sorrow ends, compassion is born.
   5. Compassion is described as energy, not sentiment.

5. Silence to energy:
   1. To penetrate beyond death, the mind must be completely silent.
   2. Silence is not produced by control, will, wish, or projection.
   3. In silence there is “nothing” or “not a thing.”
   4. Therefore there is emptiness.
   5. Therefore there is tremendous energy.
   6. This energy is compassion.

6. Compassion to sacred:
   1. Thought is fragmented, limited, finite, and memory-bound.
   2. Anything made by thought is not sacred.
   3. Compassion is not the final point; the dialogue asks what is beyond it.
   4. The sacred is approached as a living thing that cannot be examined as an object.
   5. Relationship to the sacred comes through insight, intelligence, and compassion.
   6. The lecture closes with life identified as sacred.

## Notation Choices

- Use text notation, not physics notation. This is a conceptual map, not a mathematical derivation.

- Prefer `\rightarrow` for lecture movement or dependency, not causal necessity unless the transcript explicitly supports it.

- Use `\neq` only for explicit distinctions: biological death vs psychological death; thought sorrow vs universal sorrow; thought-made sacred vs sacred.

- Use `\sim` when paraphrasing a spoken identification that should not be treated as strict formal equality.

- Use `=` sparingly and only for strong transcript-backed statements:
  - `\text{energy} = \text{compassion}`
  - `\text{life} = \text{sacred}`

- Avoid symbols such as `E`, `S`, `I`, or `T` unless the final chapter explicitly introduces them as local schematic labels. Full words are safer and clearer for pocket layout.

- Do not use operator language, Hilbert-space notation, commutators, fields, or physics identities. None are present.

## Uncertain Mathematics

- There is no visible mathematics and no blackboard evidence. All formulas above are schematic reconstructions from the transcript.

- “Energy” is not physical energy. Do not use units, conservation language, dynamics, or field-theoretic metaphors.

- “Stream,” “waves,” “whirlpools,” and “reservoir” are metaphors. They may support a diagram but not a mechanistic model.

- “Universal sorrow ends but still exists” is a language puzzle in the dialogue. State cautiously: sorrow ends in insight for the one who sees, while the wider human fact continues.

- “Compassion born of sorrow” is unstable if compressed. The safer form is: when deeper sorrow ends, compassion/energy appears.

- “Sacred” should not be formalized as an object, state, substance, or metaphysical entity. The transcript repeatedly denies that it can be examined like a dead thing.

- Opening subtitle material and final repeated “Okay” lines are unreliable transcript artifacts and should not enter the note bank.
