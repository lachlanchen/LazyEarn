## Definitions And Objects

- `pleasure`: Thought-sustained pursuit; not merely sensation and not defined by the object pursued.

- `enjoyment`: Immediate enjoyment that becomes pleasure when thought demands more of it.

- `joy` / `happiness`: Not invitational, not directed, not produced by repetition; recognized only afterward.

- `thought`: The movement that remembers, compares, nourishes, sustains, gives direction, and demands repetition.

- `memory`: The retained image or trace of a perception, especially when thought carries it into tomorrow.

- `image`: In the sexual pleasure example, the thought-image that sustains repetition.

- `perception`: Direct seeing before thought claims, names, repeats, or directs it.

- `attention`: Whole seeing; not effort, focus-training, control, or cultivated practice.

- `control`: Suppression, conformity, religious denial, or patterned effort to manage thought.

- `discipline`: In Krishnamurti’s corrected sense, learning; not conformity or suppression.

- `intelligence`: Sensitivity arising in observation; not a higher self, God, Atman, or inner possession.

- `order`: What learning brings naturally when pleasure is observed.

- `practice`: Routine; explicitly contrasted with perception and freedom.

- `freedom`: The beginning condition for observation, not the reward at the end of practice.

## Derivation Steps

1. Pleasure And Joy Are Not Assumed Continuous
   1. Ordinary language blurs pleasure, enjoyment, joy, and happiness.
   2. Krishnamurti asks whether there is any actual continuity from pleasure to joy.
   3. The inquiry shifts from words to the structure of experience.

2. Pleasure Is Identified By Pursuit
   1. Many different objects can be pleasurable: food, walking, sex, money, power, possession, knowledge, violence.
   2. The common feature is not the object.
   3. The common feature is that pleasure is sustained, nourished, kept going.
   4. Therefore the next question is what sustains it.

3. The Tree Example
   1. A beautiful scene is perceived directly.
   2. In the moment of perception, there is no pleasure in the later sense.
   3. Thought enters afterward and says the scene was extraordinary.
   4. Memory holds the experience.
   5. Desire demands repetition.
   6. The next attempt is not the same.
   7. Substitutes arise: stimulation, drink, sex, festivals, routines.
   8. Therefore pleasure is thought-directed repetition.

4. Enjoyment Becomes Pleasure
   1. Enjoyment occurs.
   2. Thought says, “I have enjoyed it.”
   3. Thought adds, “I must have more.”
   4. Enjoyment has now become pleasure.
   5. Joy and happiness remain outside this directed movement.

5. The Control Problem
   1. Thought interferes with enjoyment.
   2. Traditional religion says: control thought.
   3. Control creates suppression, conflict, and fragmentation.
   4. Krishnamurti rejects control as the solution.
   5. Complete attention is offered instead.

6. Attention Instead Of Control
   1. In complete attention, thought does not “creep in.”
   2. This is not muscular effort or forced concentration.
   3. The poisonous-bottle example shows the structure: when danger is seen clearly, control is unnecessary.
   4. Therefore watching itself is discipline and action.

7. Intelligence Without Metaphysics
   1. Anderson suggests some abiding support behind this seeing.
   2. Krishnamurti rejects the formulation.
   3. The formulation risks importing God, higher self, Atman, or permanence.
   4. Intelligence comes in observation.
   5. Intelligence is not something possessed and then “allowed” to act.

8. Discipline As Learning
   1. Orthodox discipline means conformity, suppression, or practice.
   2. Krishnamurti redefines discipline as learning.
   3. Learning about pleasure brings order.
   4. Capacity may require time, but perception does not.
   5. Seeing and acting occur together.
