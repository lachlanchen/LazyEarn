# Narrative Map
## Opening Setup
The lecture starts with the hook of “free government money,” but quickly defines the real subject as winning SBIRs rather than merely filling out forms. Derezinski then establishes his authority through engineering, venture-creation, and federal-contract experience, using that background to motivate the first governing premise: funding sources have different “colors,” incentives, and obligations.

The chapter should preserve that practical opening. The speaker is not offering an abstract policy lecture; he is setting up a working map for deep-tech founders who need money before customers can fully understand the product.

## Beat Sequence
1. **Why this lecture exists and why the speaker can say it**  
Derezinski explains that MIT lacked a practical account of how SBIRs are won, then backs that with prior applicants, a venture round, and his own federal-contract experience. This appears first to make the lecture feel like field guidance rather than administrative training, and it leads into the claim that the source of money changes the startup’s behavior.

2. **All money is not equal**  
He introduces “different colors of money” and different funder motivations as the conceptual frame for the rest of the lecture. This comes after his biography because the biography supplies the credibility for an incentive-based view, and it leads into the first case where funding type matters: disruptive technology before customer clarity.

3. **Disruptive technology creates a customer-understanding problem**  
He contrasts incremental products, where customers can ask for cheaper/faster/better versions of what they already know, with disruptive products that customers cannot specify until shown. This motivates the central obstacle: we need to reduce technology to practice before the market can respond usefully. `Question & Answer` candidate: “If customers cannot yet understand the product, where does the funding come from?”

4. **The research-to-market boundary gives the lecture its main diagram**  
The `Research to Market Process` slide turns the previous obstacle into a stage map: technology projects explore applications, company projects execute with customer focus, and the dashed boundary separates no repeat customer traction from repeat customer traction. This beat should preserve the warning against recourse funding too early, then lead naturally to SBIR as the proper source for the pre-traction side.

5. **SBIR is quantified and contrasted with venture capital**  
He gives the roughly $4B annual SBIR pool and converts it into a roughly $20B VC-equivalent fund by using the claim that VCs deploy about one fifth of capital annually. This numerical move makes SBIR feel large and strategically important, then sets up a comparison of funder incentives rather than just fund size.

6. **Incentives explain bureaucracy**  
He contrasts angels, VCs, and SBIR program managers, then asks the room to guess the program manager’s first motivation. `Question & Answer` candidate: the class guesses science, jobs, and proper fund use; Derezinski answers that the first motivation is “not get fired,” which explains strict compliance, proposal formatting, and why small errors can be fatal.

7. **Agency structure turns SBIR from one program into many programs**  
He pivots from incentives to program architecture: SBIR may have one name, but agencies implement it differently. Market-driven agencies need outside customers to validate the market, while mission-driven agencies may be the customer and define narrow solicitations. `Question & Answer` candidate: “Is market-driven versus mission-driven just your interpretation?” The answer ties it to America’s Seed Fund/NSF framing while preserving agency variation.

8. **Phases, eligibility, and timing make the opportunity operational**  
He introduces Phase I, Phase II, Phase III, success rates, Phase IIb matching, eligibility rules, fiscal-year timing, and pre-release windows. This beat turns SBIR from a concept into a calendar and compliance problem, leading into student questions about applying, incorporation, citizenship, and duplicate funding.

9. **SBIR/STTR and university involvement expose the hidden cost structure**  
He explains that SBIR permits a research institution partner while STTR requires one, then uses the MIT overhead example to show why university participation can consume much of the award. `Question & Answer` candidate: the audience asks whether STTR is more winnable and how the allocation percentages work; the answer preserves the tradeoff between fewer applicants, more hoops, and overhead.

10. **Proposal mechanics end in a concrete outreach workflow**  
He moves from proposal structure and letters of support to the quad chart/project pitch as the right first contact with a program manager. The lecture closes by converting the whole framework into homework: identify agencies/topics, prepare the quad chart, contact the right manager, ask for fit feedback, use deadlines, treat solicitation PDFs as ground truth, and recognize that AI cannot replace human judgment.

## Transition Cues
- “All money is not equal” is the first major pivot from biography into mechanism.
- “So I wanted to make a specific characterization of disruptive technology” shifts from funding experience to the commercialization problem.
- “Where do I get that funding?” turns customer-discovery difficulty into the SBIR funding gap.
- “The way that we look at this… is by focusing on these two blue squares” introduces the slide as the organizing model.
- “OK, so perfectly designed for this” moves from the research-to-market boundary to SBIR scale.
- “Show me the incentives…” moves from fund size to funder behavior.
- “Does anybody want to take a guess…” creates the strongest explicit Q&A rhythm.
- “Just because it has the same name doesn’t mean it’s the same in all agencies” pivots into agency variation.
- “This is to your question” marks several local answers that should remain as Q&A subsections rather than be flattened.
- “This is the right way to get started” pivots from theory and compliance to the quad-chart workflow.

## Recurring Motifs
- Funding source as behavior-shaping constraint, not just cash.
- Customer traction as the boundary between exploration funding and growth/recourse funding.
- Incentives as the explanation for bureaucratic formality.
- “Fast no” as useful information, not failure.
- Agency variation as a reason to read the solicitation and contact the right program manager.
- Credibility signals: SBIR awards, letters of support, prior team execution, and reviewer-friendly evidence.
- Practical compliance: time of award, duplicate work, timecards, allocation evidence, and official PDFs.

## Pacing Risks
- Do not compress the disruptive-technology setup into a generic “valley of death” paragraph; the customer-understanding problem is what makes SBIR necessary here.

- Do not present `lecture_11_figure_02.png` as a universal startup funding theorem. It is a stage map tied to Derezinski’s practical warning about recourse funding before repeat customer traction.

- Do not skip the classroom guesses about the program manager’s motivation. That Q&A is the lecture’s clearest tension-and-resolution moment and explains the later bureaucracy.

- Do not merge market-driven versus mission-driven agencies too quickly with SBIR/STTR. The first distinction is about customer location and solicitation style; the second is about research-institution participation and allocation.

- Do not turn uncertain legal answers into legal advice. PI visa status, conflict of interest, and agency-specific rules need caveats because the lecturer gives them with caveats.

- Do not bury the closing workflow. The lecture’s practical endpoint is not “SBIR is good”; it is “make a quad chart, find the right program manager, ask for fit, and prepare the homework.”
