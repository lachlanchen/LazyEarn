## Derivation Steps
Expectation reset

1. The lecture explicitly chooses facts over myths.
2. It introduces \(H=\frac{R}{E}\) as the framing heuristic.
3. The transcript glosses this as happiness equals reality divided by expectations.
4. The session then focuses on expectations because unrealistic \(E\) can depress \(H\) even before reality changes.

Failure-rate framing

1. The slide states that only \(10\%\) are still around after two years.
2. The spoken summary restates this as roughly \(80\%\) to \(90\%\) of startups failing.
3. The lecturer immediately notes that different industries use different success metrics.
4. Therefore the statistic functions as expectation-setting, not as a universal law.

Scientist’s loop

1. Start with a business hypothesis.
2. Test it in the market.
3. Treat a bad result as evidence against that hypothesis.
4. Form the next hypothesis.
5. Read a pivot as revision after evidence, not as proof of personal worthlessness.

Decision under uncertainty

1. Innovation means there is no established roadmap.
2. Missing information forces decisions before certainty arrives.
3. Some decisions will therefore be wrong.
4. The lecture treats this as normal operating reality, not as a special defect.

Overwork and judgment

1. The week has \(168\) hours.
2. Using all \(168\) on the business removes recovery time.
3. The lecture compares \(5\) hours of sleep to impairment from \(3\) drinks.
4. Therefore overwork damages the quality of decisions that the business depends on.

Go-all-in timing

1. Estimate how much money the venture must make.
2. Estimate how much one customer is worth.
3. Infer how many customers are required.
4. Ask whether that count is realistically reachable.
5. Validate in small steps before quitting the day job.

Advisory-equity example

1. The founder considers giving advisors \(1\%\) each.
2. The advisors say \(1\%\) is too much.
3. They recommend \(0.5\%\) each instead.
4. The smaller grant is presented as a more sophisticated cap-table decision.

## Notation Choices
- Prefer the typeset form \(H=\frac{R}{E}\) in notes; reserve `R/E` only when quoting the slide literally.
- Define \(H\), \(R\), and \(E\) once from the transcript as happiness, reality, and expectations.
- Use `\text{}` for business quantities rather than inventing new abstract symbols: \(\text{survival after two years}\), \(\text{failure rate}\), \(\text{customer value}\).
- Use `\approx` rather than `=` for all underspecified statistics and comparisons.
- Use `\rightarrow` for the scientist’s loop; do not upgrade it into formal implication or probabilistic notation.
- Keep percentages as percentages: \(10\%\), \(80\%\), \(90\%\), \(1\%\), \(0.5\%\).
- Keep time units explicit: \(168\ \text{hours/week}\), \(5\ \text{hours/night}\).
- Keep “hypothesis,” “test,” “failure/evidence,” and “revised hypothesis” in roman text, not italic variables.
- Do not rewrite the failure-rate material as \(P(\text{failure})\), expected value, or any optimization model.
- Do not invent symbols for investor skepticism, resilience, loneliness, or founder fit; those stay in prose.
