## Definitions And Objects
- Position \(P_i\): the explicit demand, proposed term, or announced answer.
- Interest \(I_i\): the need, goal, fear, hope, or practical concern underneath the position.
- Option set \(O\): the family of candidate actions that may satisfy the underlying interests.
- Relationship constraint \(R\): the continuing-business or reputation constraint that prevents treating the negotiation as isolated.
- Constraint set \(C\): practical and organizational limits such as time, authority, reversibility, and business continuity.
- Founders' first-question triple \(Q_0\): why are we doing this, what does success look like, what are we giving up.
- Circle of interests: a one-page stakeholder map that externalizes distinct interest lists around a shared discussion space.
- Team agreement: an internal operating compact covering interaction norms, meetings, tools, roles, accountability, and decision practice.
- One-way decision \(D_{\text{one-way}}\): a decision that is hard to reverse and therefore demands heavier discussion.
- Two-way decision \(D_{\text{two-way}}\): a decision that can be tested and revised, so it can tolerate quicker action.
- Solution space \(Z\): the set of outcomes the negotiating side can live with while still preserving its substantive and relational interests.
- Internal-company extension \(N_{\text{internal}}\): the same interest-based model, but with added legal/confidentiality/power complications.
- Stakeholder set \(S\): the people or groups whose interests must be modeled, even if they are not all present in the room.
- Low-value/high-value trade: an item that is cheap for one side and important to the other, hence useful in generating agreement.

## Derivation Steps
1. Position to interest to option
   1. Start with the stated position \(P_i\).
   2. Ask why that position matters.
   3. Replace \(P_i\) with the underlying interest \(I_i\).
   4. Search for multiple options \(o \in O(I_i)\) rather than defending the original position.
   5. Use the fence example as the model case: cash demand becomes repair need, which admits non-cash solutions.

2. Technical preference to timing constraint
   1. Start from the claimed answer: “use C++.”
   2. Ask why that answer is being defended.
   3. Surface the time-to-competence constraint: learning another language costs months.
   4. Re-express the issue as a scheduling interest rather than a language ideology.
   5. Reopen the design space around implementation speed.

3. Founder alignment before legal drafting
   1. Ask the first-question triple \(Q_0\).
   2. Practice active listening by restating the other person's answer.
   3. Write both founders' interests into the circle-of-interests map.
   4. Check whether each side has actually heard something new.
   5. Only then move toward founder fit, division of work, and formal agreement.

4. Governance from hidden interests
   1. Surface motives and sacrifices first.
   2. Move to founders agreement: ownership, contributions, equity, departure contingencies.
   3. Move to team agreement: culture, communication, tools, meetings, roles, accountability.
   4. Treat disagreement procedures as part of the operating system, not an afterthought.
   5. Revisit the structure periodically because company conditions change.

5. Equal cofounders and deadlock
   1. Note the structural symmetry \(s_1=s_2=0.5\).
   2. Ask what happens when disagreement cannot be resolved informally.
   3. Separate reversible from irreversible decisions.
   4. Introduce fallback mechanisms: mediation, consultants, staged trials, pre-agreed escalation.
   5. Treat the lawyer's \(0.51/0.49\) advice as a crude anti-deadlock device, not the lecture's preferred theorem.

6. Sandra case as failure analysis
   1. Fix the case facts from the overview slide.
   2. Separate student interests from Sandra's interests.
   3. Generate candidate agreements that satisfy selected interests on both sides.
   4. Compare those candidate agreements with what actually happened.
   5. Conclude that effort and enthusiasm did not substitute for explicit negotiation or documentation.

7. Internal-company extension
   1. Keep the same interest model.
   2. Increase the number of relevant parties so that \(n>2\).
   3. Add asymmetry in power, legal exposure, and confidentiality.
   4. Continue mapping stakeholders one by one.
   5. Position one's own interests in terms that other stakeholders can also accept.

8. Judy promotion case
   1. Enumerate stakeholders \(S_{\text{Judy}}\).
   2. Infer each stakeholder's likely interests.
   3. Translate Judy's promotion request into the manager's and company's goals.
   4. Build a concrete action plan that already looks like execution of the target role.
   5. Present both competence and interest alignment, not just ambition.

9. Little Inc versus Big Inc
   1. Enumerate the internal stakeholder set before the external meeting.
   2. Write down each side's interests, including reputational and accounting concerns.
   3. Construct the acceptable region \(Z\) before negotiation begins.
   4. Evaluate the first offer against \(Z\), not against a reflex to keep bargaining.
   5. Accept if the offer satisfies the firm's real interests and preserves the relationship.

10. Sharing interests under strategic risk
   1. Distinguish shareable interests from dangerous disclosures.
   2. Offer interests that help define the joint problem.
   3. Do not disclose vulnerabilities that would simply weaken your position.
   4. If the other side reciprocates, widen the common problem-solving space.
   5. If the other side refuses to share, the feasible agreement space becomes harder to identify.

## Notation Choices
- Use \(P_i\) for position and \(I_i\) for interest throughout.
- Use \(O\) for option set and \(o\) for a single option.
- Use \(Z\) for solution space or acceptable region; do not introduce multiple competing symbols for BATNA-like concepts not named in the lecture.
- Use \(R\) for relationship/reputation constraint.
- Use \(C\) for shared discussion space in the circle-of-interests figure, not for company.
- Use \(\mathcal{P}\) for the party set in abstract negotiation models.
- Use \(s_i\) for ownership share or equity fraction.
- Use \(D_{\text{one-way}}\) and \(D_{\text{two-way}}\) for reversibility classes of decisions.
- Use \(S\) for stakeholder set when the negotiation includes indirect or internal actors.
- Keep all added notation explicitly editorial; none of it is visible on the slides.
- Preserve visible slide labels in prose exactly where useful: `Circle of Interests`, `Overview`, `Negotiations within a company`, and quoted `power levels`.
- Avoid importing heavier game-theory notation unless the later drafting phase truly needs it; this lecture's math is deliberately light and structural.

## Uncertain Mathematics
- No visible equations appear in the selected frames; every formula in this bank is either transcript-backed compression or standard reconstruction.
- \(N = (\mathcal{P}, \{I_p\}, O, R, C)\) is a useful editorial model, but it is not lecture notation and should be introduced cautiously.
- \(Z\) as a formal solution-space symbol is inferred from Garber's “solution space” and “zone of solution” language; the lecture does not define it rigorously.
- \(a_{\text{first offer}} \in Z \Rightarrow \text{accept}\) is only a compact rendering of the contract case logic, not a universal rule.
- \(s_1=s_2=0.5\) and \(0.51/0.49\) should be framed as governance illustrations; the lecture does not prove a general optimal ownership formula.
- \(D_{\text{one-way}}\) and \(D_{\text{two-way}}\) are note-writer shorthand for reversibility, not explicit lecture notation.
- The Judy and Little/Big stakeholder sets are partial abstractions from spoken examples; the transcript gives categories and roles, not a formal finite model.
- The Sandra case involves several garbled transcript regions, so any more detailed formalization of interests or agreement variables should be kept conservative and checked against the screenshot evidence first.
