# Chapter Plan
## Lecture Arc
- The lecture begins with Hadzima's handoff and Garber's self-positioning as both engineer and mediator. That opening matters because she does not treat negotiation as decorative business polish; she roots it in the practical failure mode of startups whose people and relationships break before the technology does.
- From there she makes the first conceptual split: negotiation can be imagined as win-lose, or as collaborative problem solving. The pivot is not merely ethical; it is structural, because repeated interaction, reputation, and future dealings mean that a startup almost never lives in a true one-shot world.
- She then tightens the analytic frame around a single recurring question: what are the parties' interests? The lecture uses the fence example and the programming-language example to move from visible positions to underlying needs, and from underlying needs to a larger option space.
- Only after that abstract mechanism is in place does she specialize to startup life. The talk narrows first to cofounders, and the first live classroom exercise asks not for terms or equity but for motives, success criteria, and sacrifices.
- The `Circle of Interests` slide marks the lecture's first visual compression. Garber recaps the partner exercise by literally putting both founders' interest lists around a shared circle, and that drawing becomes the lecture's core method for organizing negotiation material on one page.
- She then broadens again, but now within the startup: honest assessment of cofounder fit, founders agreements, equity, what happens if someone leaves, team agreements, culture, roles, and decision rules. The lecture's rhythm here is cumulative rather than theoretical; each governance topic is presented as another place where hidden interests eventually surface.
- The next pivot turns a governance concern into a classroom puzzle: what do 50-50 cofounders do when they disagree? Garber solicits practical answers, lets the room propose reversible decisions, consultants, mediation, and tie-breaking devices, and then uses the lawyer anecdote about 51/49 splits as a deliberately crude foil rather than a final solution.
- The Sandra case is the lecture's major worked example. Garber slows the pace, introduces the case via slide, then separates the hidden interests of Sandra and the students, has the room generate possible agreements, and only afterward reveals that in reality there was no agreement at all.
- That debrief becomes the bridge to the next layer of abstraction. She moves from founder negotiation failure to internal-company negotiation, adds complications such as more than two parties, unequal power, legal implications, and confidentiality, and then explicitly says that even here she still draws her little circle of interests.
- The final third of the lecture applies the same method twice: first to Judy's promotion case inside a firm, then to the Little Inc/Big Inc contract dispute outside the firm. In both stories the operative mechanism is the same: enumerate the relevant stakeholders, infer or ask about their interests, and prepare a solution space before the meeting.
- Near the end the lecture raises a final tension that should survive on the page: can sharing our interests be used against us? Garber answers with two stories, one from an acquisition-style meeting and one from IBM/3M practice, to argue for selective, problem-oriented disclosure rather than naive transparency.
- The close is a recap, not a new theorem. Understand cofounder interests, document and revisit decisions, prepare thoroughly, and listen for new interests in the room; Hadzima's closing anecdote then reinforces the same meta-point from the other side of the table.

## Section Outline
1. Introduction: Why Negotiation Belongs in a Startup Course  
   Begin with Hadzima's handoff and Garber's motivation: ventures often fail through people and relationship breakdown before technology fails. A standalone `Question & Answer` subsection should appear here: `Question & Answer: Why is negotiation a venture skill rather than a legal afterthought?`

2. Win-Lose, Collaboration, and Reputation  
   Preserve Garber's early contrast between zero-sum bargaining and collaborative problem solving, together with the reminder that there is no true “one and done” in entrepreneurial life. A standalone `Question & Answer` subsection should appear here: `Question & Answer: Is negotiation fundamentally adversarial or collaborative?`

3. Interests, Positions, and the Expansion of Options  
   This section should unfold slowly through the fence example and the C++ example, because this is the lecture's real analytic spine: move from stated demand to underlying need, then from need to multiple possible solutions. A standalone `Question & Answer` subsection should appear here: `Question & Answer: What is the difference between a position and an interest?`

4. Founder Negotiation: First Questions and the Circle of Interests  
   Keep the classroom exercise in order: why are you doing this, what does success look like, what are you giving up, then active listening, and only then the diagrammatic recap through the circle of interests. A standalone `Question & Answer` subsection should appear here: `Question & Answer: How do we make hidden founder motives visible before conflict arrives?`

5. From Founder Fit to Formal Agreements  
   Move from honest assessment of skills and personalities into founders agreements, equity, departures, team agreements, culture, roles, and decision rules; this should read like the lecture gradually thickening the startup's internal constitution. A standalone `Question & Answer` subsection should appear here: `Question & Answer: What should equal cofounders do when they disagree?`

6. The Sandra Case: A Negotiation Failure in Slow Motion  
   Preserve the case in full narrative order: overview slide, separate hidden interests, classroom proposals, and the reveal that there was never a real agreement. A standalone `Question & Answer` subsection should appear here: `Question & Answer: Why did effort, enthusiasm, and opportunity still fail to produce an agreement?`

7. Negotiation Within and Beyond the Company  
   End with the internal-company complication slide, Judy's promotion case, the Little Inc/Big Inc contract dispute, the question about whether interests can be used as weapons, and the final practical summary on preparation and listening. A standalone `Question & Answer` subsection should appear here: `Question & Answer: Should we explicitly share our interests with the other side?`

## Mathematical Content To Include
- [transcript-backed] The lecture's basic state split between adversarial and collaborative negotiation. This should stay verbal, but the notes may carefully encode the contrast as two regimes: zero-sum bargaining versus joint problem solving with relationship preservation.
- [standard reconstruction] A compact negotiation schema such as  
  \(N = (\text{parties}, \text{interests}, \text{options}, \text{relationship constraints})\).  
  This is not displayed in the lecture, but it is a faithful compression of Garber's repeated setup.
- [transcript-backed] The distinction between positions and interests, using the fence example and the programming-language example as worked demonstrations rather than abstractions.
- [standard reconstruction] A compact logical chain  
  \(P_i \rightarrow I_i \rightarrow O\),  
  where \(P_i\) is a stated position, \(I_i\) the underlying interest, and \(O\) the set of options that satisfy the interests once they are surfaced.
- [transcript-backed] The fence example in words: `I want $500` is the position; `I need to repair the fence` is the interest; having someone repair the fence directly becomes a new option.
- [transcript-backed] The programming-language example in words: `We need C++` is a position; `we need the fastest implementable route because learning a new language costs months` is the interest.
- [transcript-backed] The claim that understanding our own interests enlarges the space of possible solutions. This is one of the lecture's few quasi-derivational steps and should be preserved explicitly.
- [frame-backed] The founder-interest decomposition in `lecture_07_figure_02.png`, where the lecture places two founders' motives around a shared blank circle.
- [standard reconstruction] A schematic notation beside that figure such as  
  \(I_{\mathrm{F1}}, I_{\mathrm{F2}} \rightarrow C\),  
  where \(C\) is the shared page or conversation space. This should be presented as editorial notation, not as something visible on the slide.
- [transcript-backed] The “first questions” triple for founders: why are you doing this, what does success look like, what are you giving up.
- [standard reconstruction] A compact founder vector  
  \(Q_0 = (\text{motivation}, \text{success}, \text{sacrifice})\),  
  useful for organizing that subsection without pretending the lecture gave formal symbols.
- [transcript-backed] The 50-50 governance problem as a structural deadlock risk in cofounder negotiation.
- [standard reconstruction] A cautious ownership notation  
  \(s_1 = s_2 = 0.5\),  
  contrasted with the lawyer's anecdotal \(0.51/0.49\) recommendation. This should be framed as a practical illustration, not as the lecture's preferred theorem.
- [transcript-backed] The distinction between one-way decisions that are hard to reverse and two-way decisions that can be tested and revisited.
- [standard reconstruction] A reversible/irreversible decision notation such as \(D_{\text{one-way}}\) and \(D_{\text{two-way}}\), or a verbal reversibility parameter, if the prose benefits from it.
- [frame-backed] The Sandra case structure visible in `lecture_07_figure_03.png`: prototype limitation, undocumented relationship, heavy student investment, and pre-competition withdrawal risk.
- [transcript-backed] The internal-company complication list: more than two parties, different power levels, legal ramifications, and higher confidentiality requirements.

- [transcript-backed] The external-company negotiation method in the Little Inc/Big Inc story: enumerate interests on both sides before the meeting and think through an acceptable solution space.
- [standard reconstruction] A cautious solution-space notation  
  \(Z = \{a : a \text{ is acceptable under the parties' interests and constraints}\}\).  
  The lecture does not formalize this, but it does explicitly talk about a “zone of solution” or solution space.
- [transcript-backed] The practical theorem-like claim that the first offer may be the best offer if it already lies inside the acceptable region and preserves the relationship.
- [transcript-backed] The Judy promotion example as a stakeholder decomposition rather than an arithmetic exercise.
- [standard reconstruction] A stakeholder set  
  \(S = \{\text{Judy}, \text{manager}, \text{company}, \text{teammates}\}\),

- The transcript contains duplicated and noisy introductory material around 00:00:58 to 00:01:29. That section should be normalized from context rather than transcribed literally.
- The read-aloud Sandra case slide segment around 00:23:43 to 00:24:37 is heavily garbled because a student is reading the slide aloud. Use `lecture_07_figure_03.png` and the cleaner later restatement of Sandra's and the students' interests to stabilize that section.
- There are additional garbled patches in the student-interest brainstorming around roughly 00:28:20 to 00:30:10, in the acquisition anecdote around roughly 00:57:12 to 00:57:48, and in Hadzima's closing anecdote after 01:03:15. Those passages should be paraphrased cautiously and only at the level the transcript securely supports.
- The lawyer's advice to avoid 50-50 and make it 51/49 should remain an anecdotal warning, not a normative theorem. Garber's own pedagogical aim is to give founders better tools than litigation, not to claim that every company should adopt a single ownership ratio.
- The terms `solution space` and `zone of solution` are used loosely in the lecture. If we standardize them in the notes, we should do so gently and consistently, without importing heavy bargaining theory vocabulary that the lecture itself does not rely on.
- `lecture_07_figure_04.png` is slightly transitional in timing: the spoken line is already moving back toward the circle-of-interests method. It still belongs in the chapter, but as a supporting checklist rather than as a standalone conceptual climax.
- The prose should sound like a guided derivation of practical reasoning rather than a generic business summary. We should mostly write in the first person plural, as if we are filling in the negotiation model step by step with Garber, while keeping speaker identity clear and not flattening her into anonymous textbook voice.
