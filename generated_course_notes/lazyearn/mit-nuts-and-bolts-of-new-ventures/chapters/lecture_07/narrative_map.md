# Narrative Map
## Opening Setup
The lecture opens with a handoff from Hadzima to Garber, and Garber immediately sets the stakes: startup failure is often a people problem before it is a technology problem. That opening is not ornamental; it gives the whole hour its governing motivation, namely that negotiation belongs inside venture formation because relationships, alignment, and communication determine whether value can survive long enough to be commercialized.

Just after that, Garber places herself in two roles, engineer and mediator. This is the lecture's first tonal cue for the chapter: we are not choosing between rigor and human complexity, but learning to treat negotiation as a disciplined way of uncovering what people are actually trying to solve.

## Beat Sequence
1. Handoff, credibility, and stakes for why negotiation matters `[Q&A candidate]`
Garber first tries to establish that negotiation is not a soft add-on but a central venture skill because many companies fail from breakdowns among founders and teammates. It appears here to justify why the course is shifting from presentation to negotiation, and it leads naturally into a more formal account of what negotiation is supposed to do.

2. Two models of negotiation: win-lose versus collaboration `[Q&A candidate]`
She next establishes the lecture's first conceptual split: we can treat negotiation as zero-sum extraction or as joint work on a shared problem. This appears early because the audience needs a governing frame before any exercises, and it leads into reputation, repeat interaction, and the claim that startup negotiations are rarely one-shot encounters.

3. Interests versus positions as the real analytic move `[Q&A candidate]`
Garber then slows down and introduces the core mechanism of the lecture: asking what people really want, not just what they say they want. This comes at exactly the right moment because it turns the abstract collaboration claim into a usable method, and it leads into the fence example, the programming-language example, and the general idea that surfacing interests expands the option set.

4. Founder negotiations begin with motives, success criteria, and sacrifice `[Q&A candidate]`
Once the interests framework is on the table, she narrows from generic negotiation to startup formation and asks the class to treat one another as provisional cofounders. This appears here because it is the first live application of the method, and it leads directly into active listening and the need to put both founders' motives into a common frame.

5. The `Circle of Interests` makes hidden founder motives visible `[Q&A candidate]`
Garber then recaps the exercise by drawing the circle-and-sticks structure and explaining that the point is to get all the parties' interests onto one page at once. This beat matters because it turns conversation into a durable visual object, and it leads forward into honest cofounder assessment, skill complementarity, and the more formal governance questions founders usually postpone.

6. Governance thickens: founders agreement, team agreement, and the 50-50 deadlock problem `[Q&A candidate]`
She next broadens from motives to structure: ownership, contribution, equity, departures, culture, roles, accountability, and decision rules. This appears here because the lecture is showing that unresolved interests do not disappear when a company forms, and it leads into the classroom puzzle about what equal cofounders should do when they disagree.

7. The Sandra case turns the method into a failure analysis `[Q&A candidate]`
Garber slows the pace again, introduces a true MIT case, separates the interests of Sandra and the students, and asks the room to construct agreements that might satisfy both sides. This beat appears when the audience is ready for a richer example, and it leads into the reveal that in reality there was no agreement and no real conversation about what each side wanted.

8. From failed founder negotiation to internal-company negotiation
After the Sandra debrief, she uses the failure as a bridge: once we move inside a company, negotiation becomes more complicated because there may be more parties, unequal power, legal consequences, and confidentiality constraints. This appears here because it generalizes the earlier founder logic without abandoning it, and it leads into the Judy promotion example as a more structured multiparty interest-mapping exercise.

9. External negotiation: Little Inc versus Big Inc, solution space, and the first-offer puzzle `[Q&A candidate]`
The final major beat applies the same framework outside the company in a contract dispute, showing how Garber's team mapped stakeholders, interests, and acceptable outcomes before meeting the other side. It appears late because the lecture now has enough machinery to handle a genuinely messy case, and it leads into the closing tension about whether sharing interests openly helps or exposes you.

10. Final tension and recap: can interests be used as a weapon? `[Q&A candidate]`
Near the end, Garber explicitly raises the risk that openness about interests can be exploited, then answers with stories that argue for selective, problem-oriented disclosure rather than naive transparency. This closing beat belongs near the end because it revisits the whole lecture under pressure, and it leads naturally into the summary: understand cofounder interests, document decisions, prepare thoroughly, and keep listening for new interests in the room.

## Transition Cues
- Garber often pivots with plain structural markers such as “so the first thing,” “so now,” and “the next one,” which makes the lecture feel cumulative rather than digressive.
- She uses recap-style turns like “what you're doing here is…” to convert an exercise into a principle, especially after the cofounder interview and the Sandra case.
- She sometimes backtracks explicitly, as in “let me go back,” which matters because the lecture is not a perfectly linear slide deck; the notes should preserve these returns when they clarify motivation.
- Several transitions are classroom-question pivots: “what do you think,” “what would you do,” “what really happened,” and “was this useful,” each of which marks a local tension before resolution.
- The move from one scale to another is usually signaled verbally: from negotiation in general, to founders, to company governance, to role-play, to within-company negotiation, to other-company negotiation.
- Late in the lecture, she uses story openings such as “this is a true story” and “let me tell you one more story” to reset attention and show that the same method survives across contexts.
- The closing summary is cued by explicit compression language: “summary startups,” then a short list of durable practices, followed by Hadzima's reinforcing anecdote.

## Recurring Motifs
- Interests over positions: the lecture returns repeatedly to the idea that visible demands are only the surface and that progress comes from asking why.
- Putting everything on one page: the `Circle of Interests` is not just a slide but a recurring habit of thought, resurfacing in founder work, internal conflict, promotion cases, and company-to-company negotiation.
- Relationships and reputation are persistent constraints, not side issues. Garber keeps reminding us that today's negotiation lives inside tomorrow's business ecosystem.
- Documentation matters because memory drifts and circumstances change; the lecture pairs “write it down” with “revisit it later.”
- Preparation is treated as a form of analysis: before the meeting, we map parties, interests, risks, and possible solution space.
- Multiplicity of stakeholders: as the lecture advances, the number of relevant actors increases, but the method does not fundamentally change.
- Classroom interaction is part of the pedagogy. The lecture keeps moving from claim, to exercise, to debrief, to more general claim.

## Pacing Risks
- A draft writer may compress the opening into a generic bio and lose the crucial causal claim that startup failure often comes from relationship breakdown, which is what licenses the whole lecture.
- The early section on win-lose versus collaboration can be flattened into a moral slogan unless the notes preserve the practical reason Garber gives: repeated interaction and reputation make the collaborative frame structurally relevant.
- The interests-versus-positions section is easy to summarize too quickly; if the fence and programming-language examples disappear, the lecture loses its main pedagogical derivation.
- The founder-interview exercise should not be reduced to a checklist alone. Its spoken purpose is to make people hear one another through active listening before any formal agreement is discussed.
- The `Circle of Interests` should not appear as an isolated figure without the classroom setup that motivates why the diagram exists.
- The long governance section can turn into a dry catalog of founders agreements and team agreements; the notes need to preserve that Garber is gradually thickening the company's internal decision structure.
- The 50-50 disagreement segment is easy to overcompress into “avoid deadlock,” but the lecture's energy comes from the room proposing real mechanisms, then hearing the lawyer's crude 51/49 answer as a foil rather than a final theorem.
- The Sandra case must retain its reveal structure: setup, interest discovery, attempted agreements, then the factual outcome that there was no agreement and no serious conversation.
- The final external-negotiation case can become too case-specific unless the notes keep showing how it reuses the same interests-based method at a larger scale.
- The late question about whether interests can be weaponized should remain a standalone tension; if it is folded into the summary, the chapter will lose one of the lecture's most useful moments of self-critique.
