# Chapter Plan
## Lecture Arc
- The lecture opens with Joseph Hadzima framing the evening as a paired sequence: first presenting the venture, then negotiating around it. Bob Jones then takes over and begins not with technique, but with motivation: why this topic deserves time in a venture-formation course at all.
- From there he pivots into value creation. We move from the abstract question “why presenting matters” to the concrete answer that ventures must repeatedly persuade customers, hires, partners, investors, and even family members.
- Once that need is established, he narrows the problem: a pitch is not just speech, but a presentation aimed at producing a specific behavior from a specific audience. The slide on potential audiences marks this transition from generic communication to audience-specific strategy.
- The next pivot is the lecture’s first real conceptual compression: every audience is implicitly asking WIFM, “what’s in it for me?” That lets him move from audience taxonomy to pitch design.
- He then changes tempo and establishes a baseline through live volunteer pitches. The rhythm becomes diagnostic: short pitch, immediate critique, then extraction of general principles from what failed or partly worked.
- After the live critiques, he recaps the core mechanism more explicitly: lead with benefits, then provide reason to believe, and tell a story rather than dumping facts. The da Vinci and Nordstrom anecdotes are not decorative; they are worked examples of this principle.
- A second pivot takes us from content to delivery mechanics: brevity, memory limits, microphone use, speaking pace, and practice. This section turns presentation into a constrained performance problem rather than a purely conceptual one.
- The lecture closes by specializing again, this time to venture capital. The broad pitch framework is narrowed to investor logic, then widened once more in the summary: know the audience, make them see their problem, show the better solution, and practice until the talk is economical and clear.

## Section Outline
1. Introduction: Why Presenting a Venture Belongs in the Course  
We should begin with Hadzima’s framing and Jones’s motivation for the session, because the lecture first argues for the importance of presentation before teaching any method. This keeps the chapter anchored in venture creation rather than generic public speaking.

2. Audiences, Objectives, and the Meaning of a Pitch  
This section should define the pitch as a goal-directed presentation and then move directly into the audience list. A standalone `Question & Answer` subsection should appear here: `Question & Answer: Should every audience hear the same pitch?`

3. The Audience’s Hidden Question: WIFM  
Here we make the lecture’s main analytic turn: every audience asks “what’s in it for me?”, whether or not they say it aloud. The section should unfold audience by audience, especially customers and potential employees, because that is where the lecture converts taxonomy into mechanism.

4. Baseline Pitches and What They Reveal  
The volunteer pitches should remain in narrative order as worked failures and partial successes, not as digressions. A standalone `Question & Answer` subsection should appear here: `Question & Answer: What went wrong when the pitch did not match the audience?`

5. Benefits First, Then Reason to Believe  
This is the lecture’s strongest conceptual rule and should be the chapter’s central method section. The live rewrites, the cell-phone example, the Leonardo da Vinci letter, and the Nordstrom anecdote all belong here because they all teach the same structure from different angles.

6. Delivery Under Constraints: Time, Memory, Voice, and Practice  
This section should treat presentation as a bounded system: limited time, limited audience memory, and limited tolerance for confusion. The microphone discussion belongs here because Jones treats intelligibility as a real competitive advantage, not a cosmetic extra.

7. Investor-Specific Logic and the Final Compression  
The chapter should end with the venture-capital specialization and then the summary heuristics, because the lecture itself moves from general pitch logic to investor logic and then back to universal closing rules. A standalone `Question & Answer` subsection should appear here: `Question & Answer: What do investors actually want to know?` and a short final one at the end: `Question & Answer: Who should give the pitch?`
