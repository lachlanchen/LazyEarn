## Definitions And Objects

- \(D\): source of market disruption; use only the three visible slide categories unless the transcript supplies an example.

- \(\text{IP}\): intellectual property that enables defensible differentiation; in this lecture it mainly means patents plus protected know-how and trade secrets.

- \(\text{proof of principle}\): crude evidence that the core science can work; not the same as a finished product or complete prototype.

- \(\text{MVP}\): minimum viable product or crude usable artifact that lets customers react to something closer to actual use.

- \(\text{market learning}\): customer interviews, market testing, sample-part feedback, willingness to pay, and evidence about which users care.

- \(\text{technology development}\): tests, subcomponent experiments, risk reduction, prototype revisions, and product architecture decisions.

- \(\text{beachhead market}\): first niche that cares enough to pay or engage seriously; not the total addressable market.

- \(\text{strategic decision}\): choice about business, market, team, product, or pricing that should be remade when new information arrives.

- \(\text{non-strategic decision}\): operational choice that can be delegated, such as office allocation, furniture, accounting software, or conference rooms.

- \(H,R,E\): Hadzima heuristic from the course wrap-up; \(H\) is happiness, \(R\) is reality, and \(E\) is expectations.

## Derivation Steps

Z Corporation pricing pivot:

1. Initial market view: incumbents sold high-end rapid-prototyping machines for roughly \(\$100{,}000\) to more than \(\$1{,}000{,}000\).

2. Initial strategy: enter low end with an office-compatible, easier-to-use printer at \(\$20{,}000\).

3. New evidence: incumbents announced office-compatible machines at \(\$60{,}000\).

4. Customer and trade-show evidence: Z Corp’s parts were lower quality in some respects, but its process was about \(10\times\) faster.

5. Strategic revision: price at \(\$60{,}000\), matching the new competitive reference point.

6. Practical rule from the case: one can lower price after launch more easily than raise it.

7. Outcome in the anecdote: the higher price helped cover unexpectedly high production, sales, and market-education costs.

Market and technology iteration:

1. Start with IP-enabled differentiation, not a generic idea.

2. Identify possible markets broadly; Marina gives a target of about \(20\) ideas.

3. Build a prototype, storyboard, sample part, or proof-of-principle artifact.

4. Put the artifact in front of customers and collect feedback.

5. Ask whether the technology can shift toward the market signal.

6. Preserve the IP moat while revising the market thesis.

7. Repeat because neither the market nor the product path is fully known at the start.

Customer evidence hierarchy:

1. Compliments from family, friends, or observers carry weak evidence.

2. Customer feedback on an abstract idea is useful but limited.

3. Feedback on a crude prototype is stronger.

4. Feedback from near-real use of the intended product is stronger still.

5. Payment, deposits, or R&D funding are the strongest signals described in the lecture.

Venture-capital fit:

1. VC provides cash, advice, connections, and validation.

2. Some businesses need fast growth or heavy capital, so VC may be necessary.

3. VC can also let a company move ahead before product-market fit is proven.

4. VC expectations may force growth faster than a market can absorb.

5. A slower, profitable, founder-owned company may be better for some founders than a failed unicorn attempt.

CEO syndrome mechanism:

1. Early CEO receives skepticism, rejection, and challenge.

2. Challenge forces rework of product, product-market fit, and value proposition.

3. Rework produces success.

4. Success makes others treat the CEO as a visionary.

5. Fewer people challenge the CEO.

6. CEO becomes insulated from contrary information.

7. Poor decisions become more likely.

8. Advisory challenge and honest feedback interrupt the loop.

## Notation Choices

- Use \(\approx\) for spoken approximate quantities: \(20\) years, \(10\) years, \(10\times\), one year, two years, and operating scale.

- Use \(\sim\) for rough guidelines rather than measured quantities, especially \(\text{words per slide}\sim10\text{--}20\).

- Use \(\longleftrightarrow\) for the reconstructed market/technology feedback relation; do not treat the slide’s `=>` as a formal implication.

- Use \(D\) only for disruption categories, not for demand or dollars.

- Use \(\$\) amounts as case facts, not universal startup benchmarks.

- Keep \(H=R/E\) as a course-closing heuristic, not a mathematical model to derive.
