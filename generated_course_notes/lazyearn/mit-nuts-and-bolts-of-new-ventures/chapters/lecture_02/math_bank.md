## Definitions And Objects
- \(M\): money the venture needs to make over a stated period.
- \(V_c\): customer value over that same period; in this lecture it is closer to period revenue per active customer than to formal lifetime value.
- \(N\): required number of customers, obtained by dividing money needed by customer value.
- \(R\): revenue.
- \(C\): cost.
- \(g(t)\): qualitative blood-glucose level over time; use only for schematic overnight discussion.
- “Bandwidth”: Jones’s qualitative term for the width of blood-glucose fluctuation; not a formally defined biomedical quantity here.
- “Customer value”: price times purchase frequency on a common time base.
- “Customer” must not collapse user, payer, champion, retailer, wholesaler, or educator into one role.
- “Slotting fee”: payment demanded by a retailer for shelf placement when a new product displaces known shelf revenue.
- “SKU”: stock keeping unit; used to motivate wholesaler economics, not a derivation by itself.
- “Reuse rate”: frequency with which the same buyer uses or reorders the product across a year.
- “Motivation pile”: ordinal ranking of segments by willingness to spend now, not by demographics.

## Derivation Steps
**Backward venture arithmetic**
1. Start with the income target, not with the product story.
2. Put that target on an explicit time base, e.g. per month.
3. Estimate customer value on the same time base.
4. Divide money needed by customer value.
5. Use the result to test whether the venture is feasible at all.

**Guitar example: schoolchildren / parents**
1. Set the target at \(M=\$5{,}000/\text{month}\).
2. Estimate lesson revenue as \(\$25/\text{hour}\) and twice per month.
3. Get \(V_c=\$50/\text{month}\).
4. Compute \(N=5{,}000/50=100\).
5. Conclude that the original market definition is too demanding for the solo operator.

**Guitar example: adults / older social market**
1. Keep the underlying activity roughly the same.
2. Change the customer definition to adults paying for nostalgia, social life, and ensemble experience.
3. Use the lecture’s higher willingness-to-pay claim to get \(V_c\approx \$400/\text{month}\).
4. Compute \(N\approx 5{,}000/400=12.5\).
5. Round in prose to “about a dozen or so.”
6. Infer that changing the customer definition changes the economics.

**Regain forecast collapse**
1. Start from positive signals: clinical benefit, no-fluid format, strong clinician endorsement, and \(\$3\) per bar.
2. Build a sales forecast from those endorsements.
3. Launch and compare actual sales to forecast.
4. Note the monthly gap: \(\$35{,}000\) actual against \(\$400{,}000\) forecast.
5. Compute that performance is under \(10\%\) of plan.
6. Re-open the model rather than blaming execution alone.

**Regain customer-motivation decomposition**
1. Ask who actually becomes part of the dialysis customer base.
2. Use the quoted shares: \(33\%\) from mismanaged diabetes and \(44\%\) from mismanaged high blood pressure.
3. Infer that roughly three quarters of the target market has a long history of weak self-care motivation.
4. Conclude that clinical need does not imply willingness to pay.

**Nighttime diabetes mechanism**
1. Daytime control uses small meals, small snacks, and regular small insulin doses.
2. Nighttime usually replaces that with one big snack and one big insulin bolus.
3. Food converts to glucose quickly, so the early spike is higher.
4. The duration is not longer in the way patients need.
5. By around \(2\) a.m., food support is gone while insulin is still active.
6. The danger window runs roughly to \(6\) a.m.
7. Product-design target becomes slower glucose release through the night.

**Channel-value experiment**
1. Ask users where they expect to buy the product.
2. Ask what price they expect in that channel.
3. Observe two piles: grocery-store answers near \(\$0.49\), pharmacy answers near \(\$1.25\).
4. Hold the product fixed.
5. Infer that channel changes perceived value.

**Retail-entry threshold tactic**
1. Assume the local pharmacy manager has a purchase amount below which corporate approval is unnecessary.
2. Guess that threshold at about \(\$100\).
3. Set the starter kit at \(\$99.95\).
4. Remove perceived downside with a no-risk refund promise.
5. Seed consumer demand first, then send those buyers into the store.
6. Let fast sell-through replace slotting-fee logic.

**Wholesaler-entry tactic**
1. Note that a large chain store carries thousands of SKUs.
2. Infer that the store prefers consolidated wholesaler delivery and invoicing.
3. Seed nearby pharmacies directly so reorder demand will exist.
4. Offer the wholesaler system-entry plus its normal margin.
5. Get the product into the wholesaler database before the reorder wave hits.
6. Scale by selling proven customer demand upstream.
