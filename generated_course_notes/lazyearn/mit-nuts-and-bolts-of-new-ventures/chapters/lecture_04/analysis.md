# Chapter Plan
## Lecture Arc
- Hadzima opens by lowering the temperature: this is background, not legal advice, because legal outcomes are fact-dependent and laws change. He then explicitly justifies the dense pace, framing the lecture as a practical hazard map for founders rather than a doctrinal survey.
- He immediately imposes order by moving through the life cycle of a new venture from idea to financing stages, and by cross-cutting that timeline with topic rows such as intellectual property, entity choice, people, and financing. This matrix is the lecture’s first structural spine, even though we do not have the slide itself.
- The first major pivot is motivational: why care about IP at all? He answers with the move from tangible to intangible firm value, then narrows from intangible assets to intellectual capital to intellectual property.
- From there he proceeds taxonomically through forms of protection: none, trade secret, trademark/service mark, copyright, and patent. The pace alternates between definition, duration, cost, and practical use, with anecdotes used to keep the distinctions concrete.
- The lecture then tightens around patents as the most structurally important regime for startups. Here the narrative becomes more analytical: patent rights as exclusion rights, patents as property boundaries, disclosure timing, first-inventor-to-file, freedom to operate, prior art, prosecution, cost, and strategy.
- A second major pivot comes when he moves from doctrinal patent rules to startup operating strategy: patent mapping, patent thickets, provisional applications, disclosure as anti-picket-fence strategy, and Tesla as a broader ecosystem play. This is where business-model logic and legal structure start to fuse.
- He then compresses the discussion into founder checklists: assignment agreements, work-for-hire, NDAs, investor behavior, and disciplined lawyer management. That practical recap sets up the next transition to university licensing, where the same ownership and commercialization questions reappear in institutional form.
- The lecture closes by pivoting from IP to legal form and then to tax structure: corporation versus partnership, C versus S treatment, exit-tax logic, and finally the Section 83 trap. The ending is a crescendo from general entity choice to a concrete founder-level timing puzzle about stock issuance, vesting, valuation, and tax exposure, before the lecture breaks off due to time.

3. `Patents as Exclusion Rights, Timing Constraints, and Strategic Assets`  
Here we keep the lecture’s central analytic stretch: patents as fences rather than use permissions, cross-license logic, novelty/non-obviousness/usefulness, enabling disclosure, first-inventor-to-file, and freedom to operate. Include standalone `Question & Answer` subsections here: `If we have a patent, are we free to practice the invention?` and `Should we patent this idea, or disclose it to block others?`

4. `Patent Process, Prior Art Mapping, and Cost Discipline for Startups`  
This section should preserve the operational arithmetic of patenting: provisional applications, one-year windows, drafting and prosecution costs, foreign filing burden, and the use of patent maps to choose commercialization direction. The tone should be “let us see how timing, cost, and landscape interact,” not “here is a static checklist.”

5. `Working With Lawyers and Moving University IP Into a Startup`  
The lecture naturally recaps here: disciplined use of counsel, invention disclosures, early drafting, and then a fast but important excursion into Bayh-Dole, MIT ownership rules, options, licenses, royalties, and equity. Include a standalone `Question & Answer` subsection here: `How do we take university IP into a startup without quietly mis-owning it?`

6. `Entity Choice, Tax Elections, and the Section 83 Trap`  
This final section should move in the same tightening sequence as the lecture: general partnership risk, why founders incorporate early, C versus S tax logic, then the Section 83 formula and its founder-stock examples. Include a standalone `Question & Answer` subsection here: `Why does stock not get issued in time, and how does an 83(b) election avoid the trap?`

7. `Founder Equity as Negotiated Structure, Not Myth`  
The closing pages should preserve the lecture’s last turn from tax mechanics to founder culture, sacrifice, IP contribution, vesting, and option-pool sizing. This should feel like an unfinished but deliberate handoff into later financing material rather than a fully closed ending.
