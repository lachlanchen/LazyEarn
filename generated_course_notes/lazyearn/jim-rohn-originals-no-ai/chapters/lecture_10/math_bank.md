## Derivation Steps
1. Three-reasons structure.
   1. State the outcome: rich by 31.
   2. Announce three reasons rather than one hidden secret.
   3. Name reason one as environment, reason two as opportunity, reason three as teacher.
   4. Compress them into a causal summary for later use.

2. Opportunity derivation.
   1. Start from “America is easy.”
   2. Infer that the country is full of opportunity.
   3. Search for one opportunity.
   4. Try it.
   5. If it is not the one, another opening appears.
   6. Repeat the try-again cycle.

3. Two-six-year contrast.
   1. Mark \(S_1\) as the messed-up years from 19 to 25.
   2. Mark \(S_2\) as the corrected six years ending in millionaire status.
   3. Hold outer variables approximately fixed.
   4. Ask why the result changed.
   5. Move the variable from outside conditions to the self.
   6. Conclude: the second block differs because “I changed.”

4. Not-much-list reduction.
   1. Pose an external improvement: relatives, prices, economy, party power, someone else’s plans.
   2. Ask what it would do for future and fortune.
   3. Answer: not much.
   4. Repeat across several examples.
   5. Infer that external rescue is not the main explanatory variable.
   6. Replace it with personal agency: you must make the difference.

5. Change-yourself derivation.
   1. State Shoaff’s promise: if you change, everything changes for you.
   2. Deny the need to change government, prices, or taxes.
   3. Ask what changes first.
   4. Answer: philosophy.
   5. Expand philosophy into mind, thought, information, knowledge, and decisions.
   6. Conclude that life outcomes change downstream of that internal shift.

6. Wind-and-sail derivation.
   1. Reject wishing for a more favorable wind.
   2. Replace that wish with the wisdom to set a better sail.
   3. Treat wind as the external condition one does not control.
   4. Treat sail-setting as the internal adjustment one does control.
   5. Infer that progress depends on handling the given wind, not waiting for a new one.

7. Easy-definition derivation.
   1. Return to the opening claim that getting rich was easy.
   2. Define easy as “something I could do.”
   3. Insert the parenthesis that he worked hard.
   4. Separate ease from effortlessness.
   5. Preserve the compatibility of “easy” and disciplined labor.

8. Success-versus-failure derivation.
   1. Raise the objection: if it was easy, why did the others not get rich?
   2. Answer: it is easy not to.
   3. Generalize into the formula “easy to do = easy not to do.”
   4. Locate the success/failure split in the response to easy daily acts.
   5. Compress the success side as non-neglect.

9. Neglect cascade derivation.
   1. Name neglect as the major reason for not having more.
   2. Describe neglect first as infection.
   3. Let untreated infection become disease.
   4. Show one neglect leading to another and another.
   5. Apply it to money, time, business, and health.
   6. Conclude that accumulated neglect over years produces the undesired life-state.

## Notation Choices
- Use \(E, O, T\) for environment, opportunity, and teacher only in compact reconstructions; keep the prose forms nearby so the symbols do not feel imported from a foreign model.
- Use \(S_1\) and \(S_2\) for first six years and second six years.
- Use \(X_{\text{out}}\) when compressing the list of outer variables; expand it in prose the first time.
- Use \(P\) for philosophy only after the lecture explicitly names philosophy as the first change.
- Use \(D\) for easy daily acts and \(N\) for neglect in the second half.
- Use \(=\) for quoted identity or spoken definition, such as \(\text{Easy}=\text{something I can do}\).
- Use \(\Longrightarrow\) for operational or causal consequence.
- Use \(\Longleftarrow\) when an outcome is explained by an upstream cause.
- Use \(\approx\) for “about the same,” not for numerical approximation.
- Use \(>\) only for rhetorical preference or superiority, as in better sail over better wind, not for a measured inequality.
- Use \(\to\) for chain processes such as opportunity sequences or neglect cascades.
- Keep long verbal quantities in \(\text{...}\); the lecture’s mathematics is mostly relational and verbal, not symbolic.
