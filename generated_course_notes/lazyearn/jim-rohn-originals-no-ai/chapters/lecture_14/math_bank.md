## Derivation Steps

Failure by accumulation

1. Start with a small wrong judgment.
2. Repeat it daily.
3. Immediate punishment may be weak or invisible.
4. Delay does not cancel accumulation.
5. Months and years turn the pattern into bad health, weak finances, or poor results.
6. Therefore failure is accumulated error, not sudden catastrophe.

Success by accumulation

1. Choose a simple discipline.
2. Practice it daily.
3. Early return appears quickly enough to reinforce the practice.
4. One new discipline encourages the next.
5. Disciplines spread across health, money, communication, and self-management.
6. Therefore success is accumulated discipline, not one dramatic leap.

Philosophy versus circumstances

1. Compare two periods with roughly the same external world.
2. Government, taxes, economy, relatives, and prices are described as “about the same.”
3. Outcomes are radically different.
4. The changed term is philosophy.
5. Philosophy redirects attitude and activity.
6. Results then change.

Results as audit

1. Ask for countable outputs.
2. Insert the number, not the story.
3. Read the number as evidence about philosophy, attitude, and activity.
4. Use bad numbers to locate error.
5. Use good numbers to confirm progress.

Marketplace value ladder

1. Time is necessary to work.
2. The lecture denies that time alone is what gets paid.
3. Payment tracks value brought in that time.
4. Raise value while holding time fixed.
5. Income can therefore rise without adding hours.
6. Hence “work harder on yourself than on your job.”

Goal-setting logic

1. An undesigned future produces apprehension and hesitant steps.
2. A written future design produces anticipation.
3. A clear promise makes the price bearable.
4. A large goal forces the pursuer to stretch.
5. What matters most is the person produced in the pursuit.

70/10/10/10 allocation

1. Start from one dollar of income.
2. Limit spending to 70 cents.
3. Allocate 10 cents to charity.
4. Allocate 10 cents to active capital.
5. Allocate 10 cents to passive capital.
6. Repeat from small amounts upward.
7. The plan matters more than the starting amount.

Push-up model

1. Current best may be only \(5\).
2. Rest briefly.
3. Do \(5\) more.
4. Repeat the cycle.
5. Total output exceeds the first apparent ceiling.
6. Therefore current best is not total capacity.

Sharing enlarges capacity

1. Share one idea with \(n\) people.
2. Each listener hears it once.
3. The speaker repeats it \(n\) times.
4. Repetition deepens the speaker’s own grasp.
5. Capacity grows by pouring out, not by hoarding.

## Notation Choices

- Use \(P, A, X, R, L\) for philosophy, attitude, activity, results, lifestyle.
- Use \(C\) for the mostly fixed givens; do not split seed/soil/rain/sunshine/economy into separate variables unless a later diagram truly needs it.
- Use \(e_t\) and \(d_t\) only for cautious daily-accumulation schematics; \(t\) should mean day count.
- Use \(\Rightarrow\) for causal lecture logic; reserve \(=\) for direct spoken identities or bookkeeping totals.
- Use \(\approx\) for rough lecture ratios and historical numbers.
- Keep the mnemonic formulas in words: “could / should / don’t,” “could / should / will,” “do what you can,” “do the best you can.”
- Keep percentages explicit for the money rule; only use \(I,s,c,a,p\) when compact notation is needed.
- For marketplace relations, use \(V\) for value and \(Y\) for income; avoid hourly-wage formulas that imply pay is a function of time alone.
- Keep “promise,” “price,” “apprehension,” and “anticipation” in English unless a later table needs short labels.
- Treat all notation as transcript-derived editorial notation, not recovered board notation.
