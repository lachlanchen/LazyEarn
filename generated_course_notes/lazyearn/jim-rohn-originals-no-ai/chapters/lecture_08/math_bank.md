## Derivation Steps

1. Deserve-law via agricultural example
   1. Start from the common intuition: we need a harvest.
   2. Reject need as sufficient cause.
   3. Introduce planting as prior qualifying action.
   4. Conclude that reaping belongs to planters.
   5. Generalize from harvest to broader outcomes.

2. Deserve-law via opportunity
   1. Ask how open doors are found.
   2. Replace passive wish with continual knocking.
   3. Replace passive need with active searching.
   4. Conclude that opportunity is opened to those who act repeatedly.
   5. Extend the same logic to asking and receiving answers.

3. Process of deserving
   1. Start from a state of need.
   2. Add a small visible qualifying act.
   3. Repeat with a second concrete act.
   4. Interpret the sequence as “beginning the process of deserving.”
   5. Emphasize that transformation is stepwise, not instantaneous.

4. Marketplace law
   1. Begin with the statement of need.
   2. Note that need does not open the vault or the field.
   3. Introduce seed, willingness, and work as admissible inputs.
   4. Replace “How can I get?” with “How can I earn?”
   5. Conclude that resources flow toward qualification, not exposed need.

5. Receiving from giving
   1. State the wish to receive.
   2. Identify this as self-interest.
   3. Enlighten it by introducing giving as the initiating act.
   4. Conclude that giving starts the receiving process.
   5. Reframe “better to give than receive” as process order, not mere moral ornament.

6. Greatness from service
   1. Ask how one becomes great.
   2. Reject self-concern as sufficient.
   3. Introduce service to others as the operative step.
   4. Expand from helping self to helping many.
   5. Conclude that service to many leads to greatness.

7. Qualification from small things
   1. Begin with the wish to preside over much.
   2. Check present stewardship over little.
   3. Demand faithfulness and discipline when amounts are small.
   4. Use few people, few dollars, and few listeners as test cases.
   5. Conclude that stewardship over much is earned in miniature.

8. Success ingredients
   1. Collect good ideas.
   2. Let one idea lead to another.
   3. Add good plans in multiple domains.
   4. Add time-handling and patience.
   5. Conclude that success is an assembled process, not a single event.

9. Patience and social stability
   1. Observe that growth takes time.
   2. Extend this from crops to people, family, and self.
   3. Introduce the rule that “most will” or “enough will.”
   4. Clarify with the Sodom-and-Gomorrah exception.
   5. Conclude that patience relies on a general expectation of enough cooperative agents.

10. Problem-solving on paper
   1. Remove the problem from the mind.
   2. Put it on paper and describe it fully.
   3. Ask whether that is all of it.
   4. Split the page into problem and answers/solutions.
   5. Work question 1: what could I do?
   6. Work question 2: what could I read?
   7. Work question 3: who could I ask?
   8. Ask others only after developing working papers.
   9. Conclude that the attempt builds independent mental discipline.

## Notation Choices

- Use \(S_{\mathrm{enl}}\) for enlightened self-interest when symbolic shorthand is useful.
- Use \(D\) for deserving/qualification and \(N\) for need throughout.
- Use \(P\) and \(R\) for planting and reaping only in the harvest analogy; do not reuse them for reading or research.
- Use \(\rightarrow\) for process flow and practical transformation.
- Use \(\Rightarrow\) only for stronger consequence or when the spoken logic is emphatic.
- Prefer text-heavy equations over abstract algebraic symbolism.
- Keep quoted maxims in roman text inside math mode when displayed.
- Use \(W\) for working papers and \(X\) for the problem statement in the final procedural section.
- Distinguish \(R\) for reaping from \(R_b(X)\) for reading/research by keeping the latter subscripted.
- Do not introduce calculus, optimization, probability, or economic equilibrium notation; none of it is source-faithful here.
- Treat “most will” as a qualitative stability rule, not a statistical claim requiring formal probability notation.
