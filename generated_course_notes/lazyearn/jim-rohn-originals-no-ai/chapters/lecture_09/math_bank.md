## Definitions And Objects
- `\text{wind}`: external conditions, including economics, politics, and circumstance
- `\text{sail}`: internal adjustment, especially philosophy, skill, discipline, and self-change
- `\text{value brought to the marketplace}`: the lecture’s operative cause of pay
- `\text{personal development}`: the first major subject; improvement of self rather than conditions
- `\text{OPE}`: other people’s experiences
- `\text{future as promise}`: the designed future that creates anticipation rather than apprehension
- `\text{price}`: the disciplines, books, classes, and effort required by a goal
- `\text{financial independence}`: living from income generated by one’s own resources
- `\text{active capital}`: money set aside to make profit directly through enterprise
- `\text{passive capital}`: money furnished to others for use, yielding interest
- `\text{power position}`: the lender rather than the borrower
- `\text{marketplace ladder}`: the vertical pay ladder from low hourly pay to very high compensation

## Derivation Steps
Value, time, and pay:
1. It takes time to bring value to the marketplace.
2. The lecture explicitly denies that pay is for time itself.
3. Therefore equal time can produce unequal pay.
4. The difference is attributed to unequal marketplace value.
5. The income ladder is then used as the visual consequence of that claim.

Climbing the marketplace ladder:
1. Start with a low rung such as `\$5` per hour.
2. Ask why higher rungs such as `\$50` or `\$500` are paid.
3. Answer: the person is more valuable to the marketplace.
4. Conclude that climbing the ladder means becoming more valuable.
5. Translate that into self-work: work harder on yourself than on your job.

From goal clarity to easier discipline:
1. An undesigned future produces apprehension.
2. A designed future becomes a promise.
3. Every promise carries a price.
4. If the promise is clear and powerful, the price feels easier to pay.
5. Therefore goal setting is not decoration; it is what makes discipline bearable.

From philosophy to financial independence:
1. Poor philosophy is described as spending first and investing the remainder.
2. Rich philosophy is described as investing first and spending the remainder.
3. The lecture then imposes the dollar split `1.00 = 0.70 + 0.10 + 0.10 + 0.10`.
4. The two investment tenths become active and passive capital.
5. Over `20` years, those accumulated resources are supposed to generate sufficient income.
6. When that resource income covers living needs, the lecture calls this financial independence.

Profits versus wages:
1. Wages are tied to employment and limited work arrangements.
2. Profits can begin earlier and are presented as scalable.
3. Therefore the lecture ranks profits above wages for wealth-building.
4. This ranking motivates the `0.10` allocation to active capital.

## Notation Choices
- Use text-style symbolic statements rather than abstract single-letter economics notation in the main chapter.
- Prefer `\text{pay}`, `\text{time}`, and `\text{value brought to the marketplace}` over invented variables unless compression is necessary.
- If compression is needed in a local derivation, use `V_{\text{mkt}}` for marketplace value and define it once.
- Keep dollar amounts explicit with currency symbols: `\$5`, `\$50`, `\$500`, `\$80\,\text{million}`.
- Use `\to` for temporal or rung-to-rung movement, as in `15 \to 35` and `\$5 \to \$6`.
- Use `>` and `<` only for lecture maxims or ranking relations, not as claims of rigorous economic law.
- Write the allocation rule in decimal-dollar form `1.00 = 0.70 + 0.10 + 0.10 + 0.10` to match the lecture’s “out of a dollar” framing.
- Use `\text{}` for all definitional slogans and spoken formulas; avoid formal operators not used in the lecture.
- Treat `\text{compound interest}` as a named object, not a derived formula, because the lecture names it but does not compute it.
