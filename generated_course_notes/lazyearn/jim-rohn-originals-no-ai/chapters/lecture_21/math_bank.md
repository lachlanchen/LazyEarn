## Definitions And Objects
- \(H\): what happens; the external event stream common to everyone.
- \(A\): what you do about what happens; response, conduct, or action.
- \(C\): circumstances; the outer givens held fixed in the 90-day experiment.
- \(B\): the person you have become; the developed self that explains present attraction.
- \(F\): future; mainly determined by response rather than bare event.
- \(V\): marketplace value; the economically rewarded contribution.
- \(M\): marketplace; explicitly glossed by Rohn as reality.
- \(T\): time; the medium in which value is delivered, not what is directly bought.
- \(P\): pay, income, or money; the countable output used as the first diagnostic.
- \(r\): ladder rung or wage level; the place one occupies on the economic ladder.
- \(S\): self-work; the package of skills, graces, philosophy, attitude, language, communication, and abilities.
- 90-day change experiment: the procedural rule “do something different the next 90 days than the last 90 days.”
- Attraction principle: the claim that present possessions and condition are drawn by the person one has become.
- Human value: moral, communal, familial, or spiritual worth; explicitly not identical to marketplace value.
- Demand/waiting: rejected strategies for raising pay without raising value.

## Derivation Steps
1. Future-control derivation
   1. Start with the claim that what happens happens to all of us.
   2. Therefore events alone cannot explain the major part of divergent futures.
   3. Move the active variable from \(H\) to \(A\), what we do about what happens.
   4. Conclude that the major part of \(F\) tracks response, not mere occurrence.

2. Same-circumstances update rule
   1. Hold \(C\) fixed.
   2. Change conduct for the next 90 days.
   3. Use small examples: books, health disciplines, family relationship, any small change.
   4. Infer that different \(A\) under the same \(C\) can yield different results.

3. Attraction and self-change derivation
   1. State that current condition is tied to the person one has become.
   2. Apply the rule to Rohn’s broke condition: pennies, no bank balance, creditors, broken promises.
   3. Ask how to change that condition.
   4. Shift the variable from outer world to self.
   5. Conclude: if you change, everything changes for you.
   6. Compress once more: to have more, become more.

4. Value-time-pay derivation
   1. Start with money because it is countable.
   2. State that pay comes from bringing value to the marketplace.
   3. Identify marketplace with reality.
   4. Add that time is required to deliver value.
   5. Reject the claim that one is paid for the hour itself.
   6. Replace “pay for time” with “pay for value put in time.”
   7. At fixed \(T\), larger \(V\) yields larger \(P\).

5. Same-time multiplier derivation
   1. Hold \(T\) fixed.
   2. Ask whether \(V\) can double.
   3. Ask whether doubled \(V\) gives doubled \(P\) in the same time.
   4. Answer emphatically: yes.
   5. Summarize the rule: to earn more money in the same time, become more valuable.

6. Ladder derivation
   1. Recast the economy as a ladder, not a bed.
   2. Place the bottom rung near \(\$4/\text{hour}\).
   3. Reject fixation on staying forever at the bottom rung.
   4. Identify upward motion with increasing value.
   5. Infer that more value means movement up the ladder.

7. How-to-get-more-money derivation
   1. Ask how to get more money.
   2. Raise strike or demand as a candidate method.
   3. Reject demand as a path to riches.
   4. Raise waiting for a raise as another candidate method.
   5. Reject waiting as slower and weaker than climbing.
   6. Replace both with the operative rule: become more valuable.

8. Phone-call proof and self-work derivation
   1. Present the phone call worth millions.
   2. Ask why that opportunity came to him.
   3. Answer: he had become valuable.
   4. Move from anecdotal proof to general maxim.
   5. State the pair: hard work on job makes a living; hard work on self makes a fortune.
   6. Expand self-work into skills, graces, philosophy, attitude, language, communication, and abilities.

## Notation Choices
- Prefer text-heavy displayed equations over symbolic compression unless the symbolic version is doing real work.
- Use
  - \(H\) for what happens,
  - \(A\) for what you do about it,
  - \(C\) for circumstances,
  - \(B\) for the person you have become,
  - \(F\) for future,
  - \(V\) for marketplace value,
  - \(T\) for time,
  - \(P\) for pay or income,
  - \(r\) for ladder rung,
  - \(S\) for self-work or self-development.
- Use \(\Longleftarrow\) for strategic dependence or source, especially when an outcome is said to come from a change in person.
- Use \(\Longrightarrow\) for operational consequence, especially when a local step leads to a result.
- Use \(\not\Longrightarrow\) for rejected causal claims such as demand leading to riches.
- Use \(>\) and \(<\) only for the lecture’s preference or dominance comparisons, not as literal quantitative inequalities.
- Use \(\to\) for ladder movement or sequence of wage positions.
- Keep \(\text{Marketplace}=\text{Reality}\) in words rather than compressing it into abstract notation.
- Keep money explicit as \(\$4/\text{hour}\), \(\$5\), or “worth millions” when the lecture itself speaks that way.
- Do not import textbook economics notation such as supply-demand curves, marginal productivity notation, or optimization language.
