## Definitions And Objects

- \(W\): wages; the job-based income stream.
- \(P\): profits; entrepreneurial or network-marketing return.
- \(W_{\mathrm{ft}}\): full-time job wages.
- \(P_{\mathrm{pt}}\): part-time profit from the side business.
- \(h_{\mathrm{pt}}\): weekly part-time time budget.
- \(E\): shared environment, the lecture’s “wind.”
- \(S\): response variable, the lecture’s “set of the sail.”
- \(O\): outcome, destination, arrival, income result.
- \(n\): number of attempts, talks, or people approached.
- \(s\): number of successful responses, yeses, or joiners.
- \(r\): observed success ratio, used only as editorial shorthand for the law of averages.
- \(I\): income level.
- Birds: the class of prospects or opportunities lost before engagement.
- Rocky ground: the class that starts, then withers quickly.
- Thorns: the class choked by distractions, small cares, or competing trivialities.
- Good ground: the class that persists and produces results.
- \(Y\in\{30\%,60\%,100\%\}\): good-ground outcome levels, kept as spoken percentages rather than formal yield notation.
- Individual work: sponsor attention given one-to-one.
- Group work: sponsor attention given in meetings, classes, or shared settings.
- \(\text{buy}\to\text{sell}\): inventory-first capitalism sequence.
- \(\text{sell}\to\text{buy}\): sell-first capitalism sequence, included cautiously because the transcript is noisy.

## Derivation Steps

Part-time fortune ladder

1. Start with full-time wages \(W_{\mathrm{ft}}\).
2. Add part-time effort \(h_{\mathrm{pt}}\).
3. First threshold: part-time profit reaches the level of the full-time wage.
4. Second threshold: part-time profit reaches twice the full-time wage.
5. Once those thresholds are crossed, the invitation becomes stronger because the story is now visible in lifestyle change.

Wind and sail control split

1. The same wind blows on everyone.
2. Different people arrive at different destinations.
3. Therefore the difference is not assigned to wind alone.
4. The lecture assigns the difference to the set of the sail.
5. The practical conclusion is that change must occur in philosophy, discipline, and response.

Law of averages: ratio formation

1. Repeat the same basic action enough times.
2. Count attempts as \(n\) and positive responses as \(s\).
3. A ratio \(r=s/n\) begins to appear.
4. The lecture’s first example is \(10\to1\).
5. Once the ratio appears, the lecture claims it tends to continue.

Scale beats better efficiency

1. One person has a high ratio: \(10\to9\).
2. Another person has a low ratio: \(100\to10\).
3. Compare absolute outcomes: \(10>9\).
4. Therefore larger volume can beat better efficiency.
5. This is the lecture’s justification for “make up in numbers what you lack in skill.”

Skill improves the ratio

1. Begin with \(1/10\).
2. Continue acting instead of stopping at the first ratio.
3. Skill accumulates through repetition.
4. The ratio improves to \(2/10\).
5. It can improve again to \(3/10\).
6. The baseball analogy normalizes failure frequency while preserving high payoff.

Recruiting arithmetic

1. Use the working ratio \(10\to3\).
2. Treat three joiners as sufficient.
3. Treat seven non-joiners as expected residue, not personal catastrophe.
4. Therefore the operational rule is: gather the full ten and let the average work.
5. This supports the invitation line “come and be one of the seven.”

Sower as an average-distribution model

1. Start with an ambitious sower and good seed.
2. Some seed is lost to birds.
3. Some seed starts, then dies on rocky ground.
4. Some seed is choked by thorns.
5. The sower keeps sowing instead of studying every loss.
6. Eventually some seed lands on good ground.
7. Good ground still splits into unequal outcomes: \(30\%,60\%,100\%\).

Disappointment discipline

1. Early losses are treated as structural classes, not unique mysteries.
2. Birds, rocky ground, and thorns are accepted as normal categories.
3. The lecture rejects extended “why” analysis.
4. The operative response is to keep sowing.
5. Therefore persistence is grounded in classification, not optimism alone.

Deserve-not-need rule

1. The lecture distinguishes need from planting.
2. Harvest is linked to planting, not to need.
3. Sponsor attention therefore follows action taken.
4. “You step, I step” becomes the local update rule.
5. The result is an attention economy based on response, not sympathy alone.

Attention allocation

1. The lecture states that \(80\%\) of the people do \(20\%\) of the business.
2. Therefore the top \(20\%\) can justify individual work.
3. The remaining \(80\%\) are handled by group work.
4. Group work is presented as less confrontational and more scalable.

Skill over capital

1. Money alone does not buy the future.
2. Skills, ambition, courage, and ingenuity are treated as causal.
3. Therefore low initial capital does not block entry.
4. This supports the two sequences \(\text{buy}\to\text{sell}\) and \(\text{sell}\to\text{buy}\).

## Notation Choices

- Use \(W\) for wages and \(P\) for profits throughout.
- Use subscripts `ft` and `pt` for full-time and part-time.
- Use \(h_{\mathrm{pt}}\) for weekly part-time hours.
- Use \(E\) for environment and \(S\) for sail-setting or response.
- Use \(O\) for outcome only in compact editorial schematics.
- Use \(n\) for attempts and \(s\) for successes.
- Use \(r=s/n\) for the law-of-averages ratio; avoid \(p\) or probabilistic language unless clearly marked as editorial.
- Prefer spoken transition notation like \(10\to1\), \(10\to3\), \(100\to10\) over more abstract probability notation.
- Keep the baseball figure as \(.300\) when citing the analogy; elsewhere prefer fractions or percentages.
- Keep the sower outcomes as \(30\%,60\%,100\%\), not decimals.
- Use verbal category labels `birds`, `rocky ground`, `thorns`, `good ground`; do not rename them into abstract statistical classes in the final notes.
- Use arrows for the capitalism sequence: \(\text{buy}\to\text{sell}\), \(\text{sell}\to\text{buy}\).
- Treat all formal symbols here as note-writer shorthand, not as original spoken notation.
