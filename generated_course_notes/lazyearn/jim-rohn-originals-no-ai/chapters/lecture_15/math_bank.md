## Derivation Steps
Five-piece causal chain
1. Start with \(P\), not with circumstance.
2. Treat \(P\) as the “set of the sail.”
3. Let \(P\) shape \(H\).
4. Let \(H\) govern \(A\).
5. Let \(A\) produce \(R\).
6. Let accumulated \(R\) show up as \(L\).
7. Use bad \(R\) to revisit \(P\). 

Failure accumulation
1. Pick a small judgment error.
2. Repeat it daily.
3. Early penalties may look negligible.
4. Negligible penalties accumulate.
5. Accumulation appears later as financial, physical, or personal disaster.

Success accumulation
1. Pick one simple discipline.
2. Practice it daily.
3. First returns are small but real.
4. Small returns encourage another discipline.
5. Disciplines reinforce one another.
6. Reinforcement becomes an upward life-process.

Marketplace pay rule
1. Time is necessary but not what is bought.
2. The marketplace buys value in time.
3. Increase \(V\) while holding time fixed.
4. The same block of time can now earn more \(Y\).
5. Therefore self-development is the earnings lever.

Finance allocation rule
1. Begin with total income \(I\).
2. Hold spending to at most \(0.70I\).
3. Reserve \(0.10I\) for charity.
4. Reserve \(0.10I\) for active capital.
5. Reserve \(0.10I\) for passive capital.
6. If the ideal split is impossible at first, move toward the plan anyway.
7. The lecture’s explicit rule: the plan matters more than the amount.

Goal-setting logic
1. An undesigned future produces apprehension.
2. Apprehension yields hesitant steps.
3. A designed future produces anticipation.
4. Clear promise makes the price easier to pay.
5. Therefore goals must be written, concrete, and owned.
6. The deeper yield of a goal is what it makes of the pursuer.

Seasonal life-process
1. Winter is inevitable.
2. The task in winter is not complaint but becoming wiser, stronger, better.
3. Spring brings opportunity.
4. Opportunity must be seized quickly.
5. Summer requires both nourishment and defense.
6. Fall requires reaping without complaint and with responsibility.

## Notation Choices
- Use \(P,H,A,R,L\) for the five major pieces: philosophy, attitude, activity, results, lifestyle.
- Use \(\to\) for directional causal order or dependence, not formal proof.
- Use \(\Rightarrow\) for practical consequence, motivational inference, or lecture-slogan consequence.
- Use \(\neq\) only where the lecture explicitly distinguishes terms, especially \(\mathrm{Sincerity}\neq\mathrm{Truth}\) and \(\text{pay}\neq\text{time}\).
- Use \(>,\le\) only for explicit evaluative or numerical claims: \(T>M\), \(S\le 0.70I\), \(O>I\).
- Keep verbal objects in \(\text{...}\) or \(\mathrm{...}\); this lecture’s mathematics is mostly verbal-schematic.
- Keep result-numbers as count variables with descriptive subscripts, not abstract optimization variables.
- Keep finance percentages tied to “out of every dollar” language when drafting chapter prose; the cent-language is part of the lecture’s pedagogy.
- Treat the seasonal model as named states, not as differential dynamics or a formal cycle equation.
- Treat “profits,” “wages,” and “interest” as distinct income channels, but do not introduce full accounting notation the lecture never uses.
- Avoid theorem-style notation, equilibrium notation, probability notation, or calculus language; the source does not support it.
